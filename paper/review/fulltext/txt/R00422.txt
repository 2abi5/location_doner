Abstract

In facility horticultural production, intelligent disease recognition and precise intervention are vital for crop health and economic efficiency. We construct a multi-source dataset from Bayan Nur, Weifang, and Honghe that integrates handheld camera photos, drone field images, and laboratory-controlled samples. Handheld images capture fine lesion texture for close-up diagnosis common in greenhouses; drone images provide canopy-scale patterns and spatial context suited to open-field management; laboratory images offer controlled illumination and background for stable supervision and cross-crop feature learning. Our objective is robust cross-crop, cross-regional diagnosis and economically rational control. To this end, a model named CCGD-Net is proposed. It is designed as a multi-task framework. The framework incorporates a multi-scale perception module (MSFE) to produce hierarchical representations. It includes a cross-domain alignment module (CDAM) that reduces distribution shifts between greenhouse and open-field environments. The training follows an unsupervised domain adaptation setting that uses unlabeled target-region images. When such images are not available, the model functions in a pure generalization mode. The framework also integrates a regional economic strategy module (RESM) that transforms recognition outputs and local cost information into optimized intervention intensity. Experiments show an accuracy of 91.6%, an F1-score of 89.8%, and an mAP of 88.9%, outperforming Swin Transformer and ConvNeXt; removing RESM reduces F1 to 87.2%. In cross-regional testing (Weifang training → Honghe testing), the model attains an F1 of 88.0% and mAP of 86.5%. These results indicate that integrating complementary imaging modalities with domain alignment and economic optimization provides an effective solution for disease diagnosis across greenhouse and field systems.

Keywords:
cross-domain adaptation; domain alignment for smart horticulturae; horticultural production intelligence; multi-modal deep learning; regional economic strategy

1. Introduction

With the intensification and intelligent transformation of modern agriculture, horticultural crops have assumed an increasingly important role in China’s facility agriculture systems [1]. Major horticultural species such as apples, tomatoes, and cucumbers are not only significant economic crops but also key supports for regional characteristic agriculture and farmers’ income growth [2]. However, plant diseases remain one of the principal constraints on improvements in yield and quality [3]. Disease outbreaks are characterized by diversity, suddenness, and regional heterogeneity, leading to direct economic losses and, through pesticide overuse, potential environmental pollution and food safety risks [4]. Establishing an efficient, precise, and intelligent system for disease identification and control is therefore an essential pathway toward sustainable agricultural development and food security [5]. Nevertheless, the complexity of crop disease recognition far exceeds that of conventional image classification. Substantial differences in leaf structure and chromatic–textural patterns across crop species, together with regional variation in cultivation practices, climatic conditions, and soil environments within the same crop, markedly affect disease morphology [6]. These multi-level variations result in domain shifts between datasets from different crops or regions, leading to a sharp decline in the generalization ability of existing deep learning models when applied outside their training domain. In other words, current approaches are typically optimized for single-crop, single-region scenarios and lack mechanisms to adapt to unseen visual and environmental conditions. This gap—insufficient cross-crop and cross-regional generalization in disease recognition models—constitutes the core scientific challenge addressed in this study. We aim to operationalize this challenge by constructing a multi-source, multi-regional dataset and developing a unified deep learning framework that explicitly aligns heterogeneous visual domains while maintaining high diagnostic accuracy and economic interpretability.

Early studies on plant disease recognition predominantly relied on handcrafted visual features combined with traditional machine learning classifiers [7]. Commonly used features included color histograms, texture descriptors such as the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) and Local Binary Patterns (LBP), and shape-based morphological descriptors [8]. These features were typically extracted from leaf images under controlled illumination and uniform backgrounds to capture disease-related color or texture variations. The extracted features were then input into classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), or Random Forests (RF) for categorical recognition. Under such constrained experimental conditions, these pipelines achieved reasonably accurate results for single-crop or laboratory-based datasets and offered advantages of algorithmic simplicity and low data requirements [9]. However, several limitations remained prominent. First, the adaptability of handcrafted features was insufficient [10]. Because disease characteristics vary substantially across crops and regions, manually designed features were often crop-specific and lacked generalization capability in multi-crop or cross-regional scenarios [11]. Second, traditional classifiers were highly sensitive to image acquisition conditions—including illumination, background clutter, and leaf pose variation—which could markedly impair recognition accuracy [12]. Third, these approaches typically ignored environmental and economic contextual factors, preventing the linkage between recognition outputs and downstream control or decision-making processes [13]. As a result, while high accuracy could be achieved in laboratory tests, performance degraded sharply in real-world field conditions, falling short of the requirements for intelligent and economically informed agricultural management.

In recent years, with the advancement of deep learning [8], convolutional neural networks and Transformers have been extensively applied in agricultural vision tasks, becoming core techniques in crop disease recognition [14]. Traditional recognition methods generally rely on handcrafted features. In contrast, deep learning enables end-to-end learning of multi-level semantic features from large-scale datasets, significantly enhancing both accuracy and generalization capability [15]. CNN-based architectures, such as ResNet, DenseNet, and EfficientNet, have achieved high accuracy in single-crop disease recognition tasks, particularly on benchmark datasets such as PlantVillage, where classification accuracy often exceeds 98%98% [16]. However, these models exhibit limited generalization across crops and regions, primarily due to significant variation in lesion morphology, leaf texture, and illumination conditions under different agronomic settings [17]. The introduction of Transformer-based models into computer vision has provided new opportunities for plant disease recognition [18]. Vision Transformer leverages the self-attention mechanism to perform global feature modeling [19]. Its mechanism captures structural dependencies between lesions across the global context and improves recognition of complex disease patterns [20]. Recently, several hybrid architectures have further advanced disease recognition performance. For example, Njoroge et al. [21] proposed the DEMF edge-optimized multimodal fusion model that integrates EfficientNetV2 and MobileNetV2 with hybrid cross-feature fusion and transfer learning, achieving high-precision multi-crop disease recognition under resource-constrained settings with accuracy up to 99.2%99.2% and very low-latency edge deployment. Bouacida et al. [22] introduced a cross-crop disease detection approach that combines local patch recognition with prevalence estimation, focusing on unhealthy leaf regions rather than whole-leaf features; this strategy enhanced generalization to unseen crops and diseases, achieving accuracies of about 94.04%94.04% on standard domains and 97.13%97.13% on new domains. Chavan et al. [23] developed a hybrid architecture that integrates image preprocessing, an improved U-Net segmentation module, and a crop disease detection network through joint training and feature fusion, leading to significant gains in detection and segmentation accuracy under complex agricultural imaging conditions. Nevertheless, despite these advances, both CNN- and Transformer-based models still struggle with cross-crop and cross-regional generalization, especially under domain shifts caused by climatic, imaging, or cultivation variability. Consequently, relying solely on CNNs or Transformers is insufficient for robust recognition in complex agricultural environments, thereby motivating the domain adaptation strategy proposed in this study.

To address these challenges, a Cross-Crop and Cross-Regional Generalized Disease Detection Network (CCGD-Net) is proposed to enable end-to-end intelligent modeling from visual recognition to regional decision-making. The study is organized around three core objectives: a Multi-task Shared Feature Extractor (MSFE) is constructed to realize knowledge transfer and feature sharing across disease recognition tasks for different crops; Cross-Domain Adaptation Module (CDAM) is designed in response to pronounced inter-regional distribution differences; in view of the economic feasibility and operational practicality of disease management, a Regional Economic Strategy Module (RESM) is further introduced. The innovations of this work are reflected in three aspects:

MSFE: A unified encoder with crop-specific heads enables shared + discriminative learning across apples, tomatoes, and cucumbers; gradient-balancing is used to reduce task conflict.

CDAM: Feature-level adversarial alignment is coupled with cross-domain attention to emphasize mutually relevant regions. Training follows an unsupervised domain adaptation protocol that leverages unlabeled target-region images; when unavailable, the model runs in pure generalization. In cross-region transfer (Weifang→Honghe).

RESM: Image-derived risk is linked with region-specific economic parameters (price, yield, loss rate, control cost, efficiency) to output plot-level intervention intensities and region-level recommendation maps (e.g., dosage bands, do/avoid). This integration turns recognition into actionable, economically rational guidance and reduces unnecessary interventions.

Through these contributions, CCGD-Net not only maintains high accuracy and robustness across multiple crops and diverse environments but also transforms recognition outputs into executable regional control and economic decision schemes, offering a new technical pathway toward intelligent, economical, and sustainable crop disease management.

2. Related Work

2.1. Deep Learning Methods for Crop Disease Recognition

In recent years, with the advancement of deep learning, convolutional neural networks and Transformers have been extensively applied in agricultural vision tasks, becoming core techniques in crop disease recognition [14]. Traditional recognition methods generally rely on handcrafted rules [24]. In contrast, deep learning enables end-to-end learning of multi-level semantic features from large-scale datasets, significantly enhancing both accuracy and generalization capability [15]. CNN-based architectures, such as ResNet, DenseNet, and EfficientNet, have achieved high accuracy in single-crop disease recognition tasks, particularly on benchmark datasets such as PlantVillage, where classification accuracy often exceeds 98%98% [16]. However, these models exhibit limited generalization across crops and regions because agricultural imagery suffers from heterogeneous distribution shifts, including illumination variation between greenhouse and open-field settings, morphological differences in leaves and lesion appearance across cultivars and growth stages, background and pose changes due to complex field scenes and variable leaf orientations, and sensor–climate discrepancies arising from different cameras and regional environmental conditions. These compounded shifts enlarge the domain gap and degrade performance when models are applied beyond their training conditions. The introduction of Transformer-based models into computer vision has provided new opportunities for plant disease recognition [18]. Vision Transformer (ViT) leverages the self-attention mechanism to perform global feature modeling [19]. It’s mechanism captures structural dependencies between lesions across the global context and improves recognition of complex disease patterns [20]. However, despite ViT’s superior representational capacity, its performance remains dependent on the availability of large-scale labeled data [25]. In scenarios involving distribution shifts, its generalization across crops and regions remains limited. Consequently, relying solely on CNNs or Transformers is insufficient for robust recognition in complex agricultural environments, thereby motivating the domain adaptation strategy proposed in this study.

2.2. Domain Adaptation and Transfer Learning in Agricultural Vision

To overcome performance degradation caused by data distribution discrepancies across crops and regions, various approaches based on transfer learning and domain adaptation have been developed [26]. The central idea is to align feature distributions between a labeled source domain and an unlabeled or sparsely labeled target domain, enabling the model to perform well in new environments [27,28]. In classical transfer learning, the goal is to minimize the joint loss over both domains [29]. Another class of methods directly minimizes statistical distance between feature distributions. Typical examples include maximum mean discrepancy and correlation alignment [30,31]. Their domain adaptation techniques have been widely applied in agricultural vision tasks such as leaf disease classification, fruit defect detection, and weed identification [32,33,34]. Nevertheless, due to the influence of climate, illumination, and cultivar variability, the domain gaps in agricultural imagery are often more pronounced than those in industrial domains, resulting in instability and sensitivity to noise in traditional alignment methods [35]. This issue is particularly acute in cross-crop scenarios, where lesion semantics vary considerably; relying on low-level alignment alone often leads to semantic drift [36]. Consequently, dynamic domain adaptation at the semantic level is considered a critical yet unresolved challenge in crop disease recognition [37].

2.3. Economic Decision Models for Disease Control and Regional Management

Beyond recognition accuracy, the ultimate objective of crop disease management lies in maximizing economic returns while minimizing risk. Classical agricultural economics typically incorporates risk assessment and cost–benefit analysis, using the economic threshold to determine whether control actions should be taken [38]. And incorporates probabilistic risk models and subsidy mechanisms at the regional scale. Recently, studies have begun to integrate deep learning–based disease detection outputs with agricultural economic models by transforming probabilistic recognition results into inputs for economic threshold and cost–benefit analyses, linking visual risk estimation to control decisions. Building on this trend, our CCGD-Net extends the concept by embedding a differentiable economic optimization layer that fuses disease risk maps, environmental factors, and local economic parameters to generate quantitatively optimized intervention intensities and policy recommendations. This coupling enables a closed-loop pipeline from image-based diagnosis to economically rational management, providing verifiable support for regional planning and subsidy formulation.

3. Materials and Method

3.1. Data Collection

The data collection for this study was conducted from March 2023 to September 2024 and comprises two categories: (i) locally collected datasets from three representative horticultural production regions in China—Bayan Nur (Inner Mongolia), Weifang (Shandong Province), and Honghe (Yunnan Province)—acquired by our team under institutional research agreements; and (ii) publicly available datasets from open repositories (e.g., PlantVillage, AI Challenger), used strictly under their stated open-access terms for research. A consolidated summary is provided in Table 1. In each local region, representative farmlands or greenhouse plots were selected according to local cropping systems, and disease samples of apples, tomatoes, and cucumbers were continuously recorded across growth stages to ensure temporal and spatial diversity. Field image acquisition combined plant-protection unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and handheld high-definition cameras. Although the UAV platform included both 4K visible-light and multispectral sensors (DJI Phantom 4 Multispectral; bands at 450 nm, 560 nm, 650 nm, 730 nm, 840 nm, and pan-RGB), only RGB channels were used for model training and evaluation in this study to maintain modality consistency with handheld and laboratory imagery; multispectral data were logged for potential future analysis. UAV flights were conducted at 1.5–2.5 m above the canopy with a 60° oblique angle, following a serpentine (lawn-mower) trajectory with approximately 70% forward and 60% side overlap at a ground speed of 1.8–2.2 m.s−1s−1; each sortie covered about 10 mu (≈0.67 ha) and recorded GPS coordinates for geo-referencing. Handheld photography (e.g., Canon EOS 90D, Sony Alpha 7R IV; 24–45 MP) captured close-up views of diseased organs under natural illumination from multiple angles to enrich variation in lighting, pose, leaf morphology, and lesion patterns. Laboratory imaging was performed under controlled illumination and background using high-CRI LED light sources and neutral panels to acquire flattened leaf scans as standardized references for model calibration and comparison.

Composition and quantitative statistics of collected data.

To enhance cross-domain robustness, two categories of data sources were used: (i) locally collected images from Bayan Nur (Inner Mongolia), Weifang (Shandong Province), and Honghe (Yunnan Province), obtained through institutional research collaborations; and (ii) publicly available datasets such as PlantVillage and AI Challenger–Agriculture, which were used strictly under their open-access academic licenses (e.g., CC BY 4.0). To support the subsequent economic analysis module, environmental and economic parameters were simultaneously collected from each local region, including temperature, humidity, illumination intensity, precipitation, control cost, market price, yield, and disease loss rate. Environmental data were recorded by DHT22 temperature–humidity sensors, LDR light sensors, and automated weather stations at 10-min intervals. Economic data were compiled from routine production and market records maintained by local agricultural cooperatives and technology extension centers. Specifically, control-cost components (pesticide, labor, machinery, and fuel) were extracted from farm bookkeeping and cooperative procurement ledgers; market prices were obtained from provincial agricultural bulletins and verified against weekly quotations from regional wholesale markets; and plot-level yields were taken from growers’ harvest logs and cross-checked with county-level agricultural statistics. The disease loss rate was defined as the proportion of yield reduction attributable to a specific disease relative to the expected healthy yield, expressed as (𝑌healthy−𝑌diseased)/𝑌healthy(Yhealthy−Ydiseased)/Yhealthy. It was estimated for each crop–disease combination through periodic quadrat sampling (1 m × 1 m plots) conducted during the imaging campaign, complemented by expert visual assessments from local plant-protection specialists. The resulting estimates were validated against historical yield–loss relationships reported in national agricultural extension manuals and peer-reviewed studies for apples, tomatoes, and cucumbers in the corresponding regions. All economic variables were expressed in local currency (CNY), aggregated to a weekly timescale, temporally aligned with the imaging sessions, and subjected to rigorous quality control (double-entry verification, outlier removal, and missing-value imputation) to ensure consistency and comparability across regions. After the model is trained, different deployment scenarios require different image types. For greenhouse or handheld inspections, high-resolution close-up images captured by mobile devices or portable cameras are sufficient because they contain fine-grained lesion textures for accurate classification. For open-field horticulture, the model is designed to operate effectively using only drone-based imagery. The MSFE module captures multi-scale structural cues, while the CDAM module aligns UAV domain characteristics across regions, enabling the model to identify canopy-level disease signals without relying on close-range imagery. After integration and preprocessing, a multimodal dataset comprising approximately 20,000 high-quality disease images with corresponding environmental and economic data was established, providing a robust foundation for training and validating the proposed cross-crop and cross-regional generalized disease detection model.

3.2. Data Preprocessing and Augmentation

In cross-crop and cross-regional disease recognition tasks, the multisource and heterogeneous nature of the data constitutes a primary factor affecting model performance. Significant discrepancies exist among datasets in terms of imaging conditions, acquisition devices, illumination intensity, and background interference. Without effective preprocessing, deep learning models are susceptible to noise and distribution shifts, leading to unstable feature extraction and degraded generalization capabilities. Therefore, systematic image alignment, denoising, and augmentation must be conducted prior to model training to unify the feature space and enhance information diversity. The goal of image preprocessing is to reduce non-disease-related interference and improve the distinguishability of diseased regions. 3.3. Proposed Method

3.3.1. Overview

In the proposed framework, processed disease images, along with crop identifiers and regional labels, are input in batches into a unified shared encoder. This encoder, based on a global self-attention visual backbone, performs patch embedding and long-range dependency modeling across the entire image, producing semantically aligned universal representations. These representations are then fed in parallel into multiple crop-specific decoding heads. Each branch learns crop-specific variations in color, texture, and lesion morphology on top of shared features, resulting in fine-grained representations suitable for classification and localization. During multi-task training, learnable task weights are employed to automatically balance loss contributions and gradient directions across tasks, thereby alleviating gradient conflicts caused by data imbalance and promoting inter-task knowledge transfer. Simultaneously, a cross-domain adaptation module is integrated at the intermediate feature level, where a domain discriminator and a domain attention unit are inserted. The former encourages the shared encoder to generate domain-invariant features with weak domain identity through adversarial training, while the latter assigns higher importance to critical regions based on cross-domain relevance, thus mitigating distribution shifts caused by climatic, imaging, and cultivation differences while maintaining semantic consistency. For the output stage, each crop-specific branch produces both class predictions and spatial response maps. The spatial localization is obtained using a supervised activation mapping mechanism built upon Class Activation Mapping (CAM) principles, refined with weak bounding-box or polygon annotations for a subset of the training data. This hybrid strategy combines the interpretability of CAM/Grad-CAM with explicit supervision, ensuring that lesion heatmaps are spatially consistent with annotated regions while remaining applicable to unlabeled samples through self-guided attention regularization. The resulting class logits and response maps are fused at the decision layer to yield image-level and plot-level risk scores. The identification outputs, together with spatiotemporal indices, are then input into the regional economic strategy module, which maps disease probabilities, potential yield losses, market prices, and control costs to economic thresholds and utility functions. This enables the determination of whether intervention is required for each plot and suggests optimal control intensities. Additionally, risk heatmaps and economically optimal intervention maps are generated over a gridded spatial domain. Through the pipeline of “shared encoding—crop-specific branching—domain alignment—unified prediction—economic decision-making,” CCGD-Net seamlessly integrates cross-crop shared learning and cross-regional robust transfer, transforming visual recognition results directly into spatially explicit and economically actionable management recommendations.

3.3.2. Multi-Task Shared Feature Extractor

The MSFE constitutes a core component of CCGD-Net, designed to construct a crop-invariant feature space that enables the joint learning of universal semantic features and crop-specific characteristics. As shown in Figure 1, the module adopts a symmetric multi-task architecture based on vision transformer. The input is a preprocessed disease image tensor 𝑋∈ℝ𝐻×𝑊×𝐶X∈RH×W×C. A linear embedding layer first partitions the image into fixed-size patches and projects them into a feature space with dimension 𝑑=768d=768. These embeddings, augmented by positional encodings, are fed into a transformer encoder comprising 12 layers of multi-head self-attention (MSA) blocks, each with 12 attention heads and a 3072-dimensional feedforward network. This structure enables global modeling of spatial dependencies and morphological variations among lesions, capturing both shared patterns and fine-grained differences across various crop diseases.

Within the multi-task learning framework, the universal features 𝐹𝑠Fs produced by the shared encoder are passed into multiple crop-specific decoding heads, each corresponding to a specific crop task. To facilitate dynamic interaction across tasks, a task fusion kernel is introduced at intermediate layers. It performs inter-attention operations over the query vectors 𝑄𝑖Qi, key vectors 𝐾𝑖Ki, and value vectors 𝑉𝑖Vi of each task, enabling semantic sharing and reweighting of feature representations. Specifically, attention weights for each task are compensated by features from other tasks during the fusion stage, allowing the model to preserve structural consistency while capturing individual variations, thereby enhancing cross-crop transferability. In terms of parameter design, the shared layers of MSFE contain approximately 85 M parameters, while each crop-specific decoding branch contains about 10 M, allowing stable training on a single GPU. To balance the contributions of different tasks, learnable task weights 𝑤𝑖wi are adopted to dynamically adjust the influence of each task’s loss function. The overall loss is defined as:

where ℒ𝑖Li denotes the cross-entropy loss of the i-th task, 𝜃𝑠θs represents the shared parameters, and 𝜆λ is the gradient balancing coefficient. Here, ∇𝜃𝑠ℒ𝑖∇θsLi is the gradient of the i-th task loss with respect to the shared encoder parameters, normalized to unit ℓ2ℓ2 norm to prevent scale dominance across tasks. The mean gradient ∇¯𝜃𝑠∇¯θs is computed as the arithmetic average of all normalized task gradients:

∇¯𝜃𝑠=1𝑀∑𝑖=1𝑀∇𝜃𝑠ℒ𝑖∥∇𝜃𝑠ℒ𝑖∥2.∇¯θs=1M∑i=1M∇θsLi∥∇θsLi∥2.

(14)

The balancing coefficient 𝜆λ is empirically tuned within [0.1,0.5][0.1,0.5] based on validation performance, with 𝜆=0.3λ=0.3 used in the final implementation to ensure stable gradient alignment without over-constraining task-specific learning. This formulation enforces directional consistency among task gradients in the shared feature space, mitigating gradient conflict and enhancing inter-task knowledge transfer. This design effectively mitigates gradient conflicts among tasks and enhances joint optimization stability. Mathematically, the core principle of MSFE is to establish a mapping from the input image to multi-task outputs via a shared encoder 𝑓𝑠(·)fs(·) and crop-specific heads 𝑓𝑖(·)fi(·), expressed as 𝑦𝑖=𝑓𝑖(𝑓𝑠(𝑋))yi=fi(fs(X)). Here, 𝑓𝑠(·)fs(·) captures crop-invariant features, while 𝑓𝑖(·)fi(·) learns crop-specific representations. Through joint optimization of task objectives, the model converges toward a shared latent space that encapsulates disease information across multiple crops, achieving the objective of "shared universal features and separated task-specific features" at the representation level. This design offers significant advantages for the current task. First, the multi-task shared structure enables knowledge transfer across tasks, improving learning performance for data-scarce crops. Second, the incorporation of cross-task attention fusion dynamically reweights features at the semantic level, allowing disease characteristics from different crops to be uniformly modeled in the global representation space, thereby enhancing generalization. Finally, the module provides robust high-level semantic inputs for the subsequent cross-domain adaptation module, facilitating domain alignment and feature filtering during regional transfer, and laying a solid foundation for the overall system’s stability and scalability.

3.3.3. Cross-Domain Adaptation Module

The CDAM serves as a pivotal component in the CCGD-Net framework, specifically designed to address feature distribution discrepancies across regions. Its objective is to achieve semantic-level alignment between the source and target domains through the integration of semantic consistency constraints and adversarial feature alignment mechanisms. As illustrated in Figure 2, this module comprises two core substructures: the adversarial feature aligner and the domain attention block.

3.3.4. Regional Economic Strategy Module

The RESM receives the plot-level disease risk heatmaps and crop–region embeddings from the recognition branch and constructs a differentiable decision network that integrates three data streams: spatial risk, temporal environment, and economic parameters. The objective is to generate continuous intervention intensities and optimal economic strategies at both the pixel and plot levels. As illustrated in Figure 3, the module is implemented via a two-branch encoder and one-branch decoder structure. The spatial branch takes as input a risk map 𝑅∈ℝ64×64×1R∈R64×64×1, which is processed by a stack of three convolutional layers (Conv1: 64×64×1→64×64×3264×64×1→64×64×32, Conv2: 64×64×32→32×32×6464×64×32→32×32×64, Conv3: 32×32×64→16×16×12832×32×64→16×16×128), each with stride 2, batch normalization, and GELU activation. Global average pooling is then applied to yield a vector 𝑧sp∈ℝ256zsp∈R256. The temporal–economic branch receives 24-hour environmental and price–cost sequences for each plot, denoted as 𝐸∈ℝ144×𝑑𝑒E∈R144×de (10-minute sampling interval, 𝑑𝑒=8de=8, including temperature, humidity, light, rainfall, market price, labor, and pesticide unit costs). This sequence is processed by patching (patch size = 16), linear projection (16𝑑𝑒→12816de→128), two-layer transformer encoder with four heads, and a decoder with two fully connected layers (128→256→256128→256→256), yielding 𝑧ts∈ℝ256zts∈R256. 3.4. Experimental Setup

We designed a coherent suite of experiments to verify that CCGD-Net meets the study objective of cross-crop, cross-region disease recognition with economically rational guidance. (i) Overall performance: we compared CCGD-Net with representative CNN/ViT baselines and domain-adaptation variants under identical partitions to demonstrate end-to-end recognition effectiveness. (ii) Ablation study: we removed MSFE, CDAM, and RESM in turn to isolate their roles in multi-scale representation, domain alignment, and economically consistent decision outputs, thereby linking architectural choices to measurable improvements. (iii) Cross-region/cross-crop generalization: we trained on one region (or crop subset) and tested on disjoint regions (or held-out crops), enforcing non-overlap of plots, dates, and acquisition sessions; this assesses transferability and reflects real deployment where agroecology and imaging conditions differ. Regarding label scope, the model is a multi-class classifier: it distinguishes healthy versus multiple disease categories and supports organ-aware recognition (leaf, fruit, whole plant/canopy) through class labels and spatial risk maps. In this study, quantitative results primarily report leaf-level categories (handheld/laboratory images) and canopy-level signals (UAV imagery); fruit-level diseases are supported by the framework and can be included when sufficient annotated data are available.

Through the integration of the aforementioned hardware environment and hyperparameter configurations, a favorable balance was achieved among computational efficiency, model accuracy, and result stability throughout the experiments; determinism flags were enabled where applicable to limit variance beyond intended randomness.

3.4.3. Baseline Models for Comparative Evaluation

In the comparative experiments of this study, six representative and widely adopted baseline models were selected, including ResNet50 [39], EfficientNet-B4 [40], Swin Transformer [41], DeiT [42], DANN [43], and DSAN [44]. ResNet50 represents a classical residual network architecture, in which residual connections are introduced to address the vanishing gradient problem commonly observed in deep networks. This design enables stable training and enhances the model’s feature representation capacity. EfficientNet-B4 adopts a compound scaling strategy that efficiently balances depth, width, and input resolution, resulting in high parameter utilization and a favorable trade-off between accuracy and computational cost. Swin Transformer, as a hierarchical vision transformer, incorporates a shifted window attention mechanism that maintains global modeling capabilities while significantly reducing computational complexity. DeiT (data-efficient image transformer) improves transformer performance under limited data availability by leveraging a distillation strategy, exhibiting low data dependency and strong generalization ability. DANN (domain-adversarial neural network) is a classical adversarial domain adaptation model that achieves cross-domain feature alignment by minimizing classification loss while simultaneously maximizing the loss of a domain discriminator, thereby mitigating distribution shifts between source and target domains. DSAN (domain-symmetric adaptation network) introduces a symmetric architecture and minimizes discrepancies in multi-layer feature distributions to achieve more stable semantic transfer, maintaining high adaptation accuracy under complex domain shift scenarios. For fairness, all baseline models were trained under identical experimental settings, including the same data augmentation pipeline, learning rate schedule, batch size, and early stopping strategy. This standardized configuration guarantees that performance differences reflect intrinsic model capacity rather than training discrepancies.

4. Results and Discussion

4.1. Overall Performance Comparison

This experiment was conducted to comprehensively evaluate the overall performance of the proposed CCGD-Net in crop disease recognition tasks and to compare it against representative mainstream models. By applying a consistent data partitioning scheme and unified evaluation metrics, including accuracy, F1-score, mAP, and parameter count, the comparative analysis aimed to assess the models’ accuracy, robustness, and generalization capability, thereby validating the effectiveness and advancement of the proposed MSFE, CDAM, and RESM in practical scenarios.

As shown in Table 2, conventional convolutional neural networks such as ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B4 exhibited relatively lower performance in both accuracy and F1-score, primarily due to their reliance on local texture features and lack of global context modeling capabilities. Transformer-based architectures such as Swin Transformer and DeiT demonstrated improved performance, with Swin Transformer benefiting from its hierarchical sliding window design that effectively integrates local receptive fields with global semantics. However, these models did not account for cross-domain data distribution discrepancies, limiting their generalization ability. The introduction of domain adaptation mechanisms in Swin+DANN and Swin+DSAN yielded further improvements, indicating the effectiveness of aligning source and target domain feature distributions in mitigating regional transfer barriers. The proposed CCGD-Net outperformed all comparative models across all three metrics, which can be attributed to targeted mathematical design optimizations. The MSFE module enhanced the model’s capability to represent complex lesion morphology through multi-scale feature fusion, while the CDAM module achieved semantic alignment across regions by modeling contextual relationships. The RESM module further improved the model’s responsiveness to regional semantic features. These modules introduce dynamic matching mechanisms and attention modulation strategies within high-dimensional spaces, substantially enhancing adaptability to both intra-domain and inter-domain distribution variations, ultimately leading to superior accuracy and robustness.

Overall performance comparison between baseline models and the Proposed CCGD-Net (mean ± std over 5 runs).

4.2. Ablation Study

This experiment was designed to validate the individual contributions of each module in the CCGD-Net through ablation analysis. By successively removing the MSFE, CDAM, and RESM, the roles of each component were evaluated using accuracy, F1-score, and mAP metrics. Additionally, a configuration using only the backbone network, Swin Transformer, was included as a baseline to quantify the explicit performance gains introduced by the proposed modules.

As shown in Table 3, the full CCGD-Net configuration achieved the best performance across all evaluation metrics, with an F1-score of 89.8% (±0.4) and consistent gains over all ablated variants. A significant drop was observed when the MSFE module was removed (F1 83.2% ±0.8), highlighting its critical role in comprehensive feature extraction; training logs further show slower convergence and higher false negatives on fine-grained lesions, indicating that multi-scale convolution–fusion improves boundary fidelity and preserves high-frequency textures across spatial scales. Removing the CDAM module also led to considerable degradation (F1 85.6% ±0.7), underscoring its function in addressing distribution shifts between source and target domains; proxy shift metrics (e.g., higher domain discriminator accuracy and increased MMD between region-specific embeddings) confirmed larger inter-domain variance when CDAM is absent, while attention-guided alignment in CDAM reduced this discrepancy and improved calibration (lower ECE). Although the RESM module contributed a relatively smaller improvement in pure recognition metrics (F1 89.8% vs. 87.2% ±0.6 without RESM), its inclusion significantly enhanced decision quality: threshold stability improved, false-positive interventions decreased in low-return regimes, and simulated economic return increased under the same operating point, suggesting that economically aware regional semantics provide complementary, decision-relevant information. We also observe synergistic effects—MSFE and CDAM together yield larger gains than either alone—implying that richer multi-scale features benefit more from domain-level alignment. Collectively, the three modules form an integrated architecture with MSFE, CDAM, and RESM, which jointly drive the observed accuracy, robustness, and decision efficacy improvements.

4.3. Cross-Region Generalization Results

This experiment evaluates the generalization capability of CCGD-Net under cross-region and cross-crop settings while explicitly preventing data leakage. For cross-region verification, the training set and the test set are drawn from disjoint geographic regions (e.g., train on Weifang and test on Honghe), with no overlap in fields/plots, growers, or acquisition sessions; images captured from the same plot or on the same date never appear across splits. For cross-crop verification, when assessing transfer across crop types, the model is trained on a subset of crops and tested on held-out crops in a different region, ensuring that leaf/plant identities, plots, and collection dates do not intersect. Device- and modality-specific biases are controlled by not sharing raw sessions across splits (handheld, UAV, and laboratory sessions are assigned wholly to either train or test), and image normalization/statistics are computed on training data only. Hyperparameters are tuned on a region-specific validation set without access to target-region labels; early stopping and model selection use the validation loss from the source side. This protocol, which differs from conventional same-region evaluation, better matches real deployment requirements where agroecology, climate, background, and device conditions shift between regions. It thus provides a stringent benchmark of transferability, representational generality, and robustness for cross-domain disease recognition.

As shown in Table 4, CCGD-Net demonstrated high accuracy and F1-scores across all six cross-region pairs, with performance fluctuations within a narrow range of less than 2 percentage points, indicating strong cross-regional adaptability. Notably, transfers from Weifang to Honghe and from Weifang to Bayan Nur yielded the highest F1-scores of 88.0% and 87.8%, respectively. This superior performance may be attributed to the diversity present in the Weifang dataset, particularly in lighting conditions, lesion morphology, and background complexity, which likely enabled the model to learn more generalizable discriminative features during training. From a mathematical perspective, this robustness can be ascribed to the MSFE and region-aware attention mechanisms, which allow the model to capture statistically stable mid-to-high frequency lesion patterns during training. These representations remain reliable even under regional domain shifts. Furthermore, the latent space alignment strategy introduced by the CDAM module effectively compresses distribution discrepancies, enabling consistent and robust representations in the target domain. Overall, the results confirm that CCGD-Net possesses strong cross-region recognition capabilities, making it suitable for practical deployment in real-world agricultural disease monitoring across diverse geographic regions.

4.4. Discussion

4.4.1. Parameter Interpretation and Sensitivity

From a theoretical standpoint, the Regional Economic Strategy Module (RESM) can be viewed as a differentiable decision layer that maps disease risk p to an intervention intensity 𝛿∈[0,1]δ∈[0,1] by maximizing a concave profit functional Π(𝛿)=𝑉𝑌[1−ℓ𝑝(1−𝜂𝛿)]−𝐶0𝛿−Γ𝛿2Π(δ)=VY[1−ℓp(1−ηδ)]−C0δ−Γδ2. The strict concavity in 𝛿δ yields a unique optimum with interior solution 𝛿∗(𝑝)=(𝑉𝑌ℓ𝜂𝑝−𝐶0)/(2Γ)δ∗(p)=(VYℓηp−C0)/(2Γ) (projected to [0,1][0,1] if necessary). This expression provides clear economic meaning for each parameter: V (unit price) and Y (baseline yield) scale the upside; ℓ (unit damage rate) and 𝜂η (mitigation efficiency) govern how risk converts to recoverable loss; 𝐶0C0 (marginal control cost) raises the action threshold; and Γ>0Γ>0 (smoothness/penalty) regularizes overly aggressive prescriptions. Comparative statics follow directly: 𝛿∗δ∗ increases monotonically with 𝑝,𝑉,𝑌,ℓ,𝜂p,V,Y,ℓ,η and decreases with 𝐶0,ΓC0,Γ. Sensitivity is locally bounded—𝛿∗δ∗ is Lipschitz in p with constant (𝑉𝑌ℓ𝜂)/(2Γ)(VYℓη)/(2Γ)—so larger ΓΓ improves stability (smaller response to small probability perturbations) at the expense of responsiveness, while smaller ΓΓ sharpens responsiveness but may amplify noise. Analogously, the first-order effect of 𝐶0C0 on 𝛿∗δ∗ is negative (tightening the economic trigger), whereas higher 𝜂η expands the effective response range to risk. When spatial regularization is included via a graph Laplacian weight 𝜆λ, the global objective remains strictly concave, and 𝜆λ controls the trade-off between local precision and regional consistency in the decision surface without altering uniqueness. Together, these properties establish an interpretable and robust mapping from probabilistic risk to economically rational interventions, and clarify how parameter choices balance economic gain, decision stability, and spatial coherence.

4.4.2. Operational Deployment Across Regions and Crops

In real-world agricultural production, the timely identification and scientific control of crop diseases are essential for safeguarding yield and quality, especially in multi-region and multi-crop cultivation systems where traditional manual inspection suffers from low efficiency, subjectivity, and delayed response. The proposed CCGD-Net framework addresses these challenges by centering on economically guided intelligent disease detection, and integrating multi-scale feature extraction with cross-domain adaptation mechanisms. Under multi-region datasets, the model demonstrated strong robustness and generalization, offering effective technical support for intelligent disease management in agriculture. For example, in tomato cultivation bases in Bayan Nur, field images can be captured in real-time using portable devices or drones equipped with visual modules. The model can rapidly perform disease recognition on-device or via cloud inference, triggering prescription recommendation systems for precise intervention, reducing pesticide usage, and improving control efficiency. Meanwhile, in greenhouse-based horticultural systems in Weifang, CCGD-Net can be integrated with existing sensor networks and environmental control systems to automatically detect early disease symptoms in crops such as cucumber and pepper. This facilitates rapid warnings of disease onset and location, and supports temperature-humidity regulation and targeted spraying operations. In southern regions like Honghe, where high humidity and complex disease spectra prevail, conventional models are often vulnerable to background shifts and lighting interference. The cross-domain recognition capabilities of the proposed method, especially the adaptive learning of regional image distribution via the CDAM module, significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy and efficiency in such environments. Moreover, through the RESM module’s incorporation of economic control strategies, users can not only obtain risk-level predictions but also receive prevention recommendations grounded in economic return assessments, thereby achieving an integrated pipeline of recognition, evaluation, and intervention. Hence, CCGD-Net offers not only theoretical innovation but also practical value, making it highly applicable across multiple regions, crops, and agricultural scenarios, and driving the advancement of agricultural intelligence.

4.4.3. Producer Decision Process and Integration of CCGD-Net

In practical production, growers typically follow a cyclical decision process that moves from monitoring to action and feedback. First, multi-source sensing (portable/handheld cameras, UAVs, and greenhouse camera nodes) acquires plot-level images together with routine environmental streams (temperature, humidity, illumination, rainfall). Second, the recognition branch of CCGD-Net (MSFE with CDAM) produces disease probabilities and spatial risk heatmaps that are robust to cross-region domain shifts. Third, the Regional Economic Strategy Module (RESM) fuses risk maps, crop–region semantics, and routine economic parameters (control cost, market price, baseline yield) to infer continuous intervention intensity and a ranked list of economically rational options (e.g., no action, reduced-dose spraying, localized treatment). Fourth, the prescription is executed via existing integrated pest management (IPM) workflows—such as targeted spraying tasks, canopy microclimate regulation in greenhouses, or UAV route planning—while safety, label compliance, and residue standards are enforced by on-farm SOPs. Finally, execution outcomes (scouting confirmations, spot checks of lesion regression, yield and cost logs) are fed back to the system for threshold adjustment and periodic model updates, closing the loop between recognition, evaluation, and intervention. To support adoption in heterogeneous settings, CCGD-Net provides two integration paths. On the edge side, lightweight inference (TensorRT/ONNX) enables on-device recognition for portable terminals and UAV payloads with sub-second latency, suitable for field scouting and rapid triage. On the cloud side, batch inference and RESM optimization generate plot-level prescription maps, which are exposed through dashboards or mobile apps and can be exported to variable-rate controllers. The human-in-the-loop configuration allows growers or agronomists to review suggested intensities, adjust operational constraints (e.g., buffer zones, weather windows, budget ceilings), and log final decisions. This alignment of technical outputs with growers’ operational constraints and economic objectives translates model predictions into accountable actions, improving timeliness of control, reducing unnecessary pesticide input, and stabilizing yield and quality across regions and crops.

4.5. Limitation and Future Work

Despite the strong performance of CCGD-Net in multi-region crop disease recognition tasks, several limitations remain. First, the model retains a degree of dependency on the availability of training data from the target region. Second, although the CDAM module enhances cross-region adaptability, it faces challenges in reconstructing feature distributions under extreme climatic variations or with uncommon crop varieties, limiting its generalization capability. Furthermore, the RESM module currently adopts a static economic parameter-based strategy, without incorporating dynamic input-output data or real-time market prices, which restricts the flexibility of the decision strategies in practical settings. Future research will address these limitations in three directions. First, self-supervised learning and generative adversarial methods will be explored to improve the model’s adaptability in few-shot and unlabeled domains, enhancing regional transferability. Second, multi-modal sensor data such as temperature, humidity, and gas indicators will be incorporated to build a joint modeling framework of imagery and environmental information, strengthening early disease detection in both stability and timeliness. Third, agricultural economic simulators and yield prediction models will be integrated, enabling the dynamic updating of control strategies to better align with real-world economic decision-making needs.

5. Conclusions

Across datasets from three regions, the Proposed CCGD-Net model outperformed mainstream baselines, achieving 91.6% accuracy, an F1-score of 89.8%, and an mAP of 88.9%. All three modules—multi-scale feature extraction (MSFE), cross-domain attention matching (CDAM), and regional economic strategy (RESM)—demonstrated enhanced and complementary performance. Integrating these modules into existing IPM workflows would help align field decisions with cost–benefit considerations and environmental objectives. These findings confirm the effectiveness and robustness of the Proposed CCGD-Net in cross-regional horticultural disease recognition and economic decision support.
