Abstract

BACKGROUND

Crop diseases can lead to significant yield losses and food shortages if not promptly identified and managed by farmers. With the advancements in convolutional neural networks (CNN) and the widespread availability of smartphones, automated and accurate identification of crop diseases has become feasible. However, although previous studies have achieved high accuracy (>95%) under laboratory conditions (Lab) using mixed data sets of multiple crops, these models often falter when deployed under field conditions (Field). In this study, we aimed to evaluate disease identification accuracy under Lab, Field, and Mixed (Lab and Field) conditions using an assembled data set encompassing 14 diseases of apple (Malus × domestica Borkh.), potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.). In addition, we investigated the impact of model architectures, parameter sizes, and crop-specific models (CSMs) on accuracy, using DenseNets, ResNets, MobileNetV3, EfficientNet, and VGG Nets.

RESULTS

Our results revealed a decrease in accuracy across all models from Lab (98.22%) to Mixed (91.76%) to Field (71.55%) conditions. Interestingly, disease classification accuracy showed minimal variation across model architectures and parameter sizes: Lab (97.61–98.76%), Mixed (90.76–92.31%), and Field (68.56–73.81%). Although CSMs were found to reduce inter-crop disease misclassifications, they also led to a slight increase in intra-crop misclassifications.

CONCLUSION

Our findings underscore the importance of enriching data representation and volumes over employing new model architectures. Furthermore, the need for more field-specific images was highlighted. Ultimately, these insights contribute to the advancement of crop disease identification applications, facilitating their practical implementation in farmer's fields. © 2024 Society of Chemical Industry.

1 INTRODUCTION

Crop diseases stem from a broad spectrum of organisms, encompassing fungi, viruses, bacteria, and nematodes. Their detrimental impact extends beyond merely impeding crop growth and production. They often precipitate food shortages, particularly in developing countries.1 Early and precise identification of specific diseases is paramount for making effective crop protection decisions. Nevertheless, the accurate diagnosis of many diseases poses challenges for farmers and agronomists alike. Manual diagnosis, in particular, is time-consuming and subjective.2 The ability of farmers to correctly identify crop diseases varies considerably (e.g., in 1986, 11% of cereal farmers failed to positively identify powdery mildew), potentially leading to the improper use of fungicides.3 Consequently, automated identification and documentation systems are indispensable to mitigate misidentifications and enhance the efficacy of crop protection measures.

Technological advancements in smartphones, big data, and computer vision (CV) algorithms have paved the way for automating accurate disease identification. In general, the process of correctly identifying crop diseases using CV algorithms involves two key steps: feature extraction and disease classification. Traditional CV algorithms typically involve the manual design of feature extractors, followed by the application of classic machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Machine (SVM) for disease classification.4 Although these methods have demonstrated high accuracy under laboratory conditions (i.e., images with a plain background), the process of tweaking feature extractors can be quite tedious. In addition, traditional CV algorithms have rarely been applied under field conditions.5 Recently, deep learning, especially convolution neural networks (CNNs), has emerged as a promising approach for image classification,6, 7 object detection,8 etc. In agriculture, CNNs have also shown potential advantages in crop disease identification,9-11 weed detection,12-14 and other applications.

Numerous CNN models have been employed for crop disease diagnosis, including AlexNet,7 VGGNet,15 ResNet,6 GoogleNet,16 MobileNet,17 and DenseNet.18 The majority of these models have demonstrated superior performance compared with traditional CV algorithms.19, 20 For instance, Ramcharan et al.21 conducted a comparative study on the performance of CNN models, SVM, and K-Nearest Neighbor using field-captured cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz) images, revealing that CNN models achieved the highest accuracy at 93%. Similarly, Brahimi et al.10 compared the performance of six traditional CNN models with three strategies (scratch, fine-tuned fully connected layers, fine-tuned all layers) using the Plant Village data set (https://www.kaggle.com/vipoooool/new-plant-diseases-dataset), reporting an accuracy of 99.76% for Inception-V3. Furthermore, Too9 evaluated the performance of six CNN models employing transfer learning technology, with DenseNet achieving an accuracy of 99.75%.

In addition, transfer learning and data augmentation have significantly enhanced the accuracy of crop disease diagnosis. For instance, DenseNet with 121 layers achieved an impressive accuracy of 99.75% in just 30 epochs when utilizing weights trained on the ImageNet data set, without exhibiting any signs of overfitting.9 Furthermore, the training time was notably reduced. In the realm of data augmentation, Ma et al.2 conducted a comparative analysis of CNN accuracy employing four augmentation strategies in cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) disease diagnosis, revealing an increase in accuracy from 86.8% to 93.4% with augmentation techniques such as rotation and flipping, among others. Similarly, Abayomi-Alli et al.22 using four image-processing techniques (down sampling, Gaussian blurring, motion blurring, and over-exposure) to degrade the quality of original cassava images, subsequently training a modified MobileNet-V2 model. The testing results demonstrated a statistically significant improvement.

Furthermore, a lightweight CNN model has proved adequate for handling typical crop disease data sets of relatively small size. For instance, Atila et al.23 compared the performance of EfficientNet family on the Plant Village data set, revealing that EfficientNet B0 achieved an impressive accuracy of 99.88% with the smallest model parameter size and shorter training time. Similarly, Rahman et al.11 devised a lightweight CNN model and assessed its performance against other CNN models using field-captured rice (Oryza sativa L.) disease images. Their findings showed that the designed model achieved a commendable accuracy of 93.3%, and VGG-16 achieved a slightly higher accuracy of 97.12%, albeit with a larger model size. Notably, lightweight CNN models have been specifically tailored for mobile devices, maintaining relatively high accuracy while reducing model size. Several effective models have emerged, including MobileNets, EfficientNet, and ShuffleNet.24-26 Although transformer-based models, such as vision transformer,27 have recently gained prominence, the importance of a small model remains crucial, especially if the model is intended for direct deployment on smartphones for crop disease diagnosis.

Despite the significant advancements in crop disease diagnosis achieved by CNN models, challenges persist. First, previous research has seldom focused on the use of these developed models under field conditions.9, 10, 28, 29 Although nearly all models have demonstrated very high accuracy (>95%) under laboratory conditions (e.g., the Plant Village data set), certain challenges such as complex backgrounds and disorders with similar symptoms remain.30 Second, some studies have reported accuracies exceeding 90% on field data sets, yet their data-splitting strategy was flawed. The data sets were split post data augmentation, resulting in both training and test sets containing similar characteristics (augmented images), leading to artificially inflated accuracy metrics. To be applicable in real-world field scenarios, the test set should be sourced from various databases.31, 32 Finally, most studies utilized data sets comprising multiple crops for training CNN models. However, in practical field settings, crop-specific models (CSM) for disease diagnosis would be more pragmatic (e.g., tomato farmers are primarily concerned with tomato diseases). Employing a model trained on a mixed crop disease data set may lead to misclassifications across different crops.32 In addition, disease identification using resource-limited mobile devices poses a significant challenge. For example, VGG-16 requires 500 MB of memory and boasts 138 million parameter sizes, rendering it unsuitable for user-friendly mobile application.33 Consequently, lightweight models have been proposed for practical crop disease identification in real field conditions.11, 34

This study evaluates five lightweight CNN architectures with varying parameter sizes across three conditions: Lab (images with plain backgrounds), Field (field-captured images with complex backgrounds), and Mixed (Lab and Field images mixed together). A total 33 768 images representing 3 crops [apple (Malus × domestica Borkh.), potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.)] and encompassing 14 disease types were collected and processed under these conditions. The images were meticulously divided into training, validation, and test sets. The study further analyzes the impacts of image capture conditions, model architectures and parameter sizes, and CSMs on classification accuracy. The findings are expected to facilitate the adoption of deep learning (DL) techniques under field conditions and aid farmers with limited crop disease knowledge in implementing accurate and effective crop protection measures.

Lab images were captured under controlled conditions with plain backgrounds (Fig. 1, upper), whereas Field images were sourced from actual crop fields with complex backgrounds (Fig. 1, lower). Mixed images were the combination of images from both Lab and Field conditions (Fig. 1, middle).

Lab images were sourced from the Plant Village data set,35 and the Apple Diseases data set,36 the latter encompassing five types of diseases. Original images of the Apple Diseases data set underwent augmentation technology such rotation, flipping, adjustment of sharpness. To ensure that the same image with different augmentation methods was not included in both training and test sets, augmented images were manually removed, retaining only the original images. Field images were obtained from multiple sources, including the Plant Doc data set,37 the Apple Disease data set,36 the Plant Pathology Challenge 2020 – FGVC7 (PPC),38 and a search engine (https://image.baidu.com/). The Plant Doc data set comprised 2568 images, representing 17 diseases across 13 crops. From the PPC data set, which contained 3642 images of 3 diseases (apple rust, apple scab, and multiple diseases) as well as healthy leaves, we selected images specifically for apple scab and apple rust to align with disease types in other data sets. Additional crop disease images were collected using Baidu Image (https://image.baidu.com/), with crop disease terms (in both Chinese and English) serving as keywords for image retrieval. These terms were cross-referenced with those in the Plant Village data set. Subsequently, crop disease experts labeled the collected images, resulting in the labeling of more than 1800 images. Figure 1 provides examples of diseases captured under Lab, Mixed, and Field conditions.

In total, our data set comprised 17 423 images under Lab conditions and 4821 images under Field conditions. The names of the diseases for the three crops, along with the corresponding number of images under each condition, are provided in Table 2.

These five CNN model architectures are widely employed in smartphone-based plant disease classification tasks and are mainstream in the CNN-based CV community.4, 40, 41 We utilized two sub-versions of the latest MobileNet V3 model, namely MobileNet V3-small and MobileNet V3-large, because they are lightweight models developed specifically for mobile devices. In addition, we included EfficientNet B0 because its input size (224 × 224) was consistent with other CNN models used. EfficientNet variants beyond B0 (B1–B7) were not used because of their higher computational requirements associated with increasing input tensor size. ResNets with five depths (18/34/50/101/152) were also utilized, leveraging their shortcut structure to enable deepening of the CNN. The parameter sizes of these models ranged from 11.69 million to 60.19 million. Classic VGG Nets were included for their simple architectures composed of a stack of convolution layers, with the parameter sizes exceeding 100 million across VGG-11, VGG-13, VGG-16, and VGG-19. Finally, DenseNets models with 121, 161, 169, and 201 layers were utilized because of their smaller parameter sizes and densely-connected convolution layers.

2.3 Generic versus crop-specific models

To assess the effectiveness of training CSMs in reducing inter-crop disease misclassifications, we trained 4 types of models for each of the 16 CNN architectures. These models comprised one generic model (GM) that did not differentiate between crops and three crop-specific models (CSM-Apple, CSM-Potato, CSM-Tomato).

The GM was trained separately under Lab, Mixed, and Field conditions using the total data set containing disease images of all three crops. Subsequently, we partitioned the total data set into three subsets based on crop types (apple, potato, and tomato) and image conditions (Field, Mixed, and Lab). CSM-Apple, CSM-Potato, and CSM-Tomato were then trained using the individual crop data set across the three conditions. Consequently, each type of model, including GM, CSM-Apple, CSM-Potato, and CSM-Tomato, comprised 16 CNN trained models, resulting in a total of 64 models across the 3 conditions. It is worth noting that the individual crop data set varied in the number of images and disease types (Table 2).

2.4 Data processing, split, and model training

The workflow of this study is illustrated in Fig. 2. Initially, we combined the disease images obtained under Lab and Field conditions to create the data sets for the Mixed conditions. Subsequently, the total data set was utilized to train the 16 GM models. In addition, the individual crop data set was employed to train the CSM models separately.

Workflow of comparison between generic models (GM) and crop-specific models (CSMs) under Lab, Mixed and Field conditions. Strategy A was GM trained with all crop data sets, whereas strategy B was CSMs trained with the individual crop data set (apple, potato and tomato).

These data sets were randomly divided into three subsets: a training set (comprising 60% of the images), a validation set (20% of the images), and a test set (20% of the images). The training and validation sets were utilized for fine-tuning the model's hyperparameters, whereas the test set was reserved for independent evaluation of the models. Following data splitting, data augmentation techniques were exclusively applied to the training set to prevent the introduction of similar characteristics in both training and test sets.31 Image augmentation methods included vertical and horizontal flipping, random rotation of images by 30/45/60/90°, addition of random noise, adjustment of image contrast, etc. All images were in RGB format, and were resized to 224 × 224 × 3 pixels to match the requirement of the CNN models. Transfer learning was also adopted, with the initial weights pre-trained from the ImageNet data set.42

The CNN models were implemented using Python 3.743 and Pytorch 1.11,44 and were trained using a graphics processing unit (GPU) and the CUDA 11.1 toolkit. The experimental hardware configuration consisted of an Intel(R) Core (TM) i7-8700K CPU running at 3.70 GHz with 32 GB of memory, paired with a GPU of NVIDIA GeForce GTX 3060 equipped with CUDA 11.1 and 12.0 GB of memory. Hyperparameter settings for the training are shown in Table 4. By default, the models were trained for 100 epochs with early stopping method (patience = 5) to avoid overfitting during training. The weights of the model that demonstrated the best performance on the validation set were retained.45

3 RESULTS

3.1 Accuracies across models and image conditions

Table 5 illustrates a notable increase in classification accuracy for the test set, significantly progressing from Field conditions (mean = 72%) to Mixed conditions (mean = 93%), and showing a slight further increase under Lab conditions (mean = 99%). The differences in accuracy across model architectures and parameter sizes were minor (<5%), yet more pronounced for Field conditions compared with Lab conditions. On average, accuracy was 2% lower for the test set than for the validation set under Field conditions, and 0.5% lower under Mixed conditions. However, under Lab conditions, there was virtually no difference between the validation and test sets. Across all three conditions and for both validation and test sets, the small model MobileNetV3-Small yielded the lowest accuracy, whereas the DenseNet-161 model demonstrated the highest accuracy. Surprisingly, VGG models with the highest parameter size did not achieve the highest accuracy. In addition, increasing the number of layers in these CNN models resulted in slight improvements in classification accuracy.

3.2 Generic versus crop-specific model

Under Field conditions, CSMs exhibited higher accuracy for potato (+13%) and tomato (+5%) diseases compared with the GM, with only a 1% increase for apple diseases (Fig. 3). Under Mixed conditions, CSMs demonstrated higher accuracy for potato diseases (3%), while achieving only a marginal improvement for apple and tomato diseases (1%). Interestingly, the utilization of CSMs did not enhance image classification accuracy under Lab conditions, because the accuracy was already exceptionally high (>99%) with the GM. Notably, the model accuracy for both GM and CSMs for potato and tomato significantly increased from Field to Mixed and Lab conditions, whereas accuracy saw only a slight increase for apple as conditions transitioned from Field to Mixed and Lab conditions.

3.3 Recall changes across diseases models and conditions

CSMs enhanced the recall (in comparison with GM) for all of the diseases except apple Alternaria blotch, which decreased by 1% under Field conditions and 2% under Lab conditions (Fig. 4(c)). Notably, under Field conditions, the most significant improvements in recall were observed for potato early blight and late blight, with recall increasing by 20% and 9%, respectively. Regarding tomato diseases, the recall for disease identification increased for all diseases (ranging from 1% to 8%) except for tomato yellow virus. The recall for the majority of other diseases remained consistent for both GM and CSMs.

Figure 5 illustrates the impact of training CSMs on mitigating inter-crop misclassifications compared to GM under Field conditions. In general, CSMs resulted in the reduction of inter-crop misclassifications for 9 of 14 diseases. The most significant improvements were observed for potato late blight (6%) and tomato yellow virus (5%). However, the decrease in inter-crop misclassifications did not directly translate into an increase in recall, because intra-crop disease misclassifications intensified. For instance, although the recall for potato early blight classification increased by 4%, 2% were erroneously classified as early blight.

3.4 Changes in model accuracy when cross-tested

When models trained under Field conditions were applied to Mixed and Lab conditions, the accuracy for GM dropped from 72% to 41% and 33%. Similarly, the accuracy of CSM-Tomato dropped from 62% to 29% and 25%. Although the accuracy for CSM-Apple experienced a slight decline, there was a slight increase in accuracy for CSM-Potato (Fig. 6, left). Upon evaluation of GM and CSMs trained under Lab conditions (Fig. 6, center), a marginal decrease in accuracy was observed when these models were applied under Mixed conditions, and a significant decrease was noted when they were applied under Field conditions. These patterns were consistent across both GM and CSMs. For models trained under Mixed conditions, there was minimal change in accuracy when tested under Lab conditions, but a decrease in accuracy was evident under Field conditions.

4 DISCUSSION

Accurate identification of crop diseases is essential for implementing effective protection measures. In this study, we trained a variety of CNN models with diverse architectures, utilizing different data representations (Lab, Mixed, and Field), and employing various methods (GM and CSMs). We believe that our findings offer valuable insights into the application of DL technology in crop protection across a range of conditions.

4.1 Data representation and volumes override model architectures parameter sizes

Our results indicated that models with varying parameter sizes and architectures exhibited similar accuracies (differences of <5% among models) in crop disease identification, whereas models trained and tested with data sets from Lab, Mixed, to Field conditions showed significantly differences with accuracies ranging from 72% to 99%. The minimal variation in accuracy across different model architectures suggests that all selected established CNN models possess comparable and adequate feature extraction and learning capabilities. For our specific classification task, the potential to employ a new architecture to enhance model performance appears limited, whereas the benefits associated with enriching the data representation are substantial. This outcome may be attributed to the clear and simple features present in Lab images, which could be easily extracted by all CNN models, even when using smaller models or data sets for training.10, 47 Interpretable algorithms, such as Grad-CAM, have also demonstrated successful identification of disease disorders.11, 19, 48, 49 Therefore, a deeper and more complex architecture is unnecessary for Lab images. However, images acquired from real fields present challenges in locating disease symptoms owing to complex backgrounds. Given the limited number of Field images, employing a deeper architecture would not yield significant benefits. Instead, increasing the number of images would prove more beneficial, as depicted in Fig. 7, where accuracy under Field conditions exhibited rapid and linear improvement with an increasing number of images. Certain diseases (apple rust, and apple scab) under Field conditions achieved accuracy exceeding 90% because of the relatively large number of images used for training. Furthermore, variations in the difficulties of locating and extracting symptoms among the 14 diseases contributed to differences in accuracy responses to the size of training data sets, particularly for Field data sets.30, 50 These findings align with previous reports indicating minimal changes in accuracy with increasing model size.9, 32, 37, 51, 52

Figure 7
Open in figure viewerPowerPoint

Differences in the relationship between number of images and mean test accuracy under three image conditions. Field (square) indicates images acquired under field conditions. Lab (circle) indicates images acquired under laboratory conditions. Mixed (triangle) indicates images acquired under both field and laboratory conditions.

We observed a decrease in model accuracy when models were cross-tested under different conditions, except for models trained under Mixed conditions and applied under Lab conditions (Fig. 6). This outcome underscores the significance of data representation, indicating that improving data representation is the most effective approach for increasing model accuracy.29 When applying these models in real-world scenarios, consideration of the training condition is essential to avoid misusage. We speculate that the higher accuracy observed for models trained under Mixed conditions and used under Lab conditions, compared with models trained and used under Mixed conditions, may be attributed to the mixed images providing sufficient knowledge to correctly identify diseases in the simpler Lab images. Furthermore, our comparison with Ferentinos32 revealed that the models trained under Lab conditions and tested under Field conditions had significantly lower accuracy than those trained under consistent conditions. Similarly, the accuracies had no significant difference across model architectures. This parallel finding underscores the significant impact of data representation on model performance, highlighting the risk of employing models not tailored to the application scenario. Barbedo31 also emphasized the importance of image data set size in bringing DL to agricultural fields, further supporting our conclusion regarding the primacy importance of data over model architectures and parameter sizes.

4.2 The pros and cons of crop-specific models

Compared with GM, CSMs generally exhibited higher accuracy in disease identification (Fig. 3). This increase in accuracy was primarily achieved by reducing inter-crop disease misclassification with CSMs. However, CSMs slightly elevated the intra-crop misclassifications. The marginal increase in accuracy was observed for apple diseases, potentially due to the relatively larger number of images available, resulting in already high accuracy levels. The primary challenge in accurately identifying diseases using these models may stem from the visual similarity among different diseases and the complexity of backgrounds in field images, such as apple Alternaria blotch and apple grey spot.53 Conversely, for potato and tomato diseases, the smaller number of Field images and the misclassifications between them (Fig. 5) contributed to lower accuracy with GM. CSMs successfully circumvented misclassifications between potato and tomato diseases, leading to improved accuracy. Furthermore, the fewer potato disease types played a role in the more significant enhancement in accuracy for potato compared with tomato.

The advantages of employing CSMs over GM were more pronounced under Field conditions compared with Mixed and Lab conditions. Furthermore, CSMs exhibited significantly higher accuracy than GM for potato and tomato diseases, while showing only marginal improvement for apple diseases. Misclassifications of disease images under Field conditions may have been exacerbated by complex backgrounds. For example, Ferentinos32 provided specific instances to illustrate the misclassifications between tomato diseases and banana diseases using models trained under Mixed conditions. Reducing the number of model outputs, that is, limiting the number of disease types, could potentially mitigate the risk of misclassification. To our knowledge, no prior studies have explored the impact of confining the number of disease types to a specific crop. Barbedo31 noted a clear relationship between the number of disease types and classification accuracy, because an increase in disease types raises the likelihood of misclassifying a disease into a similar category. Consistent with these findings, CSMs enhanced accuracy by minimizing inter-crop misclassifications. However, this underscores the necessity of specifying crops before employing CSMs. In practical applications, users of CSMs must first identify the crop they are cultivating. Although this additional step presents an extra layer of complexity compared with utilizing a GM, most farmers should be able to navigate this requirement effectively.

4.3 Limitations

A primary limitation observed was data imbalance, particularly evident in the discrepancy between the number of disease images captured in Lab and Field conditions. However, our findings indicated that CNN models achieved high accuracy even with a small data set size under Lab conditions (Fig. 7). In addition, there was an imbalance in the number of disease images across different crops under Field conditions, with apple having 2375 images, potato 665 images, and tomato 1781 images. This imbalance introduced uncertainties when comparing the performance of models across crops (Fig. 3). Despite apple having a reasonable number of images, CSM-Apple's accuracy under Field conditions closely matched previously reported accuracies.51, 54 Conversely, public data sets for potato and tomato were limited, and some private data sets were inaccessible. Most studies focused on disease identification accuracy using potato or tomato images solely under Lab conditions (e.g., the Plant Village data set).55-58 Therefore, more high-quality field disease images are a pressing need for effectively applying these models in farmers’ fields.

5 CONCLUSION

We assembled a data set comprising the disease images of three crops (apple, potato, and tomato) captured under Lab, Field, and Mixed conditions. Sixteen CNN models were trained using the all data sets (GM) as well as crop-specific data sets (CSMs). Our findings revealed that the accuracy of disease identification under Field conditions was notably lower compared with the accuracy under Mixed and Lab conditions. Interestingly, under Lab conditions, all models achieved satisfactory accuracy, even with a relatively small number of training sets. The variation in accuracy among different model architectures and parameter sizes was minimal across all conditions. Furthermore, employing CSMs showed a slight improvement in disease identification accuracy for all crops, with a notable reduction in inter-crop misclassifications. Based on our results, we conclude that the data representation and volumes, rather than model architectures and parameter sizes, pose the primary limitation for real-world application of disease identification models. Therefore, prioritizing the collection of more representative images under Field conditions is crucial to facilitate the adoption of DL models in farmers’ fields.
