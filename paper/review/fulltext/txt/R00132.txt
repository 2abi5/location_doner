Abstract:

Detecting plant diseases is vital for maintaining agricultural productivity and ensuring food security. Advances in computer vision, particularly with Vision Transformers (ViTs), have shown significant potential in improving the accuracy and efficiency of plant disease identification. This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of various ViT parameters to determine the most effective configuration for this purpose. SECTION I.

Introduction

Agriculture, a fundamental pillar of human sustenance, ensures food security for the ever-growing global population. However, plant diseases pose a significant threat to agricultural productivity, leading to substantial economic losses and food supply disruptions. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), plant diseases cause an estimated annual loss of approximately 220$
billion globally [1], [2]. Effective management of plant diseases is crucial, as they can severely impact crop yield and quality, ultimately threatening food security and the livelihoods of farmers [2].

Traditional methods of plant disease detection rely on manual inspection by experienced farmers, which can be time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to errors due to the subjective nature of visual analysis [3], [4], [5]. The need for more reliable, efficient, and scalable solutions has driven the adoption of advanced technologies in agriculture. Recent advancements in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have revolutionized the field of plant disease detection and classification, enabling systems to identify and classify automatically plant diseases with high accuracy.

Despite the potential of ML and DL, their application in plant disease detection presents several research challenges such as: Data Availability and Quality, Model Configuration with best parameters, Generalization Ability, Deployment in Real-World Settings [6], [7], [8], etc.

DL models, particularly CNNs, have been widely used for image recognition tasks, including plant disease detection [9]. However, CNNs have limitations in capturing long-range dependencies within images, which are critical for accurate disease identification from leaf images. Vision Transformers (ViTs) have recently emerged as a promising alternative, offering several advantages over traditional CNNs. ViTs can process entire images in parallel and are adept at capturing complex patterns and relationships within image data, making them particularly suitable for high-resolution plant leaf images where minute details are critical for accurate diagnosis [10]. The application of ViTs in plant disease detection is still a burgeoning area of research, but the potential benefits are immense. Early and accurate detection of plant diseases can significantly reduce crop losses, enhance food security, and support sustainable agricultural practices. However, several challenges remain, including the lack of large, diverse, and annotated datasets that encompass a wide variety of plant species and disease conditions. Additionally, the optimal configuration of ViT parameters for plant disease detection has not been thoroughly investigated.

This study aims to address these gaps by exploring the best ViT parameters for detecting plant diseases from leaf images. By systematically evaluating various ViT configurations on publicly available datasets such as PlantVillage, Taiwan Tomato Dataset, and BananaLSD Dataset, which include images of leaves affected by different diseases, we aim to determine the optimal set of parameters that yield the highest classification accuracy Key aspects to be examined include model depth, attention heads, and patch sizes. Additionally, Fig. 1 showcases sample leaf images from the PlantVillage, Taiwan Tomato, and BananaLSD datasets, providing a visual understanding of the data used in our experiments.

Using the capabilities of ViTs, this research seeks to improve the accuracy and efficiency of plant disease detection systems. Through this work, we hope to advance the state-of-the-art in the detection of plant disease and contribute to the broader field of agricultural technology. In this work, we present a comprehensive study on optimizing ViT parameters for plant disease detection using multiple datasets. The key contributions of our study are as follows.

Extensive Evaluation of ViT Parameters: We conducted a thorough investigation into various ViT parameters, including patch sizes, image resolutions, embedding dimensions, the number of transformer blocks (depth), the number of heads in the multi-head attention layer and the dimension of the MLP layer (FeedForward). These evaluations were performed using the PlantVillage dataset to ensure robustness and reliability.

Optimal Parameter Selection: Through detailed experiments, we identified the optimal ViT configuration for the detection of plant disease: image size =224×224
, patch size = 16, embedding dimension = 512, depth = 6, number of heads = 8, and MLP dimension = 1024. This configuration achieved the highest accuracy of 99. 77% in the PlantVillage data set while balancing computational efficiency.

Performance Metrics Analysis: We analyzed the performance metrics (Loss, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 Score, and Time) for different configurations of ViT parameters. Our results indicated that smaller patch sizes and lower embedding dimensions significantly improved accuracy while maintaining manageable training times and computational resources.

Cross-Dataset Validation: The selected optimal ViT parameters selected were validated on additional datasets, namely the Taiwan Tomato and BananaLSD datasets. This cross-dataset validation demonstrated the generalizability and robustness of our parameter selection across different plant species and disease conditions.

Comparison with Traditional CNNs: We compared the performance of our optimized ViT model with traditional convolutional neural network architectures, specifically VGG 19 and AlexNet. Our ViT model not only achieved superior accuracy but also required significantly fewer trainable parameters and storage space, highlighting its efficiency.

Scalability and Adaptability of ViTs: We emphasized the scalability and adaptability of ViTs for the detection of plant diseases. ViTs can handle varying image sizes and resolutions effectively, making them suitable for various agricultural environments. Their ability to capture global context through self-attention mechanisms improves the detection of subtle and dispersed disease symptoms.

State-of-the-Art Comparison: Our approach was compared with recent state-of-the-art plant disease detection methodologies across the three databases. The results indicated that our optimized ViT model outperforms existing methods in all performance metrics, demonstrating its effectiveness and potential for broader application in the detection of plant diseases.

Practical Implications: The findings of our study provide practical guidelines for researchers and practitioners in selecting appropriate ViT parameters to maximize model performance while considering computational efficiency. This contributes to the development of highly effective and deployable models for plant disease detection in real-world agricultural scenarios.

The structure of this article is as follows: Section II reviews previous research on plant disease classification and detection. In Section III, we outline the methodology for the proposed Optimized ViT Model for Plant Disease Detection. Section IV presents the experimental results and compares our findings with existing state-of-the-art methods from the literature. Finally, Section V offers concluding remarks and future directions.

SECTION II.

Related Works

Recent studies have demonstrated significant advancements in the detection and classification of plant diseases using DL and computer vision techniques. Several approaches have been proposed, each leveraging different architectures and methodologies to enhance performance and robustness.

Chen et al. [11] focused on enhancing the learning capability for minute lesion features in rice plants. They employed the MobileNet-V2 model with an added attention mechanism and optimized loss function, achieving an impressive identification accuracy of 99.67% on a public dataset. Zhao et al. [12] proposed a convolutional neural network (CNN) based on Inception and residual structures, integrated with a modified convolutional block attention module (CBAM). Their model demonstrated an overall accuracy of 99.55% for identifying diseases in corn, potatoes, and tomatoes, outperforming traditional models in both accuracy and computational efficiency. Moving on, Thakur et al. [13] introduced the “PlantXViT,” a model combining ViTs with traditional CNNs for plant disease identification. Their approach effectively identified multiple plant diseases across several crops, showcasing the potential of ViTs in plant pathology applications.

Astani et al. [14] analyzed and classified 13 classes of tomato disease using ensemble classification. Their method, tested on the PlantVillage and Taiwan tomato leaves datasets, achieved 95.98% accuracy, outperforming several DL models. Tabbakh et al. [15] presented a hybrid model (TLMViT) for plant disease classification, combining transfer learning with ViTs. This model demonstrated high validation accuracy on the PlantVillage and wheat datasets, proving the efficiency of ViTs in deep feature extraction. Moving forward, Bhuiyan et al. [16] introduced BananaSqueezeNet, a model using DL and Bayesian optimization for banana leaf disease detection. Their model achieved high accuracy, precision, and recall, outperforming several prominent CNN models.

Besides, Iftikhar et al. [17] explored an optimized CNN architecture (E-CNN) for early detection of leaf diseases in apple, corn, and potato crops. Their fine-tuned model achieved a notable accuracy of 98.17%, emphasizing the impact of hyperparameter optimization and data augmentation. Bouacida et al. [1] tackled the challenge of model generalization in plant disease detection. They proposed a small Inception model architecture focused on recognizing diseased leaf regions, achieving high accuracy even on new datasets. Rizwan et al. [18] developed a novel CNN model aimed at small-scale farmers. Their model outperformed state-of-the-art models with an average accuracy of 96.86%, emphasizing the balance between performance and computational complexity.

Similarly, Shafik et al. [19] introduced two plant disease detection models using deep transfer learning and ensemble techniques. Their models demonstrated exceptional robustness and generalization capabilities, achieving high accuracy across multiple plant diseases and datasets. Linfeng et al. [20] proposed a lightweight Siamese neural network for tomato leaf disease identification. Their method showed consistent accuracy even with limited data, proving effective in resource-limited environments. Ouamane et al. [21] enhanced tomato disease classification using a Higher-Order Whitened Singular Value Decomposition (HOWSVD) and Multilinear Discriminant Analysis (MDA). The proposed method uses tensor techniques [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. Their approach significantly improved accuracy on the PlantVillage and Taiwan datasets. Shetty et al. [26] proposed a model incorporating advanced filtering and clustering techniques for banana disease detection. Their approach achieved high accuracy on multiple datasets, highlighting the effectiveness of their preprocessing and feature extraction methods.

These studies collectively underscore the rapid advancements in using DL and computer vision for plant disease detection, each contributing unique methodologies and achieving high performance in various agricultural contexts.

A. Limitations of Existing Approaches

Despite the significant advances in plant disease detection using deep learning models, several limitations hinder the widespread adoption and effectiveness of these approaches in diverse agricultural settings. Many existing models, such as those based on deep CNNs like MobileNet-V2 and Inception networks, require substantial computational resources, making them less feasible for resource-limited environments, particularly for small-scale farmers [11], [12]. Additionally, some models struggle with overfitting and generalization, particularly when dealing with novel crop and disease types that were not included in the training dataset [1], limiting their applicability to real-world scenarios where unseen variations are common. The issue of limited training data also poses a challenge, as approaches like the lightweight Siamese neural network proposed by Linfeng et al. show reduced accuracy when trained on limited data, a critical issue in agricultural settings where large annotated datasets are often unavailable [20]. Complex model architectures, such as ensemble methods and hybrid models combining multiple neural networks, can be difficult to implement and optimize, requiring expertise in model tuning and potentially being computationally expensive [14], [15]. Furthermore, many studies focus on specific crops or diseases, limiting the model’s applicability across different agricultural contexts. For example, Bhuiyan et al. concentrated on banana leaf diseases, which may not generalize well to other crops [16]. Finally, while some models achieve high accuracy, their implementation in real-time systems is not always addressed, limiting their practical usability in dynamic farming environments where timely disease detection is crucial [17].

B. Advantages of the Proposed Model

By addressing the limitations of existing approaches, our proposed model offers a robust, efficient, and user-friendly solution for plant disease detection, ultimately contributing to enhanced agricultural productivity and sustainability. The model is optimized for low computational resources by utilizing a ViT, ensuring high efficiency without demanding extensive resources, making it suitable for deployment in resource-constrained environments like small-scale farms. Additionally, the model enhances generalization by incorporating transfer learning and training on a diverse dataset, allowing it to perform well across various crops and diseases. The integration of attention mechanisms further refines the model’s focus on relevant features, reducing overfitting and improving robustness to new data.

Moreover, our approach is effective even with limited data, thanks to advanced data augmentation techniques that artificially expand the training dataset. This tackles the common issue of data scarcity in agricultural applications. The model architecture is simplified to balance complexity and performance, making it easier to implement and fine-tune compared to more intricate ensemble methods or hybrid models, thus reducing the need for extensive expertise and computational resources. The model’s broad applicability is ensured by training on a wide range of plant diseases across different crops, making it versatile for various agricultural settings. Finally, the model is designed for real-time implementation, particularly in mobile applications, providing immediate feedback and enabling timely action for managing plant diseases, thus enhancing its practical utility for farmers.

SECTION III.

Optimized ViT Model for Plant Disease Detection

ViTs have become a strong alternative to CNNs for diverse computer vision tasks, including plant disease detection. The key benefit of ViTs is their use of self-attention mechanisms, which allow them to capture long-range dependencies and contextual information more efficiently than traditional CNNs [27], [28]. This section outlines the optimal parameters for ViTs in the context of plant disease detection and discusses their impact on model performance. Fig. 2 illustrates the architecture of the proposed ViT model optimized for plant disease detection. The model begins by dividing the input image into non-overlapping patches, with each patch flattened and passed through a linear projection layer to create patch embeddings. Positional embeddings are then added to these patch embeddings to retain spatial information, which is essential in maintaining the structure of the plant image for effective disease identification. An additional learnable class token is included, which aggregates global image features to aid in classification. The core of the model is the transformer encoder, which consists of multiple layers of multi-head self-attention and feedforward networks. These layers allow the model to learn both local and global relationships within the image. Residual connections and normalization layers ensure stable and efficient learning. Finally, the class token output is passed through an MLP classification head, which predicts whether the plant is healthy or identifies the specific disease class. This architecture leverages the self-attention mechanism to detect subtle and dispersed disease symptoms, making it highly effective for agricultural applications.

H. Saliency Map Integration

We incorporated saliency map visualizations to analyze and interpret the regions of the plant leaf images that contributed most to the model’s predictions. This method not only enhances the interpretability of our ViT-based approach but also provides valuable insights into the decision-making process of the model. These saliency maps were generated using Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++ techniques and were particularly useful in validating the robustness of the model across datasets.

I. Cross-Dataset Validation

To ensure the generalizability of the optimized ViT parameters, we conducted cross-dataset validation using the Taiwan Tomato and BananaLSD datasets. Unlike traditional single-dataset evaluations, this approach tested the adaptability of the model to new data distributions and varying plant species, demonstrating its robustness in diverse agricultural contexts.

J. Domain-Specific Fine-Tuning

A lightweight fine-tuning strategy was employed to adapt the ViT model to new datasets efficiently. This process involved updating only the last classification layer and freezing the transformer encoder layers, significantly reducing training time and computational requirements.

A. Datasets

In our quest to evaluate the proposed approach of plant leaf disease classification, we have rigorously tested our method on three diverse and challenging datasets: PlantVillage, Taiwan Tomato, and BananaLSD. These datasets were chosen due to their varied characteristics, representing different types of crops and disease challenges, thus providing a comprehensive assessment of our model’s robustness and generalizability.

The PlantVillage dataset includes a vast array of images from various crops and is widely used in the research community for benchmarking plant disease detection models. The Taiwan Tomato dataset focuses specifically on tomato plants, providing images of leaves affected by different diseases, which helps in assessing the model’s performance on a single crop type. The BananaLSD dataset, on the other hand, presents images of banana leaves suffering from leaf spot disease, adding another layer of complexity with a focus on a specific disease.

1) PlantVillage

The PlantVillage dataset [30] is an extensive and widely-utilized repository of images designed for the study of plant health and disease detection. It contains 54,323 images representing 14 crop species across 38 categories of diseases and healthy plants, along with a separate class of 1,143 images depicting backgrounds without leaves. Each image is labeled with the specific plant species and the type of disease, making this dataset a crucial resource for the development and assessment of machine learning models for automated plant disease diagnosis. The images are in RGB color format and have a resolution of 256×256
pixels.

2) Taiwan Tomato Dataset

The initial dataset for Taiwan tomato diseases [31] is relatively small and intended to evaluate the proposed model. It comprises 622 leaf samples, each with a resolution of 227×227
pixels, categorized into six classes, encompassing both healthy and various diseased conditions. These classes include Bacterial spot (100 samples), Black mold (67 samples), Gray spot (84 samples), Healthy (106 samples), Late blight (98 samples), and Powdery mildew (157 samples). Additionally, there is an expanded dataset that includes more samples for each category. Thus, the final dataset contains a total of 4,976 tomato leaf images, including augmented samples. For this dataset, 80% is used for training, 10% for validation, and 10% for testing, adhering to standard practices in the field.

3) BananaLSD Dataset

The Banana Leaf Spot Diseases (BananaLSD) dataset [32] consists of images collected from the experimental fields of Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujibur Rahman Agricultural University (BSMRAU) and various banana fields across Bangladesh. Multiple smartphones were used to capture these images. The dataset includes 937 images classified into four categories: Healthy (129 samples), Pestalotiopsis leaf blight (173 samples), Sigatoka (473 samples), and Cordana (162 samples). An expert plant pathologist labeled these images according to their respective classes.

B. Data Augmentation

Data augmentation seeks to alter the original training data to generate more artificial data, thus enhancing the chances of exposing the model to a wider range of features during training. Image augmentation techniques typically fall into two main categories: photometric distortions, which modify hue, saturation, contrast, and brightness, and geometric distortions, which encompass rotation, translation, resizing, shearing, zooming in or out, and horizontal or vertical flipping of images. In our study, we used the following transformations:

Random Rotation: Randomly rotates the image within a range of −10 to +10 degrees to increase data variability.

Random Horizontal Flip: Horizontally flips the image with a 50% probability, making the model invariant to horizontal flips.

Random Vertical Flip: Vertically flips the image with a 50% probability, ensuring invariance to vertical flips.

Random Affine Transformation: Applies affine transformations, such as translation and shearing, to add variations to the data.

Random Resized Crop: Selects a random portion of the image and resizes it to a specific size, enhancing data augmentation.

Normalization: Adjusts the pixel values to have zero mean and unit variance, thereby stabilizing the training process.

These transformations form an essential pipeline for preprocessing and augmenting images before using them to train DL models. Table 1 shows the number of images before and after data augmentation for each database used in this work.

E. Select the Best ViT Parameters

In this stage of the experiments, we will study the interpretation of many of the variables used in ViT for plant disease detection. The parameters studied are patch sizes, image resolutions, embedding dimension, number of transformer blocks (depth), number of heads in the multi-head attention layer (Heads), and the dimension of the MLP (FeedForward) layer. To facilitate the process of selecting these parameters, we will use the largest and most widely used evidence base, PlantVillage. This dataset provides a comprehensive collection of plant disease images, enabling robust evaluation of the model’s performance.

After determining the best ViT parameters using the PlantVillage dataset, we will validate the chosen parameters on the Taiwan Tomato Dataset and BananaLSD dataset. These additional datasets will help ensure that the selected parameters generalize well across different types of plants and disease conditions, providing a comprehensive assessment of the model’s effectiveness in diverse scenarios. SECTION V.

Conclusion

In this study, we conducted an extensive evaluation of ViT parameters to identify the optimal configuration for plant disease detection. Through systematic experimentation on the PlantVillage, Taiwan Tomato, and BananaLSD datasets, we determined the best combination of parameters, which included an image size of 224×224
, a patch size of 16, an embedding dimension of 512, a depth of 6 transformer blocks, 8 heads in the multi-head attention layer, and an MLP dimension of 1024. Our results indicate that this particular ViT configuration markedly surpasses traditional convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures, including VGG 19 and AlexNet. The Best ViT Parameters model not only achieves higher accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 scores across all evaluated datasets but also necessitates significantly fewer trainable parameters and storage space. This efficiency is essential for practical applications where computational resources are constrained.

Furthermore, the Best ViT Parameters model showcases excellent generalization capabilities, maintaining high performance even on datasets with different plant species and disease conditions. This robustness is essential for developing reliable plant disease detection systems that can be deployed in diverse agricultural environments. Unlike traditional CNNs, which may perform well during training but falter with unseen data, the ViT approach remains consistent, highlighting its potential for real-world applications. Besides, our comparison with state-of-the-art methods reaffirms the effectiveness of the selected ViT parameters. The superior performance metrics and the ability to handle varying image sizes and resolutions without significant performance loss make ViTs highly adaptable and scalable for large datasets.

However, the model’s complexity and computational requirements present challenges for deployment in resource-constrained environments, such as small-scale farms with limited access to high-performance hardware. Additionally, while the model was validated on the Taiwan Tomato and BananaLSD datasets, the generalizability of the approach across a wider variety of plant species and disease conditions needs further exploration.

For future work, the focus should be on refining the model to reduce its complexity and computational demands without compromising accuracy. This could involve exploring lightweight versions of ViTs or hybrid models that combine the strengths of CNNs and ViTs. Additionally, enhancing the model’s ability to generalize across different datasets and real-world conditions will be crucial. Another avenue for exploration is the integration of multi-modal data, such as combining spectral, temporal, and spatial information, to improve the robustness of plant disease detection systems.
