                                       Abstract
    In modern hydroponic agriculture systems, precise monitoring of environmental
    and growth parameters is essential. Unsupervised machine learning, particularly
    anomaly detection (AD), offers a promising tool for automation and quality con-
    trol. This study investigates the behavior of patch distribution modeling (PaDiM)
    under domain shift using strawberry images from two datasets. As the source
    domain, the Riseholme-2021 dataset is employed, which contains high-resolution
    images of outdoor-grown strawberry fruits. The target domain is a novel dataset
    comprising lower-resolution images of hydroponically grown strawberry plants
    in an indoor environment. Experiments were conducted across thirteen domain-
    mixing ratios, with performance evaluated using the area under the receiver
    operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and F1-score. The results show that the
    F1-score increases with a higher proportion of target-domain data, while AUROC
    peaks at intermediate mixtures, indicating that combining source and target data
    enhances generalization. Qualitative analysis confirms complementary strengths:
    source data improves robustness under low-light and blurred conditions, whereas
    target data performs better under high exposure but tends to misclassify foreign
    objects as anomalous.

1 Introduction
To address the mounting global demand for food, agricultural practices must evolve
to adapt to increasingly limited resources. The 2024 Global Hunger Index (GHI) as-
signs a world hunger score of 18.3 (moderate), indicating that hunger remains a serious
issue till 2030 [1]. At the same time, the world’s population, currently about 8.2 bil-
lion, is projected to grow to roughly 9.7 billion by 2050, increasing the demand for
food [2]. Hydroponics offers a promising approach to these challenges by enabling crop
production independent of soil and local climate. Modern hydroponic systems, typi-
cally implemented in greenhouses or indoor vertical farms, circulate precisely balanced
nutrient solutions to plant roots, supported by environmental sensors that optimize
growth conditions. Because plants are grown in inert media or suspended, they avoid
soil-borne constraints (such as nutrient leaching, climate changes or pathogens) and
can be cultivated year-round.
    Hydroponic setups thus allow tight control of water, nutrients, and other environ-
mental factors to maximize plant growth. Nutrients in hydroponic systems are
recirculated and matched precisely to plant needs, virtually eliminating runoff losses.
The controlled conditions also lower pest pressure, often minimizing pesticide use. As
these systems scale in complexity and economic importance, precise and automated
monitoring becomes crucial for ensuring high-quality, efficient production [3]. This
includes not only the continuous tracking of sensor-based metrics but also the visual
inspection of plant health. Tasks that can be enhanced through intelligent computer
vision systems.
    As promising as hydroponic cultivation is, its application in computer vision
remains challenged by the limited availability of training data. In particular, hydro-
ponic strawberry datasets contain relatively few images, and anomalous cases are even
scarcer [4]. This scarcity constrains the applicability of conventional supervised learn-
ing methods, which require large, annotated datasets. Unsupervised anomaly detection
(AD) therefore represents a compelling alternative, as it relies solely on normal data
to learn a baseline representation and does not depend on explicit anomaly labels.
    Since no publicly available datasets of hydroponically grown strawberries exist,
datasets of field-grown plants represent one possible complementary resource [5].
However, differences in lighting, background structures, and growth environments dis-
tinguish field from hydroponic imagery and may affect model transferability. In this
context, it can be advantageous to combine the breadth of field data with even small
amounts of hydroponic samples, as this allows models to incorporate domain-specific
characteristics while still leveraging the diversity of field-grown datasets. Investigating
this is central to understanding how robust computer vision systems for automated
monitoring in agriculture can be designed under conditions of limited data availability.
    The present study investigates the impact of including both target domain data to
achieve a domain shift and using a state-of-the-art unsupervised AD method. To this


                                            2
end, multiple datasets combining different proportions of field-grown strawberry data
and a novel hydroponic dataset, consisting of images captured from an indoor farming
system, were constructed. By examining the impact of gradually increasing the amount
of hydroponic data, the study aims to identify the effects which occur as the model
learns increasing amounts of the new domain data. The underlying hypothesis is that
exposure to even modest amounts of target domain data can substantially improve
the model robustness, thus enabling reliable AD in hydroponic systems.

2 Related work
2.1 Anomaly detection (AD)
AD refers to the identification of data points or events that deviate from established
“normal” behavior, effectively indicating the presence of anomalies. Commonly used
in this context are unsupervised learning techniques, including one-class models, which
rely solely on normal (non-anomalous) data for training. These methods are prevalent
because, in many real-world applications, labeled examples of anomalies are rare,
costly to obtain, or difficult to define comprehensively. This allows models to detect
deviations without prior knowledge of specific anomaly types [4, 6, 7].
    Unsupervised visual AD methods can be broadly categorized by how they model
“normality.” Reconstruction-based methods learn to recreate normal inputs and detect
anomalies via reconstruction error. For example, Autoencoders, Generative Adversar-
ial Networks (GAN), or Diffusion Models are trained on normal samples so that inputs
containing defects yield large residuals when reconstructed [5].
    In contrast to reconstruction-based approaches, embedding-based methods rely on
the extraction of meaningful feature representations from deep neural networks to
characterize normality. The selected patch distribution model (PaDiM) [8] serves as
an example and will be discussed in detail in a subsequent section.
    Finally, hybrid or plug-in approaches aim to combine these paradigms. For
instance, the recent CostFilter-AD module is designed as a post-processing plug-in
that refines anomaly scores and can be integrated with either reconstruction-based
or embedding-based detectors [5]. Such hybrid designs strive to improve detection by
leveraging both precise reconstruction and discriminative feature matching.

2.2 Domain adaptation and mixed-domain training
Obtaining large quantities of labeled data for every new environment or application
domain is often costly, time-consuming, and impractical. This limitation poses a sig-
nificant challenge to model generalization and motivates the application of DA, a form
of transductive transfer learning in which the task remains the same while the data
distributions differ between a source and a target domain [9]. Such distribution shifts
can result from variations in lighting, resolution, texture, or view-point. To alleviate
the need for repeated data annotation, several DA techniques have been proposed,
including domain-invariant feature learning, domain mapping, and normalization
statistic alignment [9]. These methods have demonstrated notable improvements in
cross-domain performance across various applications [10–12].


                                           3
    In addition to conventional DA techniques, mixed-domain training has emerged as
a related but distinct strategy. The term lacks a standardized definition in current liter-
ature, yet it typically refers to approaches that integrate data from multiple domains,
rather than aligning them explicitly. Unlike DA, which often aims to minimize feature
distribution discrepancies between domains, mixed-domain training involves blending
source and target domains to construct a shared training space. The degree and depth
of mixing can vary significantly.
    For instance, Ma et al. [13] propose a method in the context of medical image seg-
mentation, where intermediate domain samples are generated by interpolating both
pixel- and feature-level representations between a labeled source domain and multi-
ple unlabeled domains. These intermediates serve as transitional samples, allowing
smoother knowledge transfer. In contrast, Vincent et al. [14] construct a mixed-domain
training set by combining labeled samples from different domains and introduce a
semi-supervised approach for multimission semantic segmentation of Martian surfaces,
aiming to enhance robustness and cross-domain generalization.
    The domain-mixing method presented in this work adapts the approach of Vincent
et al. [14] and applies it to an unsupervised AD task.

3 Proposed method
To investigate the research question, a structured experimental framework was con-
structed. This framework was designed to assess the performance and generalization
capacity of a chosen AD model across domains with varying levels of target domain
representation in the training data.

3.1 Overview of experimental design
Figure 1 visualizes the experimental design of this work. It begins with the evaluation
and selection of an AD model by testing various models on the target dataset. The
chosen model is finetuned and then trained on constructed mixed-domain datasets
and its performance is assessed. Afterwards, additional datasets with finer granularity
are constructed. The final step is to thoroughly evaluate all the configurations to see
how performance on the target dataset is affected by different domain-mixing ratios.

3.2 Data preparation
To conduct the experiments, two image datasets capturing strawberry plants in various
growth stages and conditions are used.

3.2.1 Source domain: modified Riseholme-2021 dataset

comprises 3,520 images of strawberries captured in various growth stages taken from
an open-field polytunnel farm [15]. The samples are categorized into four classes: ripe,

signs of illness, mold, or other deformities.

3.2.4 Patch distribution model (PaDiM)
The model used in this paper, PaDiM, leverages the distribution of patch-level embed-
dings across normal training images, to detect outlier samples. As illustrated in
Figure 5, a pretrained convolutional neural network (CNN) is employed to extract fea-
ture maps from multiple semantic levels. Specifically, activation vectors are sampled at
corresponding spatial positions from three different layers of the CNN, each capturing
information at a different scale and level of abstraction. These vectors are then concate-
nated to form a unified patch embedding vector xij , representing the local region at
position (i, j ) in the image. For each such spatial location, the set of embedding vec-
tors obtained from all training images, Xij = {xij |k ∈ [1, N ]}, is assumed to follow a
multivariate Gaussian distribution N (µij , Σij ), where µij and Σij denote the sample
mean and covariance matrix respectively. This probabilistic modeling, which includes
correlations between feature dimensions and between semantic levels, allows PaDiM to
capture the variability of normal patterns at each patch position in a principled way.
Unlike methods which rely on non-parametric nearest-neighbor comparisons during
inference, PaDiM encodes the normal class compactly via parametric distributions,
enabling faster and more scalable anomaly localization. [8]

3.2.5 Finetuning
The base PaDiM model is adjusted to achieve optimal performance on the hydroponic
test dataset. The following adjustments were made:

• Utilizing larger backbone architecture WideResNet50, due to its higher feature
  representation capacity compared to smaller alternatives such as ResNet18
• Limiting feature extraction to the third convolutional layer, as this configuration
  has been shown to provide a favorable balance between semantic abstraction and
  spatial detail for anomaly localization.
• The number of features retained after dimensionality reduction was set to 550, in
  alignment with recommendations from [8] and to accommodate the increased feature
  dimensionality of the WideResNet50 backbone.
• Furthermore, the backbone was not pretrained, in contrast to standard practice, to
  allow the model to better adapt to the specific complexity and variability of the
  domain-specific datasets used in this study.

3.2.6 Mixed-domain training experiments
To evaluate the effects of incorporating target domain data, five initial training config-
urations were defined, varying the ratio of source and target data in 25% increments, as
shown in Figure 6. This initial series aims to provide a general overview of performance
trends across the full range of domain compositions.
    Based on the outcomes of these experiments, additional configurations were intro-
duced in the intermediate range between 55% and 85% target domain data, together
with supporting points to ensure stability of the observed trends, as shown in Figure 7.
    To maintain a consistent dataset size across all configurations, the number of sam-
ples was limited to 462 anomalous and 2,033 normal instances per training set (accord-
ing to the max data samples from the source data). For each configuration, samples
were randomly selected from the respective domains based on the defined ratio and
then shuffled to generate the final training dataset. To ensure statistical robustness and
mitigate variability caused by random initialization or sampling effects, each mixed-
domain configuration was generated three times using independent random draws.

3.2.7 Quantitative metrics
In the following section, the metrics used for the quantitative evaluation of the domain-
mixing experiments are introduced. The AD task is formulated as a binary image-level
classification problem, where each image is classified as either normal or anomalous.
Four possible outcomes are considered. A true positive (TP) refers to a normal image
that is correctly identified as normal, while a true negative (TN) denotes an anomalous
image that is correctly detected as anomalous. A false positive (FP) occurs when an
anomalous image is incorrectly classified as normal, and a false negative (FN) refers
to a normal image that is mistakenly identified as anomalous.
    From these basic outcomes, several key performance metrics are derived. Recall,
also known as the true positive rate, is defined as

                                    R=                                               (1)
                                         TP + FN

and defines the proportion of actual normal images that are correctly identified.
Precision is defined as
                                           TP
                                   P =                                            (2)
                                        TP + FP
and refers to the proportion of images predicted as normal that are normal. The false
positive rate (FPR) is given by

                                             FP
                                  FPR =                                              (3)
                                           FP + TN

and refers to the proportion of anomalous images that are incorrectly classified as
normal.
     Based on the recall and false positive rate, the Area Under the Receiver Operat-
ing Characteristic Curve (AUROC) is computed. The AUROC quantifies the model’s
ability to distinguish between normal and anomalous images across all possible clas-
sification thresholds. A value of 1.0 indicates perfect discrimination, while a value of
0.5 corresponds to random guessing.
     In addition, the F1-Score is used as a threshold-based evaluation metric. It is
calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a single measure
that balances both quantities:

     Together, AUROC and F1-Score offer complementary perspectives: while AUROC
evaluates the ranking quality of anomaly scores, the F1-Score assesses classification
quality at a chosen threshold, making them suitable for assessing performance trends
across varying training data compositions.

5 Discussion
The following points interpret and summarize the core findings of the domain-mix-
ing experiments with PaDiM. Each observation provides insight into the behavior of
the algorithm under different training data configurations, contributing to a better
understanding of domain transfer effects.

5.1 Robustness to lighting conditions
The improved performance of models trained with more source domain data on dark
or blurry images is likely attributable to the higher resolution and visual clarity of the
source dataset in this specific case and may not be observed in domains with different
image characteristics. This outcome appears counterintuitive at first, as the hydro-
ponic dataset includes augmented samples such as brightness variation and defocus.
Nonetheless, the Riseholme-2021 dataset may provide more structurally consistent
and noise-free features, which proves beneficial for low-contrast scenarios. In contrast,
target domain-trained models show higher resilience under bright and overexposed
conditions, where source domain-based models are more likely to fail. This suggests
that washed-out features and reduced contrast remain challenging for all configura-
tions, but hydroponic models offer slightly better adaptability to such conditions,
presumably due to their broader exposure to different conditions classified as normal.



                                               16
Fig. 12 Example of potential False Positives


5.2 Differences in anomaly detection sensitivity
Models trained with more source domain data demonstrate a more aggressive detec-
tion behavior, frequently misclassifying normal image regions as anomalous. Target
domain models tend to overfit, especially to uncommon patterns. This suggests that
decision thresholds are often tuned to maximize sensitivity, which can come at the
cost of precision. This behavior may be explained by a model’s increased sensitivity to
structurally uncommon yet semantically normal features particularly those underrep-
resented in the training data. Overall, these findings indicate that both models tend
to react cautiously to ambiguous regions, which affects the interpretation of anomaly
maps and contributes to false positive variation.

5.3 Misclassification of foreign objects
Strings, although present in both datasets, are more frequently misclassified as anoma-
lies by hydroponic-trained models. Source domain-trained models handle such features
more robustly, likely due to more consistent or contextually embedded representation
in the training data. The reason for this discrepancy remains unclear and warrants
further investigation.

5.4 Generalization
Despite not being present in the source domain training data, flowers in the test images
were not classified as anomalies by any configuration. This indicates a level of semantic
abstraction in the learned normality concept, suggesting that the models can distin-
guish structurally novel yet contextually normal elements from true anomalies. This
result highlights the generalization potential of AD models in unsupervised settings.
    The highest AUROC values occur at intermediate domain-mixing ratios, partic-
ularly those favoring a higher proportion of hydroponic data. This suggests that
inclusion of target domain data improves generalization, though the optimal com-
position remains unclear. Despite this, the source domain model still shows a slight
performance advantage in some metrics. This may be due to subtle statistical or struc-
tural properties in the source domain images that facilitate sharper decision boundaries
under fixed thresholds.
    Each dataset provides distinct strengths but also introduces domain-specific biases
when used in isolation. A combined training dataset allows the model to learn from
a broader distribution of visual patterns, thereby reducing overfitting to localized
characteristics of either domain. This synergy results in more balanced generalization,
with improved robustness to variation and fewer domain-specific misclassifications.
These effects explain why models trained on intermediate domain mixtures yield the
highest AUROC scores, reflecting an effective trade-off between specificity (domain
fit) and generalization (transferability). The progressive decrease in F1-score with


                                             18
increasing target domain proportion indicates a trend that is not yet fully understood.
Presumably, the low quality of the images contributes to this decrease in F1-score;
however, the absolute deviation remains minor.

5.5 Limitations and scope of this study
The present analysis is limited to the PaDiM model trained on a domain-specific
dataset of strawberry images. All conclusions and interpretations are thus model-
and dataset-specific. While they offer valuable insights, they should be regarded as
indicative rather than universally generalizable. Broader conclusions regarding domain
transferability, anomaly sensitivity, or data composition strategies require further
validation across different models and use cases.
    A key challenge lies in the comparability between the source and target domains.
Despite preprocessing efforts, the hydroponic dataset exhibits lower image resolution
than the source data, which reduces the visual detail available for anomaly infer-
ence. Rather than merely being a limitation, this difference represents a realistic and
meaningful domain shift, providing a challenging setting for evaluating the model’s
transferability. The model must adapt to lower-quality input while maintaining detec-
tion performance, which more closely reflects practical conditions in cross-domain
scenarios.

6 Future work
While this study offers foundational insights into domain transition effects in unsuper-
vised AD, it also provides an in-depth investigation of the PaDiM model within the
specific context of detecting ripe strawberries. Together, these perspectives highlight
both general patterns in domain-mixing and model-specific behavior under real-world
agricultural conditions, pointing to several promising directions for future research.

6.1 Other models
The findings presented in this work are based on a single AD framework. To draw
more general and statistically reliable conclusions about unsupervised AD, future work
should involve a systematic evaluation of additional models from Anomalib, including
both classical feature-based methods (e.g., Patch-Core, SPADE), reconstruction-
based methods (Autoencoder or GANs) and recent deep generative approaches
(e.g., DRAEM, FastFlow) [16]. Such comparisons could reveal whether the pat-
terns observed, such as optimal domain-mixing ratios and model sensitivities, are
architecture-dependent or generalizable across different paradigms.

6.2 Anomaly definition and possible technical embedding
The current AD setup defines ripe strawberries as anomalous without distinguishing
between healthy and defective ripe fruit. This binary classification, while effective
for evaluating domain transfer, lacks the granularity needed for practical applications
such as automated quality control. A possible extension would involve integrating
a second-stage AD model or classifier capable of distinguishing between consumable


                                          19
and non-consumable ripe strawberries to detect signs of mold, deformation, disease or
other relevant occurrences.
    The outcomes of this study can also serve as a basis for developing adaptive AD sys-
tems in industrial and agricultural inspection pipelines. The models can be deployed on
edge devices with camera systems, where domain-specific fine-tuning is either imprac-
tical or too resource-intensive. In this context, a generalist model trained on a carefully
selected mixed dataset may offer a more robust and cost-effective solution for AD
under real-world conditions.
    Another possible technical embedding could involve prescreening strawberry
images with a fast and lightweight anomaly detector (such as PaDiM) to detect
whether fruits are likely to be ripe based on visual anomalies. If ripeness is detected,
a more complex object detection or instance segmentation model can then be selec-
tively triggered to analyze growth stages, fruit count, or harvest readiness with higher
precision. This cascade approach offers several advantages: Efficiency, as computation-
ally expensive models are only run when necessary; Scalability, particularly for edge
deployment in smart farming scenarios; and robustness, as initial filtering through AD
can handle variability in lighting, occlusion, or camera positioning.

6.3 Further research fields
Finally, the quantitative and qualitative evaluation frameworks developed in this
study, especially those involving domain composition and visual error analysis, can
be extended to other applications such as manufacturing quality control, medical
imaging, or environmental monitoring. These future directions will help close the gap
between academic AD research and its practical deployment in dynamic, data-diverse
environments.

7 Conclusion
This study combines fundamental insights into domain transition behavior with a
focused analysis of the PaDiM model applied to the task of detecting ripe strawberries
under real-world conditions. This work addresses a particularly relevant challenge:
achieving robust AD performance when only limited or mismatched training data are
available.
    The results show that a balanced mixture of source- and target-domain data
improves model robustness, with the best AUROC observed at intermediate mixing
ratios. Source domain training data proved beneficial under low-light or degraded
image conditions, while target domain data alone led to more conservative, but less
precise, AD. These findings suggest that careful control over training data composition
can compensate for domain gaps and limited annotations.
    Despite working with sparse, unlabeled datasets, the model was able to generalize
to unseen yet semantically normal features, highlighting the potential of unsuper-
vised AD in resource-constrained settings. Qualitatively, the study demonstrated clear
trends in model behavior across different domain compositions. However, the precise
effect size cannot be accurately quantified based on the current results. Consequently,
these conclusions should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive.

   This study thus provides valuable guidance for the development of AD systems in
industrial and agricultural applications, where annotated data are scarce and domain
variability is high. Future work should extend this analysis to other model architectures
and adaptation techniques to validate the generality of these findings.
