ABSTRACT

Accurate crop mapping is critical for agricultural applications. Although studies have combined deep learning methods and time-series satellite images to crop classification with satisfactory results, most of them focused on supervised methods, which are usually applicable to a specific domain and lose their validity in new domains. Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) was proposed to solve this limitation by transferring knowledge from source domains with labeled samples to target domains with unlabeled samples. Particularly, multi-source UDA (MUDA) is a powerful extension that leverages knowledge from multiple source domains and can achieve better results in the target domain than single-source UDA (SUDA). However, few studies have explored the potential of MUDA for crop mapping. This study proposed a MUDA crop classification model (MUCCM) for unsupervised crop mapping. Specifically, 11 states in the U.S. were selected as the multi-source domains, and three provinces in Northeast China were selected as individual target domains. Ten spectral bands and five vegetation indexes were collected at a 10-day interval from time-series Sentinel-2 images to build the MUCCM. Subsequently, a SUDA model Domain Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) and two direct transfer methods, namely, the deep neural network and random forest, were constructed and compared with the MUCCM. The results indicated that the UDA models outperformed the direct transfer models significantly, and the MUCCM was superior to the DANN, achieving the highest classification accuracy (OA>85%) in each target domain. In addition, the MUCCM also performed best in in-season forecasting and crop mapping. This study is the first to apply a MUDA to crop classification and demonstrate a novel, effective solution for high-performance crop mapping in regions without labeled samples.

1. Introduction

The growing population and extreme climate have made food security a hot topic of concern worldwide (Bajželj et al. Citation2014; Hao et al. Citation2020). For example, the global population is expected to increase from 7.8 billion today to 9.7 billion by 2050 (De Wrachien et al., Citation2021), resulting in enormous pressure on food production. Crop mapping can reflect the distribution and area of crops, which helps governments make assessments and formulate policies to avoid food shortages (Cai et al. Citation2018; Lobell Citation2013). Moreover, an accurate crop map is indispensable in crop yield prediction, crop insurance, and land rental (Bolton and Friedl Citation2013; Lobell et al. Citation2015). Therefore, accurate crop mapping is crucial for both scientific and practical applications.

With the increasing availability of satellite remote sensing data, using satellite data to identify crops has become a mainstream technique for crop classification (Wang et al. Citation2021; Hao et al. Citation2015; Peña and Brenning Citation2015). Compared with a single remote sensing image, time-series remote sensing images has the advantage of capturing the dynamic spectral features of crops, leading to increased accuracy in classification. Thus, using time-series images during the crop growth period for crop mapping has been an important approach (Löw et al. Citation2013; Skakun et al. Citation2017; Vuolo et al. Citation2018; Zhong, Lina, and Zhou Citation2019). Notably, machine learning methods have been combined with time-series satellite images to improve the accuracy of crop classification. (Hao et al. Citation2015; Kumar et al. Citation2015; Zheng et al. Citation2015). For example, Feng et al. (Citation2019) compared machine learning methods and statistical-based methods to distinguish rice, corn, and soybean using time-series images, the results showed that the machine learning methods were up to 10% more accurate than the statistical-based methods. Recently, deep learning methods have been widely used in crop mapping (Meng et al. Citation2021; Turkoglu et al. Citation2021; Xu et al. Citation2021; Zhong, Lina, and Zhou Citation2019; Zhou et al. Citation2019). As an advanced extension of machine learning, deep learning methods have deeper networks than the former that can automatically learn information features with multiple levels, achieving a better performance (Lecun, Bengio, and Hinton Citation2015; Schmidhuber Citation2015). For instance, Zhong, Lina, and Zhou (Citation2019) compared two widely used deep learning models and three non-deep machine learning classifiers in identifying crops using the time-series of Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI). The result indicated that the deep learning models outperformed the non-deep machine learning classifiers significantly.

However, most of the existing deep learning-based crop classification models are supervised models, which rely on many ground truth samples in the study area. A viable solution to reduce the dependence on sample labels is transfer learning, which can use the knowledge from a source dataset to improve the prediction in the target dataset (Pan and Yang Citation2009). Specifically, direct transfer is a widely used strategy. For example, studies have attempted to directly transfer crop classification models trained in sample-rich regions to sample-poor regions (Ge et al. Citation2021; Hao et al. Citation2020). However, direct transfer models may lose their validity when applied to different areas owing to domain shift (Ge et al. Citation2021). To improve the model, researchers have applied the fine-tuning technique. Fine-tuning uses a few samples from the target domain to retrain all or part of the structure of the pre-trained models from the source domain. For example, Chew et al. (Citation2020) pre-trained the VGG16 model by using the ImageNet dataset, and then the pre-trained model was retrained with crop samples for crop classification. Similarly, Suh et al. (Citation2018) fine-tuned six pre-trained models and obtained a good performance in classifying sugar beet and volunteer potato.

Despite successes, fine-tuning still requires labeled samples from the target domain. Because sample collection is labor-intensive and time-consuming, some agricultural areas, especially in developing and undeveloped counties, might not have ground-truth data for fine-tuning. Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) was proposed to overcome this problem because it can transfer knowledge learned from source domains with labeled samples to target domains with unlabeled data (Ganin et al. Citation2016). The core of UDA is aligning feature distribution between the source domain and the target domain to achieve knowledge transfer. Most UDA models focus on single-source UDA (SUDA) (Sun and Saenko Citation2016), namely, learning and transferring knowledge from a single source domain to a target domain. The Domain Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) is a widely used SUDA model in speech and image recognition tasks. For example, Wang et al. (Citation2018) applied the DANN in speaking recognition and alleviated the domain shift between two datasets. Han et al. (Citation2019) proposed a DANN-based model to identify mechanical faults and achieved good generalization capability in conditions that did not occur during training.

In practice, there are probably multiple source domains that may contain different useful knowledge for the target domain. Therefore, multi-source UDA (MUDA) is proposed to leverage all available data. The typical method to achieve MUDA is treating all source domains as one domain and then aligning data distribution as a SUDA does. Nevertheless, the domain shift is difficult to remove even for one source and one target domain, and mismatch might increase when aligning the multi-source and target domains, resulting in unsatisfactory performance in the target domain (Ganin et al. Citation2016). Consequently, novel MUDA approaches have been proposed and can be categorized into two groups (Sun, Shi, and Yuanbin Citation2015): feature representation (Chattopadhyay et al. Citation2012; Guo, Shah, and Barzilay Citation2018) and a combination of pre-trained classifiers (Guo, Pasunuru, and Bansal Citation2020; Peng et al. Citation2019). The first group designs a feature space projection in which the distribution divergence between the multi-source domains and the target domain is minimized. The second group trains classifiers in the source domains and combines the pre-trained classifiers to obtain a final classifier in the target domain. Notably, the Multiple Feature Spaces Adaptation Network (MFSAN) combines the advantages of both groups and shows valuable capabilities in many image classification tasks (Zhu, Zhuang, and Wang Citation2019). Specifically, the MFSAN first aligns the distributions of each pair of the source and target domain to learn the domain-invariant representations. Next, the MFSAN aligns pre-trained classifiers to minimize the discrepancy among all classifiers. The combination of the two alignments has been proven to guarantee remarkable performance in the target domain.

To the best of our knowledge, few studies have applied UDA approaches in crop mapping, especially MUDA approaches. Due to differences in climate conditions, precipitation and soil types in multiple domains, the phenology of the same crop differs among the domains (Wang et al. Citation2021), which intensifies the domain shift between the source domain and target domain. Full use of the knowledge from multi-source domains is necessary to achieve accurate crop identification in the target domain. This study proposed a MUDA crop classification model (MUCCM) based on MFSAN for unsupervised crop mapping using time-series Sentinel-2 images. Specifically, we attempted to answer two research questions: 1) Is the crop classification accuracy of the MUCCM better than that of the widely used machine learning methods and SUDA models? and 2) how does the MUCCM perform in in-season forecasting and crop mapping?

2. Study areas and dataset

2.1. Study areas

The U.S. and China are the largest agricultural producers worldwide and have a substantial impact on global food security (Veeck, Veeck, and Hongyan Citation2020). Among the various crops, corn and soybean are widely planted in the two countries. The spatial distribution and area information for these two crops are publicly released and updated annually in the U.S. but not in China. Therefore, this study focused on corn and soybeans, with 11 states in the U.S. as source domains and three provinces in China as target domains.

Target domains: Three provinces in Northeast China were selected as individual target domains: Liaoning (LN), Jilin (JL) and Heilongjiang (HLJ) (). The three provinces produce more than 30% and 45% of China’s annual corn and soybean production, respectively (MOA Citation2022). As shown in , the phenology dates of the crops in the target domains are more focused than those in the source domains. For example, the harvesting date for soybean in the target domains is concentrated in October, which is within the range of the harvesting date for soybean in the source domains.

Notably, the 11 states produce approximately 70% of the annual corn and soybean in the U.S. (USDA Citation2020). In addition, the planting and harvesting dates of the two crops vary across states. For example, the planting dates of corn in the 11 states ranging from mid-April to mid-Jun (), providing an opportunity to assess the effect of multi-source domains.

2.2. Sentinel-2 imagery

Sentinel-2 comprises two satellites, Sentinel-2 A and Sentinel-2 B, which provide high spatial resolution (up to 10 m) and temporal resolution (up to 5 days) images freely. Because studies have proven the reliability of top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance on classification tasks (d’Andrimont et al. Citation2020; Ge et al. Citation2021; Song et al. Citation2001) and there is few available Sentinel-2 Level-2A data in the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform, this study collected the TOA reflectance images covering the study area in 2018 to obtain the spectral features of crops. Specifically, 10 bands including Red, Blue, Green, Red-edge1, Red-edge2, Red-edge3, Near-Infrared (NIR), Red-edge4, Short wave infrared (SWIR) 1 and SWIR2 were selected as spectral features. We also selected five widely used vegetation indexes (VIs) calculated from the Sentinel-2 image: the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) (Tucker Citation1979), Green Vegetation Index (VIgreen) (Gitelson et al. Citation2002), Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) (Huete et al. Citation2002), Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) (Gao Citation1996), and Normalized Difference Residue Index (NDRI) (Gelder, Kaleita, and Cruse Citation2009). In addition, the cloud mask of the images was conducted refer to Oreopoulos, Wilson, and Tamás (Citation2011).

2.3. Ground reference

The ground reference data of the source domains were collected from the Cropland Data Layer (CDL), an annual land cover map with 30 m spatial resolution depicting the distribution of more than 100 crops across the U.S. (Boryan et al. Citation2011). For the samples of target domains, no available ground reference data has been officially released. Fortunately, You et al. (Citation2021) published 10 m spatial resolution maps of corn and soybean in Northeast China from 2017 to 2019. Thus, we regarded the 2018 crop maps from CDL and You as the reference crop maps in this study.

Because this study focused on soybean and corn, the other types were labeled as “other” (e.g. grass, water, buildings, and other crops). To obtain balanced samples (Pott et al. Citation2021), we randomly selected 3000 samples of corn, soybean, and other type in each domain, for a total of 126,000 labeled samples. For each sample, 10 spectral bands and five VIs were extracted at a 10-day interval from DOY 90 to DOY 300, resulting in 315 features. Specifically, referring to You et al. (Citation2021), five steps were implemented to generate the dataset: 1) all available images covering the whole study area in 2021 were collected within a 10-day time window; 2) the clouds of the images were masked by the adjusted cloud score algorithm; 3) 10-day composites were generated with the median values of the valid observations; 4) data gaps were filled by the linear interpolation to achieve full coverage throughout the temporal domain; 5) 10-day time series data were smoothed by using the Savitzky-Golay (SG) filter. To maintain the same spatial resolution, we resampled all images to 10 m. The image collection, preprocessing, and information extraction were all processed on the GEE platform (Gorelick et al. Citation2017).

3. Methodology

3.1. Multiple feature spaces adaptation network

Multiple Feature Spaces Adaptation Network (MFSAN) is an advanced MUDA model proposed by Zhu (Zhu, Zhuang, and Wang Citation2019). Unlike other models, the core idea of this model is to extract domain-invariant representations for each pair of source and target domains separately, instead of extracting common domain-invariant representations for all domains, which makes the model perform satisfactorily when there are significant differences among multiple source domain data.

As shown in , the MFSAN has a common feature extractor (CFE), several domain-specific feature extractors (DSFE), and several domain-specific classifiers (DSC). DSFEs are built to map the feature maps of each pair of target data and source data to obtain the domain-invariant representations. 3.2. MUDA based crop classification model

Although the original MFSAN has been successfully applied in RGB image classification tasks, it cannot be directly implemented for crop classification in this study. Specifically, the MFSAN is designed for handling RGB image patches with 256 rows and 256 columns, and our dataset is pixel-based with 315 features. Thus, we modified the shape of our dataset and the architecture of the MFSAN model to build a new MUDA based crop classification model (MUCCM).

First, the 315 features were transformed into a matrix with 15 rows (spectral dimension) and 21 columns (time dimension) to satisfy the input requirements of convolution operations in the MUCCM model. Second, the first seven ResBlocks of ResNet-50 (He et al. Citation2016) were selected as the CFE in the MUCCM. ResNet-50 is a widely used model in classification tasks and can extract useful high-level features from complex inputs. The MFSAN takes the whole ResNet-50 as the CFE, which is complex and will reduce the input to one-eighth of the original. Thus, it needs to be simplified to process our dataset. Finally, we designed 11 DSFEs and 11 DSCs in the MUCCM according to the number of the source domains in this study. Specifically, the DSFE consists of three convolution layers, each of which is connected to a batch normalization (BatchNorm) layer and an activation layer at the end. The BatchNorm layer was adopted to solve internal covariate shift and overfitting (Ma et al. Citation2021), and the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) was used as the activation layer. For the DSC, we used a Dense layer to predict crop types. shows the detailed architecture of the MUCCM.

3.4. Experimental setup

The 11 states in the U.S. were treated as multi-source domains, and each of the three provinces in Northeast China was selected as an individual target domain. Samples from the multi-source domains were split into two parts randomly: 70% for training the MUCCM model and 30% for validation. Samples from each target domain were selected as the testing dataset to evaluate the MUCCM. In the training process, the number of epochs and the batch size were set to 150 and 512, respectively. We adapted the parameters of the MUCCM model according to the result of the validation. Finally, Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) was set to the optimizer, and the learning rates of the CFE, DSFE, and DSC were initiated as 0.001, 0.01, and 0.01, respectively. The hyperparameters and of the MUCCM were initialized to 1.

To demonstrate the advantages of the MUDA model in crop mapping, we compared the model with three other models. The first model was DANN, one of the most widely used SUDA methods. For an accurate comparison, the DANN was designed to have a similar architecture to that of the MUCCM. The difference between the two models was that the DANN had merely one DSFE and one DSC. To train the DANN, data from 11 states in the U.S. were combined as one single source, and the other training settings were identical to those of the MUCCM. To compare the domain adaptation models, we designed two direct transfer learning models: including a deep neural network (DNN) model and a random forest (RF) model. The structure of the DNN was almost identical to that of the DANN, except that the DNN had no feature alignment. The critical parameters of RF were the number of trees and the maximum depth of each tree, and they were set to 400 and 20, respectively. Specifically, the deep learning models were constructed by PyTorch 1.5 in Python version 3.7, and the RF model was built by scikit-learn, a Python module for machine learning.

The MFSAN and the comparison models were evaluated based on three aspects: classification accuracy, in-season forecasting capability and mapping performance. Specifically, the overall accuracy, macro-average of F1 score (F1) and confusion matrix were adopted as accuracy metrics. For evaluating the in-season forecasting capability of the models, a fixed starting date was set to DOY 90 (planting date), and the ending date was varied from DOY 150 (early growing season) to DOY 300 (harvesting date). We used full time-series features to train each model and then tested the model using time-series features of different periods (e.g. from DOY 90 to DOY 210). Notably, the reduced time-series features were padded by 0 to preserve the original input size. This experiment allowed us to assess not only the in-season forecasting capability of the model but also the robustness under different data deficiencies. The mapping results are the best validation of crop classification models. Because the area of the three target domains is too large, we selected one county in each target domain to evaluate the models: Keshan County in HLJ, Yushu County in JL, and Pulandian County in LN. We also selected a small area in each county to verify the details of the maps generated by the models. The locations of the selected counties and areas are shown in .

4. Results and analysis

4.1. Spectral characteristics of crops

The NDVI time-series profiles for corn, soybean, and the other type in the 11 source domains and three target domains are shown in . The NDVI profiles varied by crop and domain. Notably, the other type displayed significantly different profiles from the two crops in each domain. The reason for this finding was that this category contained many land types except corn and soybean, such as grasses, forests, and buildings, resulting in a large variance in the spectrum. The NDVI profiles for corn and soybean were similar, but slight differences in their peak magnitude and peak date were observed. For example, the mean value of the peak NDVI for corn in KS and LN was below 0.6, while most corn in SD and HLJ had a peak NDVI above 0.7. The NDVI peak dates for soybean in ND and JL were very close to each other, concentrated at 220 DOY, while the NDVI peak date in the target domain LN was more than 20 days later. However, different spectral characteristics of the same crop can confuse the crop classification models and lead to poor accuracy. Notably, a model trained by the data from a single source domain that differs too much from the target domain may have low migration performance. Therefore, using data from multiple source domains to develop a high-quality unsupervised transfer learning model is necessary.

4.3. In-season forecasting capability

Early-season crop mapping is vital for agricultural applications. Thus, we also evaluated the model performance within the growing season. Specifically, the models were trained using full time-series features and tested by the features between the starting date (DOY90) and different ending dates (DOY150 – DOY300). As shown in , the three deep learning models exhibited satisfactory performance with more time-series features until the end of September, and then their accuracies stabilized. The MUCCM outperformed the other two deep learning models and achieved good performance (OA >85%) before October. Although the final accuracy of the RF model is not as good as that of the MUCCM and DANN, it achieved good accuracy (OA>75%) earlier than those of the two models. In addition, the performance of RF is stable. For example, the accuracy curves of the deep learning models fluctuated substantially and did not achieve accurate classification in the early growing season in JL, while the accuracy of the RF model increased steadily over time. A possible reason for this finding is that deep learning models are sensitive to inputs, and data distributed differently from the training pattern can lead to model instability.

Figure 9. Performance against timelines of the models trained using full time-series features in (a) HLJ, (b) JL, and (c) LN.

We also retrained and tested the MUCCM and RF in each target domain using the time-series features between the starting date and different ending dates. As shown in , the retrained MUCCM model improved steadily over time, proving that retraining is necessary for deep learning models. The accuracy of the retrained MUCCM converged at approximately DOY210, namely, three months before harvest. In addition, the accuracy of the MUCCM was better than that of the RF at almost every time point, indicating that the MUCCM had advantages in early-season crop classification in target domains.

4.4. Crop mapping performance

The mapping results are the best validation of crop classification models and shows the reference crop maps and the predicted crop maps generated by the models (trained using full time-series features) in the three selected counties. In Keshan County, the MUCCM and DANN generated results similar to the reference maps. By contrast, the DNN model misclassified many corn areas and the RF model identified the other type as soybean in northern Keshan County. However, all four crop classification models did not generate accurate crop maps in Yushu and Pulandian. Although the MUCCM outperformed the other models, the model-generated crop maps misclassified many soybean fields as corn fields. The reason why the crop maps in Yushu and Pulandian seem inaccurate may be twofold: the model does have the limitation in mixing corn and soybeans, and this limitation is not spatially consistent, and the crops in Yushu and Pulandian are almost all corn, making visualizing which areas are misclassified as soybeans easy.

5. Discussion

5.1. Model interpretation

This study conducted two experiments to discover the performance of the models against timelines in target domains. The first one aimed to test if models trained by full time-series features can keep good performance with reduced time-series features. indicates that the accuracy of each model did not increase consistently with the increasing number of time series features. Compared to the RF model, the accuracy curves of the deep learning models were more fluctuating. This could be due to the more complex structures of the deep learning models. A deep learning model consists of multiple layers, each of which consists of numerous neurons. Changing the model input may have an unpredictable effect on the model output. Therefore, the gradual increase of time-series features cannot produce a smooth increase in the accuracy of the deep learning models. The second experiment used time-series features of different lengths to retrain and test the models, and the results () indicated that the models improved steadily over time until the later stage in the season, and the deep learning model significantly outperformed the RF model for each feature combination. These two experiments demonstrated the importance of data pattern alignment between the training phase and the testing phase, and our model can keep the best performance in each alignment situation.

To explore how models classify crops, we applied the t-distributed Stochastic Embedding (t-SNE) algorithm (Van der Maaten and Hinton Citation2008) to visualize the original features of corn and soybean and the extracted features from the crop classification models. Because there were no extracted features from RF, we only applied the algorithm for the MUCCM, DANN, and DNN. The visualization results () indicated that the distributions of the original features of the two crops were disordered and chaotic. After processing through the models, the two distributions of the extracted features exhibited varying degrees of separateness. Compared with the domain adaptive models, the DNN generated two distributions with weak separability, which explained its low classification accuracy. The two distributions from DANN differ, but each category contained a substantial number of samples from the other. Moreover, the two distributions formed three aggregates, increasing the difficulty of crop identification. The distributions of extracted features from the MUCCM formed two distinct aggregates, with each representing one crop and containing fewer wrong samples than those of DANN. However, although there were errors in the two distributions of the DANN both were more aggregated than those of the MUCCM. Two reasons may explain this finding. First, the DSFEs of MUCCM had only one-eleventh of the training data and epoch of DANN, and the inadequate training conditions can make the distribution difficult to aggregate. Second, the result of the MUCCM was obtained by averaging the results of 11 extractors, which may also increase the sparseness of the distribution.

5.3. Limitations and future work

This study illustrated that the MUCCM model used the knowledge of the multi-source domains to achieve accurate unsupervised crop mapping in the target domain. However, there are several limitations. First, this study focused on soybean and corn, and the other crop types are combined into one “other” class. However, to improve transfer accuracy, the crop species in the source domain and target domain should be similar, and each crop should be sampled for modeling. The purpose of this study is to verify the advance of the proposed model, and the results indicated that the proposed model has a more generalized transfer performance than other models under the same experimental environment. In practical applications, to make the transfer results more accurate, we should ensure that the crop species of the source and target domains are consistent, and divide the “other” type into specific crops so that the accuracy will be higher. Second, the resulting maps generated by models were evaluated based on the 2018 existing crop map. Because the existing map is not 100% correct, the accuracy of the resulting maps can only be judged from the general trend, and determining whether a mismatch is an error in the resulting maps or the existing map is not possible. Third, this study only considered two main crops in the U.S. and China, while there are various crops in the two counties. In future work, we will explore the transfer performance of the proposed model in heterogeneous regions with multiple crops. Although the proposed model has a flexible structure that can easily be adapted to multiple crops, the model’s performance may degrade because crop mapping in heterogeneous regions is very challenging (Zhang et al. Citation2021; Chen et al. Citation2016). Thus, we will optimize the model by 1) adding new features, such as backscattering coefficients from Sentinel-1 image, to enhance the separability among multiple crops. 2) selecting time-series features based on crop phenology to improve the alignment between the source and target domains. 3) embedding attention modules into the model to classify crops more effectively. Finally, the study areas have close latitudes and seasons, providing the possibility for a good transfer. However, whether the present method can maintain good transfer performance in two regions with large seasonal differences requires further research. In light of the previous study (Zhao et al. Citation2020), a potential idea to improve the transfer between two distinct regions is to use prior phenological knowledge to extract the spectral features of crops at key phenological stages (e.g. sowing and flowing stages) in the two regions separately and then use these features as the input to the transfer model. This method can avoid the feature shift caused by seasons and increase the transfer performance.

6. Conclusion

This study proposed the MUCCM to explore the potential of MUDA in crop mapping. Specifically, 11 states in the U.S. were selected as the multi-source domains, and three provinces in Northeast China were selected as individual target domains. Ten spectral bands and five vegetation indexes were collected from time-series satellite imagery. To evaluate the MUCCM model, we compared it with the SUDA model, DANN, and two direct transfer models: DNN and RF. The results showed that the MUCCM model achieved good performance in each target domain, especially for in-season forecasting and actual mapping. Furthermore, we employed t-SNE to interpret the models and found that the corn and soybean features extracted by the MUCCM had the best separation. Finally, we compared the multi-source models and single-source models, and the results indicated that the performances of the single-source models were very unstable, and that the MUCCM can exhibit good performance by using multi-source data. This study demonstrated the great potential of applying the MUDA model to unsupervised crop mapping.
