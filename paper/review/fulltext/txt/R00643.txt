40       Summary
41       Hyperspectral reflectance provides rapid and precise phenotyping of plants in a non-destructive
42       manner both in field and well-controlled settings. The resulting data have been used to devise
43       machine learning (ML) models for paired measurements of different traits in diverse plants and
44       crops. Yet, despite advances in using of hyperspectral data to reliably predict crop traits of
45       interest, there are pressing issues concerning the training of ML models, the aggregation of data
46       from crop field trials, and the generalizability of the models in different prediction settings. We
47       collected hyperspectral reflectance data along with 25 anatomical, gas exchange, and chlorophyll
48       fluorescence traits from 320 recombinant inbred lines of a maize Multi-Parent Advanced
49       Generation Inter-Cross population grown across three consecutive seasons. We use these data to
50       systematically: (1) compare the performance of representative ML models for different traits,
51       including slow fluorescence kinetics whose predictability by hyperspectral data has not yet been
52       investigated, (2) evaluate the ML model performance in prediction scenarios concerning unseen
53       genotypes, unseen seasons, and the combination thereof, (3) investigate the effects of data
54       aggregation of ML model performance. These problems are addressed in a rigorous nested cross-
55       validation setting that provides a template for adequate assessment of performance of ML
56       models for diverse crop traits considering the particularities of the experimental design.

78              1. Introduction
79                   Efforts to develop climate-resilient crops go hand-in-hand with advances in high-
80        throughput phenotyping technologies that can be effectively deployed in accelerated breeding
81        (Kole et al., 2015; Langridge et al., 2021). Urgent innovations in high-throughput profiling are
82        needed given that many breeding targets include molecular and biochemical traits that are
83        challenging to measure in large breeding populations (Alseekh et al., 2021). Hyperspectral
84        reflectance is one technology that provides rapid and precise phenotyping of plants in a non-
85        destructive manner, allowing its deployment both in field and well-controlled settings
86        (Grzybowski et al., 2021). Hyperspectral reflectance captures the intensity of light reflected from
87        a plant across many specific wavelengths. The hyperspectral reflectance data (HSR) then consist
88        of aggregated reflectance intensities at different light wavelengths. HSR data have been used to
89        devise machine learning (ML) models for paired measurements of various molecular,
90        biochemical, and physiological traits in diverse plants and crops (Kumar et al., 2001; Serbin et
91        al., 2012; Yendrek et al., 2017; Silva-Perez et al., 2018; Cotrozzi et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2020;
92        Furbank et al., 2021; Heckmann et al., 2017; Rehman et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021; Yu et al.,
93        2022; Kaur et al., 2024). Despite the wide application of HSR-based ML models, key
94        methodological questions remain underexplored—particularly regarding model calibration, data
95        aggregation strategies, relevant for breeding-related decisions, as well as generalizability across
96        genotypes and growing environments.
97                   With few exceptions, involving deep learning models (Rehman et al., 2020; Yu et al.,
98        2022), most ML studies employ Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) as the standard model
99        for trait prediction using HSR data as predictive features. A key hyperparameter in PLSR is the
100       number of components (NoC), that must be carefully selected to avoid overfitting while ensuring
101       model accuracy. The value for NoC is typically identified using a calibration data set. This
102       calibration data set must not overlap with the validation dataset, used to assess model
103       performance, to prevent data leakage. However, the strategy used for selecting NoC and model
104       evaluation varies significantly across studies.
105                  Two main approaches are commonly used to train and evaluate PLSR models–proposed
106       by Burnett et al. (2021) and Filzmoser et al. (2009). The first approach, used, for instance, in
107       Serbin et al. (2012, 2014), Meerdink et al. (2016), Ely et al. (2019) and Wang et al. (2021),


                                                                             4
      bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.03.661070; this version posted July 6, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
         was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                    available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




108       involves random partitioning of the full dataset into a calibration set (typically 80%) and a
109       validation set (20%). Within the calibration set, 70% of the samples are randomly selected to
110       train the model using varying values of NoC, while the remaining 30% of the calibration set are
111       used to assess model performance of each NoC. This procedure is typically repeated 1000 times
112       to ensure robust estimation. The optimal NoC value is chosen by minimizing the predicted
113       residual error sum of squares (PRESS) statistics (Chen et al., 2004) collected from the repeated
114       sub-sampling. The second approach, known as repeated double cross-validation, employs two
115       nested cross-validations, and has been utilized in studies such as Dechant et al. (2017), Yan et al.
116       (2021) and Ji et al. (2024).
117                  In this case, an outer loop repeatedly partitions the dataset into calibration and validation
118       folds (e.g., 5-fold CV repeated 100 times). An inner loop performs k-fold CV within each
119       calibration set to identify the optimal NoC using the standard error method (Tibshirani &
120       Friedman, 2001). This method prevents the selection of excessively large NoC that might reduce
121       the mean squared error (MSE), while increasing the risk of overfitting.
122                  The assessment of the generalizability of the resulting models to unseen samples has been
123       approached in different ways. For instance, Burnett et al. (2021) assessed the uncertainty of
124       PLSR models using jackknife (leave-on-out) of the calibration data set, given that a single final
125       validation set was available. In contrast, Filzmoser et al. (2009) proposed repeated cross-
126       validation sets, allowing thorough assessment of both prediction accuracy and performance
127       variability. It is then conceivable that these differences can have impact on the findings and their
128       comparability between different studies.
129                  Here, we collected HSR data and paired measurements of 25 traits from 320 recombinant
130       inbred lines (RILs) of a maize Multi-Parent Advanced Generation Inter-Cross (MAGIC)
131       population grown across three consecutive seasons. Using this dataset, we addressed the
132       following key questions:
133       1. How can one select the optimal NoC in PLSR in an accurate and computationally efficient
134             way? To address this question, we systematically compared different inner-loop structures
135             and selection criteria using repeated cross-validation to assess their impact on model
136             performance and uncertainty.
137       2. How do ML models perform under increasingly challenging scenarios of generalization? To
138             this end, we evaluated three levels of model generalization: (i) random splits of training and
                                                                             5
      bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.03.661070; this version posted July 6, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
         was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                    available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




139             validation data; (ii) prediction of unseen genotypes within the same season; and (iii)
140             prediction of traits from unseen seasons (Figure 1a). We note that these scenarios of model
141             generalizability are particularly relevant for making decisions based on the ML models in
142             breeding.
143       3. How do data aggregation and season influence model performance? In this respect, we
144             investigated the impact of averaging raw trait and reflectance measurements at the plot or
145             genotype level. In addition, we investigated how aggregation affects the model generalization
146             scenarios, mentioned above. To this end, we made use of the three replicates of the genotypes
147             per plot, to compare models that are based on HSR and traits data aggregated by averaging
148             over plots or averaging over genotypes.
149

150                  By addressing these questions, our study provides a robust experimental and analytical
151       framework for assessing model performance on HSR data from field trials, with direct
152       implications for predictive phenotyping across genotypes, traits, and environments.

154             2. Materials and methods
155             2.1 Leaf physiological traits and reflectance measurements
156                  Field trials incorporating the maize MAGIC population (Dell’Acqua et al., 2015) were
157       performed in 2021, 2022, and 2023 at the National Institute of Agricultural Botany (NIAB,
158       Cambridge, UK). In each year, 320 recombinant inbred lines (RILs) were grown in a twice-
159       replicated alpha lattice design. The experimental design has previously been described in full
160       detail (Ferguson et al., 2025). Some RILs were specific to certain years, but 305 RILs were
161       grown and phenotyped in all three years.
162                  All phenotyping was performed on the leaf subtending the ear, which was excised at
163       dawn and returned to the laboratory for phenotyping, as described previously (Ferguson et al.,
164       2025). We have previously shown that this approach generated data comparable to measuring
165       leaves still intact to the plant (Ferguson et al., 2023).
166                  Initially, light-saturated photosynthesis (A) was determined on all leaves using LI-6400
167       infra-red gas analyzers equipped with 6400-40 leaf chamber fluorometer LED light sources (LI-
168       COR Inc., Lincoln, NE). Conditions within the leaf chambers were as follows: 1800 µmol m-2 s-
169       1 photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), 25°C block temperature, 400 µmol s-1 air flow, 65%
170       relative humidity (RH), 400 µmol mol-1 reference CO2 concentration. A randomly selected
171       subset of RILs (78 in 2021, 88 in 2022, 91 in 2023) were used for measuring the response of
172       photosynthesis to changes in the intracellular concentration of CO2 (A-Ci curve) using LI-6800
173       infra-red gas analysers equipped with standard 6cm2 leaf chambers (LI-COR Inc., Lincoln,
174       Nebraska). A-Ci response curves were collected exactly as described previously (Ferguson et al.,
175       2024). Using the A-Ci data, estimates of the maximum rate of carboxylation by
176       phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC; Vpmax) and the asymptote of the A-Ci curve (Vmax)
177       were modeled following von Caemmerer (Von Caemmerer, 2000).
178                  Leaf hyperspectral reflectance was measured on the same portion of the leaf where gas
179       exchange was performed using an ASD FieldSpec 4 Standard-Res Spectroradiometer equipped
180       with a leaf-clip (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). The light source of the FieldSpec was
181       allowed to warm up for 45 minutes prior to measurements. For each leaf, three technical
182       replicates were performed and the reflectance at each wavelength was averaged before
183       subsequent data analyses.

193             2.2       Data processing
194                   For each genotype, measurements of different traits were collected from two plots, with
195       three replicates each. To assess differences in model performance, raw data were averaged by
196       plot or genotype. Wavelengths from 400 nm to 2400 nm were used in training models to predict
197       each of the traits, except for nitrogen percentage and ratio of nitrogen to carbon percentage. For
198       these traits, wavelengths from 1500 nm to 2400 nm were used, as in Yendrek et al. (2017). To
199       account for the variability of trait response across seasons, particularly for SLA, before
200       combining different seasons as training data, the trait values from each year were centered and
201       scaled.
202

203             2.3       Machine learning models
204                   Supervised machine learning (ML) was used to develop models to predict measured traits
205       based on HSR data. Once trained, these models can predict traits for which only reflectance data
206       are available. Given that the traits are continuous variables, regression-based ML models were
207       used. The predictions were performed by using ‘pls’ (Mevik & Wehrens, 2023) and ‘caret’
208       package (Kuhn & Max, 2008), implemented in R software environment.
209                   Partial least-squares regression (PLSR) is a state-of-art ML model commonly used in
210       modeling plant traits based on HSR data as predictors (Geladi & Kowalski, 1986; Wold et al.,
211       2001). HSR typically contain more predictors (i.e., reflectance at different wavelengths) than
212       samples, and these predictors are often highly correlated, leading to a multicolinearity problem.
213       PLSR addresses these challenges by projecting the response variable onto uncorrelated

                                                                             8
      bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.03.661070; this version posted July 6, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
         was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                    available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




214       components, which are linear combinations of predictors. This approach allows the response to
215       be expressed in terms of the original predictors by considering the response during component
216       extraction. The number of components is a crucial hyperparameter in PLSR and is typically
217       tuned using different procedures, as described above. PLSR has been successfully used to predict
218       traits such as Vmax, Jmax and %N across different species (Yendrek et al., 2017; Meacham-
219       Hensold et al., 2019).
220                   For fairness of comparison to the widely used PLSR models based on HSR data, we
221       considered linear kernel SVR to avoid nonlinearity of other kernels as explanation for
222       differences in performance. As a result, the SVR also has one hyperparameter, C, referred to as
223       the regularization hyperparameter. In this study, we implemented a nested CV workflow to tune
224       the hyperparameter and validate the model using unseen data under different scenarios.
225

226             2.4       Different prediction scenarios
227                   This study evaluated the utility of HSR data for predicting physiological traits under three
228       distinct prediction scenarios, each designed to assess model generalizability under varying levels
229       of data partitioning. Model performance was assessed using the outer loop of a repeated CV
230       framework, while hyperparameter tuning was conducted within the inner loop, as illustrated in
231       Figure 1a. The scenarios are described in the following:
232                   Scenario i: Random sample prediction. In this scenario, model performance was
233       evaluated using randomly partitioned data sets. This approach enables systematic comparison of
234       model accuracy across different data aggregation strategies and seasons. The outer loop consists
235       of 20 repetitions of random 5-fold CV, allowing the collection of mean performance from five
236       folds across repetitions. Hyperparameter optimization within the inner loop was conducted using
237       either repeated CV, permutation-based, or single CV approaches.
238                   Scenario ii: Unseen genotype prediction. A 5-fold CV scheme was repeated 20 times,
239       ensuring genotype exclusivity between folds in both outer and inner loops to avoid data leakage.
240       This set-up allowed for the assessment of the model’s performance across genotypes.
241                   Scenario iii: Unseen season prediction. In this scenario, the goal was to predict traits for a
242       new, unseen season using data from other seasons in model training. Here, entire data sets from
243       one or two seasons were used for model calibration, and model performance was evaluated on

                                                                             9
      bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.03.661070; this version posted July 6, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
         was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                    available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




244       data from the held-out season. Within the calibration set, the inner loop employed random splits
245       for hyperparameter tuning. Additionally, a more stringent variation of this scenario was
246       implemented, in which the model was trained on data from a single season and was then applied
247       to unseen genotypes in an unseen season. This was achieved by withholding 1/5 of the data of
248       the genotypes from the training season and evaluating their predictions in a separate season in
249       the framework of 20 repetitions of 5-fold CV. Here, too, genotype exclusion was strictly
250       enforced during inner loop splitting to avoid data leakage.
251                  Model performance was quantified by using coefficient of determination (R2) for
252       scenarios i and ii and squared Pearson correlation (r2) for scenario iii. We used squared Pearson
253       correlation (r2) to assess the trend of predicted values rather than their agreement on measured
254       values in the most challenging scenario iii.

256             3. Results
257             3.1 Phenotyping a maize MAGIC population gathers large-scale data
258                    suitable for testing the generalizability of ML models
259                  To address our questions, we gathered leaf HSR data obtained from a maize MAGIC
260       population grown in three consecutive field seasons (i.e., 2021, 2022 and 2023) (Figure 1b,
261       Methods). The average HSR profiles from three seasons over all samples were consistent across
262       the measured wavelength region of 700 to 3500 nm. In the visible region (350–700 nm), both the
263       minimum and maximum HSR values over all samples were similar across the seasons. However,
264       by calculating the correlation of averaged HSR per genotype, the mean correlation based on the
265       data from this region ranged from 0.27 to 0.36 between three seasons (Table S1). In the near-
266       infrared region (700–1300 nm), where HSR reaches the high plateau, the minimum values
267       measured in 2022 were significantly higher than those measured in 2021 and 2023, while the
268       maximum values in 2021 were lower than those in 2022 and 2023 (Figure 1b). The average
269       correlation for this region across genotypes between seasons 2022 and 2023 was 0.5, which was
270       lower than the correlation of 0.57 observed between the 2021 and 2022 seasons and the 0.53
271       observed between the 2021 and 2023 season. The shortwave infrared region (1300–2500 nm)
272       also showed differences in the range of HSR values across the three seasons, with averaged
273       correlation of 0.50, 0.44 and 0.49 for the pairs of three seasons (Table S1). Moreover, Mantel
274       tests were performed to assess if the covariance matrix of HSR data (across genotypes) in one
275       season were correlated with the covariance matrix from another season. We found that seasons
276       2021 and 2022 showed a correlation of 0.89, seasons 2021 and 2023 had a correlation of 0.88
277       and seasons 2022 and 2023 showed a correlation of 0.93. These values indicated differences in
278       the covariance structure of the HSR data that may affect transferability of the models,
279       particularly involving training / testing with data from season 2021 that differs the most from
280       data from 2022 and 2023.
281                  In addition, 25 traits were measured or estimated during these seasons, with their
282       distribution depicted in Figures S1, S2 and S3. These traits were grouped into three categories,
283       with different number of samples in each category (Table S2). We measured six anatomical traits,
284       including: specific leaf area, relative C content (%C), relative N content (%N), carbon to
285       nitrogen ratio (C:N) as well as the stable carbon and nitrogen isotope ratios ( δ13C and δ15N).

476             3.4 Evaluating model generalizability to unseen genotypes and seasons
477                  The analysis above utilized random split between calibration and validation sets, enabling
478       a systematic evaluation of how different data aggregation strategies and seasonal variation affect
479       the predictive performance of PLSR and SVR models. To further investigate model
480       generalizability, we next assessed how well these models perform when predicting traits for
481       genotypes that were not considered during calibration. This analysis was conducted using sub-
482       sampled HSR and applied the same nested cross-validation framework within season as
483       described above, with single CV for NoC estimation. Moreover, trait-specific ML and
484       aggregation techniques were used as in the previous analysis. Uniquely, in this scenario, we
485       enforced a strict separation of genotypes between the calibration and validation sets in both the
486       inner and outer cross-validation loops, ensuring that predictions were made exclusively on
487       unseen genotypes. This is relevant to prevent data leakage that can artificially increase model
488       performance.
489                  Overall, model performance for the unseen genotype scenario was consistently lower
490       than for the random split scenario (Figure 4). For genotype-averaged structural and biochemical
491       traits, PLSR models demonstrated strong generalizability, achieving similar R2 values in both the
492       random split and unseen genotype scenarios for all three seasons. This indicates that these traits
493       exhibit more stable relationships with HSR across genotypes. Interestingly, plot-averaged ΦPSII

549       4. Discussion
550                  HSR data have been used to train predictive ML models for different traits across variety
551       of plants and crops (Kumar et al., 2001; Serbin et al., 2012; Heckmann et al., 2017; Yendrek et
552       al., 2017; Silva-Perez et al., 2018; Cotrozzi et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2020; Furbank et al., 2021;
553       Wang et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022; Kaur et al., 2024). Despite the wider adoption of these data to
554       devise ML models, several pressing issues remain poorly explored: The most important issue

                                                                            20
      bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.03.661070; this version posted July 6, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
         was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                    available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




555       deals with understanding the generalizability of the ML models to data that have not been used in
556       training. Another issue addresses the applicability of ML models with data from the field, where
557       several data aggregation alternatives are possible. In addition, advances in ML have resulted in
558       variety of models, each with their set of hyperparameters requiring adequate procedure for their
559       tuning.
560                  To address these gaps, we evaluated model generalizability across increasingly realistic
561       prediction scenarios: (1) random data splits within season(s), (2) prediction of unseen genotypes
562       within a season, and (3) prediction of unseen genotypes from entirely unseen seasons. Using a
563       multi-season field dataset from a maize MAGIC population, we also explored how model choice
564       and data aggregation influence predictive performance.
565                  In the first part of our study, we calibrated PLSR models on the 2021 dataset, examined
566       model sensitivity to NoC, and compared three methods for NoC estimation: repeated CV, single
567       CV, and permutation-based selection. All approaches yielded consistent NoC estimates and
568       similar performance on validation folds. Sub-sampling the spectral data (every fifth wavelength)
569       provided results comparable to the full spectrum, substantially reducing computational time.
570                  Several traits demonstrated consistently poor predictability in this calibration step,
571       including %C, δ15N, iWUE, stomatal limitation, NPQ induction rate and slope, NPQ relaxation
572       offset, and final NPQ. Testing nonlinear models (XGBoost, neural networks, SVR with radial
573       kernel) yielded no improvement over PLSR, except for SVR with a linear kernel, which
574       performed comparably. Consequently, for downstream analysis, we focused on comparing PLSR
575       and linear SVR using sub-sampled spectra. Given that this analysis used the entire 2021 dataset
576       for NoC estimation, it served as a calibration step rather than a validation of generalizability.
577                  To properly evaluate generalizability, we employed a nested CV design, consisting of 20
578       outer loops of 5-fold CV. For each iteration, the inner calibration loop estimated NoC using
579       either repeated CV or single CV, while the outer validation loop assessed model generalizability.
580       To evaluate model generalizability using random data splits, both plot-level and genotype-level
581       aggregation were tested across the 2021, 2022, and 2023 seasons. Weakly predictable traits
582       identified during the calibration step remained of poor predictability regardless of pre-processing
583       or ML approaches and were therefore excluded from further analyses.

584                  Among structural and biochemical traits, SLA, ‰N, and C:N ratio showed strong
585       generalizability across all scenarios for both PLSR and SVR. These traits benefited most from
586       genotype-level averaging, which consistently improved prediction accuracy. In contrast,
587       photosynthetic traits were best predicted using raw data combined with linear SVR. Among these,
588       Vmax, representing the asymptotic maximum of the A-Ci curve under CO2-saturated Rubisco,
589       demonstrated the highest generalizability. In comparison, net assimilation rate (A), measured at a
590       fixed internal CO2 concentration of 400 ppm, may be more affected by transient environmental
591       variability, resulting in lower predictive accuracy. The estimation of Vpmax, estimated from the
592       slope of the A-Ci response at low internal CO2 concentrations, showed weaker predictability,
593       likely due to its reliance on more complex C4 photosynthetic mechanisms, including C4 acid
594       transport, CO2 leakiness, and energy partitioning, which are difficult to capture through HSR
595       alone, as previously reported (Yendrek et al., 2017).
596                  This study is also the first to comprehensively predict a set of fluorescence-based traits
597       using ML applied on HSR. Among these, ΦPSII recovery offset is the best predicted and reflects
598       the quantum efficiency of PSII at the end of light exposure. Traits such as Fv/Fm (maximum
599       PSII efficiency), final ΦPSII, and maximum NPQ showed moderate performance. Amplitude-
600       based metrics (e.g., NPQ induction/relaxation amplitude,                                      ΦPSII recovery amplitude)
601       outperformed rate-based metrics, likely because amplitudes capture longer-term physiological
602       changes detectable in reflectance, whereas rates reflect rapid processes upon light on/off switch
603       with weak structural signatures (Ferguson et al., 2025). In general, NPQ relaxation and ΦPSII
604       recovery traits were more predictable than NPQ induction traits, which occur rapidly and are
605       driven by transient electrochemical gradients. In contrast, NPQ relaxation and ΦPSII recovery
606       involve pigment transitions and structural dynamics that HSR is better suited to detect. Certain
607       fluorescence traits also required higher NoC (~30), suggesting these traits involve multiple
608       pigments, biochemical reactions and dynamic regulation mechanisms, resulting in complex and
609       distributed spectral signals.
610                  As expected, model generalizability declined across the three scenarios, with the greatest
611       challenge posed by predicting unseen genotypes from an entirely unseen season, the most
612       practically relevant setting. This marked drop in performance compared to random splits
613       underscores the influence of environmental variation on the predictive power of HSR data.

614                  Our results demonstrate that there was no universally optimal ML model or data
615       aggregation strategy. Instead, trait-specific approaches were necessary to reach optimal
616       performance. HSR is well-suited for predicting integrative traits, like SLA and %N, while
617       physiological traits, such as: ΦPSII recovery and maximum PSII efficiency, exhibited more
618       moderate performance. Short-term, dynamic traits remain difficult to predict using HSR data.
619                  Our work paves the way for scrutinizing the predictive power of HSR data for different
620       classes of traits by usage of well-established CV techniques and ML models. In addition, it also
621       points out that data transformations can have often drastic effect on model performance,
622       particularly for prediction scenarios relevant in practice, e.g., breeding. Finally, our work reveals
623       the persistent challenge of predicting dynamic physiological traits, motivating further research
624       into novel feature engineering and temporal modeling approaches.
