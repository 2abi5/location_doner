Abstract

To address the challenge of balancing computational efficiency with fine-grained feature capture in complex field environments when using existing deep learning methods for tomato leaf disease detection, this paper proposes a novel lightweight classification model called Visual Mamba with Frequency-channel attention, Cross-layer attention and Salient feature suppression (VMamba-FCS). Based on the visual state-space model, this model integrates three collaborative enhancement mechanisms: a frequency-domain channel attention module, which improves the perception of disease-related textures by recalibrating features in the frequency domain; a cross-layer attention module, which promotes multi-scale feature fusion by integrating the semantic context of early layers; and a salient feature suppression module, which forces the network to learn more comprehensive discriminative features to improve robustness by suppressing overactivated feature regions during training. Experimental results on the real-world field dataset “Tomato-Village” demonstrate that VMamba-FCS achieves a classification accuracy of 93.62% and an inference speed of 126.5 frames per second (FPS) with only 1.20 M parameters, representing a 7.48% improvement in accuracy compared to the basic VMamba model. In the cross-dataset (PlantDoc) generalization test, VMamba-FCS significantly outperformed all comparison models with an accuracy of 71.3%, demonstrating its excellent domain adaptability and robustness. This work verifies the effectiveness of the multi-mechanism collaborative enhancement strategy in the state-space model architecture, providing a new lightweight solution for real-time and accurate agricultural disease detection on resource-constrained edge devices.

Keywords:
disease classification; lightweight deep learning; visual state-space model; attention mechanism; feature suppression; precision agriculture

1. Introduction

Global agriculture is undergoing a profound transformation driven by artificial intelligence (AI) and intelligent agricultural technologies. The stable production of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), as an economic crop widely cultivated worldwide, is crucial for ensuring food security and maintaining the livelihoods of small farmers. However, the invasion of various diseases, such as early blight, late blight, leafminer, and leaf blight virus, and various nutritional deficiencies often lead to yield losses of 20% to 60% [1]. The traditional visual inspection methods that rely on agricultural experts have inherent drawbacks, such as low efficiency, high cost, and susceptibility to subjective judgments, often leading to delays in prevention and control and significant economic losses [1,2].

In this context, deep learning techniques represented by Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Visual Transformers (ViTs) provide powerful tools for solving these problems. For example, the classic ResNet architecture and its variants have proven their effectiveness as powerful feature extractors in multiple recognition tasks [3,4]. Specifically, models based on the ResNet CDMV achieved a mAP value of 87% in the recognition of secondary factors in natural disasters [5]. The improved variants of this model have consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to most baseline models in image recognition tasks [3]. Furthermore, ResNet is frequently adopted as a robust benchmark model for complex visual challenges such as medical image analysis [3].

At the same time, the research community continues to explore more efficient and lightweight advanced visual architectures in pursuit of higher performance and deployment efficiency. For instance, CrossViT effectively integrates multi-scale visual features by combining image blocks of different sizes and introducing cross-branch cross-attention mechanisms. On the ImageNet-1K dataset, the CrossViT-15† model achieved a top-1 accuracy of 82.3%, and the CrossViT-18† model surpassed the performance of the ViT Base model with about half the computational cost [6]. FcaNet has theoretically generalized traditional channel attention from the perspective of frequency-domain analysis, using discrete cosine transform (DCT) and multi-frequency components to compress richer channel information. When using the ResNet-50 backbone network on ImageNet, it achieved a top-1 accuracy of 78.43%, surpassing methods such as SENet and CBAM in the same period, and achieved higher AP metrics in COCO object detection tasks [7]. DilatedFormer has designed a multi-scale dilated attention (MSDA) mechanism, which significantly reduces computational complexity while effectively expanding the model receptive field. Its Dilate-S model achieved a top-1 accuracy of 83.3% on ImageNet with only 4.8 GFLOPs, comparable to the Swin-B model with a computational load of 15.4 GFLOPs, achieving up to 70% FLOPs savings [8]. EdgeNeXt proposed an innovative hybrid architecture that efficiently combines the locality of CNNs with the global modeling advantages of Transformer through the segmentation depth separable transpose attention (SDTA) encoder, making it highly suitable for mobile vision applications [9]. RepViT drew inspiration from the efficient architecture of Vision Transformer to redesign lightweight CNNs. In the ImageNet classification task, its RepViT-M1.0 achieved over 80% top-1 accuracy with only a 1.0 millisecond delay on the iPhone 12, while RepViT-M2.3 achieved an accuracy of 83.7%, achieving an excellent balance between speed and accuracy [10]. In addition, the Hide-and-Seek weakly supervised framework forces the network to “find” and learn other relevant parts of an object when the most discriminative features are missing by randomly hiding patches in the training images. It performs better than the forward approach in weakly supervised localization tasks on the ILSVRC dataset, significantly improving the comprehensiveness and robustness of the model localization [11].

Recently, the VMamba architecture and its variants based on the state-space model (SSM) have shown great potential in multiple core visual tasks due to the linear computational complexity and powerful long-range dependency modeling capabilities of its 2D selective scanning (SS2D) module [12]. Its variant MSVMamba has shown competitiveness in image classification tasks; for example, MSVMamba Tiny achieved a top-1 accuracy of 82.8% on ImageNet [13]. Furthermore, CaVMamba combines convolution with VMamba capabilities and demonstrates superior performance on multiple public medical image segmentation benchmarks [14]. The Space Spectral Fusion Network (SSFN) based on VMamba, used for multimodal remote sensing image classification, outperforms multiple existing methods on three benchmark datasets and requires the lowest number of parameters [15]. These successful applications have validated the powerful ability of this type of model to handle fine-grained, heterogeneous and complex information, providing new ideas for high-performance lightweight deployment.

Although various deep learning models have achieved extremely high accuracy on laboratory-controlled datasets such as PlantVillage [1], their performance drops sharply in actual field scenarios with complex backgrounds, uneven lighting, overlapping leaves and coexisting symptoms, forming a prominent “reality gap” that limits the practical deployment of technology [1]. The publicly available “Tomato-Village” dataset, released by Gehlot et al. in 2023 as the first public tomato disease dataset containing real field images and complete annotations for multi-class classification, multi-label classification and object detection [1], was constructed based on 4525 naturally occurring disease samples collected in March 2022 from actual commercial tomato fields in Rajasthan, India, covering healthy leaves and seven common tomato diseases and nutritional deficiencies. Different from the standardized laboratory dataset PlantVillage, it fully retains complex interference factors such as uneven lighting, cluttered backgrounds and diverse disease manifestations in real field production and can truly simulate actual diagnosis scenarios, and thus provides an irreplaceable core benchmark for solving the above “reality gap” bottleneck and developing robust disease recognition models.

At the intersection of this practical challenge and research frontier, our preliminary exploration has shown that although efficient models such as VMamba have shown potential in tasks such as complex background recognition [16] and real-time object detection [17], they still face three core limitations when processing fine-grained and high-complexity real field tomato disease images. First, their core sequential scanning mechanism may not sufficiently capture the subtle high-frequency texture features (such as color difference and texture changes of early disease spots) exhibited by early diseases. Second, the model’s ability to integrate multi-scale contextual information (which is crucial for distinguishing different diseases with similar morphologies) still needs to be strengthened. Finally, the model may overly rely on the most significant feature regions and ignore other sub-significant regions with diagnostic significance, thereby affecting generalization robustness in complex and noisy real-world scenarios.

To address these limitations, this paper proposes the VMamba-FCS model. We employ a triple collaborative enhancement mechanism to specifically improve the basic VMamba architecture, incorporating three novel technical contributions. These contributions aim to systematically resolve the aforementioned limitations, and their effectiveness is validated in real-world datasets and cross-dataset generalization tests. The three technical contributions are as follows:

(1)

A frequency-domain channel attention module based on the orthogonal Fourier transform. By extracting global frequency-domain descriptors and recalibrating features, this module solves the problem of insufficient perception of high-frequency textures and edges in the basic VMamba model. Compared with the frequency-domain attention design of FcaNet in CNNs, this work introduces and adapts it for the first time into a state-space model with selective scanning as the core, providing structured frequency-domain representation capabilities for visual Mamba.

(2)

Cross-layer cross-attention module. By using high-level features as context to guide the current layer, the model’s ability to fuse features of multi-scale disease patterns is effectively improved. Compared to methods such as CrossViT that require building additional parallel branches, this work abandons complex architectures. Instead, it exclusively utilizes its inherent network hierarchy in a switchable manner, achieving efficient multi-scale semantic fusion with minimal computational overhead.

(3)

Salient feature suppression module. By identifying and suppressing highly activated regions in the feature maps, the network is forced to utilize sub-salient discriminative features to alleviate the overfitting of local salient regions. Compared to the Hide-and-Seek regularization method that randomly occludes the input image space, this work represents a paradigm shift from image space to feature space, achieving more accurate and direct forward regularization while enhancing the robustness of feature learning.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Dataset

This study adopted the official publicly available Tomato-Village dataset [1] to validate the performance of the proposed method based on its “single leaf single disease” multi-classification subset. This subset contains a total of 4525 high-resolution images (256 × 256 pixels), covering healthy leaf status and 7 common tomato leaf abnormalities, including early blight, late blight, leaf miner, magnesium deficiency, nitrogen deficiency, potassium deficiency, and spotted wilt virus (typical samples of each category are shown in Figure 1).

Figure 1.
Typical original image examples of 8 categories in the Tomato-Village dataset. (a) Healthy leaf. (b) Nitrogen deficiency. (c) Magnesium deficiency. (d) Potassium deficiency. (e) Early blight. (f) Late blight. (g) Leaf miner. (h) Spotted wilt virus.

All images in the subset were collected in March 2022 from open-air commercial tomato fields in Rajasthan, India, via consumer-grade mobile devices, without any manual control of shooting conditions or artificial disease inoculation. All samples fully retained the authentic interference factors in actual field production, including uneven natural lighting, cluttered soil and weed backgrounds, overlapping and occluded leaves, and naturally occurring disease symptoms with diverse morphological manifestations. This core characteristic forms a significant contrast with the laboratory-controlled PlantVillage dataset (with a pure background, standardized shooting and artificial inoculation), making it a more challenging and practically relevant benchmark for evaluating the real-world generalization ability of the model in complex agricultural scenarios.

2.2. Dataset Partitioning and Preprocessing

This study adopted a systematic data preprocessing and partitioning process to ensure the effectiveness of model training and the objectivity of evaluation. All images were first uniformly adjusted to 224 × 224 pixels through center cropping and then randomly divided into a training set, a validation set, and a testing set in a ratio of 7:1:2 using a stratified sampling strategy to ensure that the distribution of each category in the subsets was consistent with the original dataset. Finally, 3168 images were obtained in the training set, 453 images in the validation set, and 904 images in the testing set.

During the training phase, in order to enhance the model’s generalization ability to complex field environments such as lighting changes and leaf posture diversity, we implemented a strong data augmentation strategy on the training set, which included random horizontal/vertical flipping, ±15° random rotation, and color jitter (with brightness, contrast, and saturation adjustment amplitudes of 0.2). The visualization examples of related operations are shown in Figure 2. The validation set and test set remained in their original state without any enhancement processing to ensure the objectivity and impartiality of the model performance evaluation [18]. The validation set was used for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping decisions during the training process, while the test set was strictly reserved for final performance evaluation.

Figure 2.
Data augmentation.

All images underwent standardization before being input into the network: first, the pixel values were normalized from the integer range of [0, 255] to the floating-point range of [0.0, 1.0] by dividing by 255. Subsequently, the global statistics of the ImageNet dataset were used for channel-by-channel normalization, which involved subtracting the means [0.485, 0.456, 0.406] from the RGB channels and dividing by the standard deviations [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]. This was done to adapt the input data distribution to the large-scale pre-trained model, ensuring training stability and promoting model convergence.

2.3. VMamba Architecture

To address the challenge of balancing efficiency and global modeling capability in traditional deep learning models and secure an improved foundation for this work, we adopted VMamba, a visual backbone network based on the state-space model (SSM), as the core architecture [12]. The core advantage of this architecture lies in its 2D selective scanning module (SS2D), which can achieve global receptive field modeling of two-dimensional image data while maintaining only linear computational complexity, providing new possibilities for processing high-resolution and long-range contextual understanding of field disease images.

In order to effectively apply one-dimensional sequence models to two-dimensional visual data, VMamba introduced the 2D selective scanning module (SS2D). The core idea of this module is to serialize images along multiple spatial directions. The sequences in each direction are independently processed through the discrete S6 block mentioned above to learn the spatial dependencies on that path. Finally, the output features processed in all directions are fused and reconstructed into a two-dimensional feature map. This design enables the model to efficiently integrate global contextual information from images while maintaining linear computational complexity, providing important long-range modeling capabilities for visual tasks [12].

2.3.2. Limitation Analysis of Disease Detection Tasks

Although VMamba performs well in general visual tasks, its native architecture has three design limitations when processing fine-grained, high-noise, real field tomato disease images, which restrict further improvement in recognition performance:

(1)

Insufficient perception of high-frequency texture features. The continuous system nature of the state-space model (SSM) tends to smooth high-frequency signals and preserve the global structure of low-frequency signals during modeling. The early diagnosis of tomato diseases based on features such as the subtle concentric whorls formed in the early stages of disease and the faded texture between leaf veins highly relies on high-frequency details in the image. The sequential scanning mechanism of VMamba has inherent limitations in processing high-frequency, localized discriminative textures, which may lead to insufficient sensitivity in capturing finer representations of early lesions.

(2)

Lack of a multi-scale feature fusion mechanism. VMamba uses a hierarchical downsampling structure to extract multi-scale features, but there is a lack of effective interaction mechanisms between feature flows at different levels. Deep features contain rich semantic information but lose spatial details, while shallow features have completed spatial details but low semantic abstraction. In field scenes, there are significant differences in the size and morphology of disease spots (ranging from small needle-like spots to spots that cover most of the leaves). Relying solely on hierarchical feature extraction, it is difficult to achieve effective complementarity and fusion of cross-scale information, which limits the model’s ability to model multi-scale disease patterns uniformly.

(3)

A weak ability to suppress complex background interference. The global scanning mechanism of SS2D not only establishes long-range dependencies but also gives equal attention to all regions in the image. In real field images, complex backgrounds such as soil, weeds, shadows, and water droplets can easily confuse disease areas in the feature space. The lack of an adaptive attention or suppression mechanism to focus on the true disease area and filter out irrelevant background interference makes the model susceptible to noise interference during inference, leading to misjudging background features as disease signs and reducing robustness and generalization ability in complex environments.

In response to the above limitations, this article proposes the VMamba-FCS model in Section 2.4, which enhances the basic VMamba architecture by introducing three collaborative mechanisms: frequency-domain attention, cross-layer interaction, and feature suppression, in order to improve its performance and robustness in real field tomato disease recognition tasks.

2.4. VMamba-FCS Model Architecture

To address the three aspects of the inherent limitations of VMamba (high-frequency texture perception, multi-scale fusion, and background anti-interference) mentioned earlier, this study proposes the VMamba-FCS model. This model is based on VMamba and integrates three innovative collaborative modules—Frequency Channel Attention (FCA), cross-layer cross-attention (CLCA), and salient feature suppression (SFS)—to form a progressive optimization architecture of “high-frequency texture perception → multi-scale semantic fusion → robust feature learning”. It is designed specifically for fine-grained tomato disease classification tasks in real field environments. The overall architecture of VMamba-FCS is shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3.
The overall architecture of VMamba-FCS.

The model input is image 𝐼∈𝑅𝐻×𝑊×3I∈RH×W×3. Firstly, the image is divided into 4 × 44 × 4 non-overlapping blocks through a patch embedding layer, and each block is projected as an embedding vector to obtain the initial feature map. Subsequently, the features are hierarchically encoded through four feature extraction stages composed of VSSLayers. Adjacent VSSLayers are downsampled twice using the PatchMerging2D module. The specific operation includes dividing the input feature map into 4 sub-blocks in a 2 × 22 × 2 grid, concatenating them in the channel dimension, normalizing them with LayerNorm, and compressing the number of channels back to their original size through a linear projection layer. After 3 downsampling cycles, the spatial size of the feature map decreases step by step, and semantic information is abstracted step by step. Finally, the classification results are output through Global Average Pooling and fully connected layers.

2.5. VSSBlock Module

The core of this architecture design lies in targeted improvements to the basic VSSBlock. As shown in Figure 4, each VSSBlock integrates three innovative sub-modules, namely, frequency-domain channel attention (FCA), cross-layer cross-attention (CLCA), and salient feature suppression (SFS), on the basis of the original SS2D module, forming a collaborative processing flow of “feature calibration → core computation → regularization enhancement”. Specifically, the internal data flow of each VSSBlock is as follows:

Figure 4.
Improved VSSBlock.

(1)

Feature normalization and frequency-domain calibration. The input features are first normalized by the LayerNorm layer and then enter the FCA module. This module extracts global frequency-domain descriptors through the Fast Fourier Transform and recalibrates channel weights based on them, aiming to enhance the model’s perception of key high-frequency textures of diseases. The FCA module acts on the starting position of all VSSBlocks.

(2)

Modeling the core state space. The calibrated features are fed into the SS2D module to perform core selective scanning operations, establishing global spatial dependencies with linear complexity.

(3)

Regularization of the feature space. The output of SS2D then enters the SFS module. This module identifies and suppresses overactivated salient regions in the feature map, forcing the network to explore and utilize more discriminative secondary salient features as a forward regularization approach to enhance model robustness. The SFS module also applies to all VSSBlock after SS2D operations.

(4)

Cross-layer semantic fusion (triggered by conditions). After the above processing, the features will be connected to the original input through a DropPath operation to stabilize the training. Finally, if the CLCA module is enabled, the current features will interact with contextual features from the previous network layer (aligned) through cross-attention, thereby achieving high-level semantic guidance and multi-scale fusion of the current layer features. The CLCA module is enabled from the second feature stage and only applies to the last VSSBlock of each stage.

(5)

Modular design. All three enhancer modules (FCA, CLCA, and SFS) are designed to be independently enabled or disabled through configuration switches, ensuring the flexibility of the architecture and facilitating subsequent ablation experimental analysis of module contributions.

Through this hierarchical and functionally collaborative modular design, the improved VSSBlock can systematically alleviate the perception deficiency, fusion inefficiency, and overfitting problems of the basic VMamba in processing fine-grained field disease images with extremely low additional parameter overhead, thus becoming a key unit for achieving high-performance and robust classification in the VMamba-FCS model.

2.5.1. Frequency Channel Attention (FCA)

Traditional channel attention mechanisms, such as SENet, typically obtain channel descriptors through Global Average Pooling (GAP), but GAP essentially only corresponds to the lowest frequency component of the discrete cosine transform (DCT), resulting in significant information loss when generating attention weights and failing to fully utilize the complete distribution of features in the frequency domain. However, in plant disease recognition, key discriminative features such as the fine texture of lesions, color distortion, and edge information are typically manifested as specific mid- to high-frequency components in the frequency domain. To comprehensively characterize channel information and enhance the model’s perception of disease-related textures, this work redefines the channel feature compression problem as a frequency-domain analysis problem and for the first time introduces a structured frequency-domain feature calibration mechanism in the visual state-space model, namely, the Frequency Channel Attention (FCA) module.

2.5.2. Salient Feature Suppression (SFS)

In order to alleviate the problem of models overly relying on a few of the most significant discriminative regions under weak supervision or limited data, existing methods force the network to learn to focus on other components of the object by randomly hiding some regions of the input image during the training phase, thereby obtaining a more comprehensive feature representation. However, this method has two potential drawbacks: randomness may cause key discriminative regions to be masked, thereby affecting training stability and convergence speed; meanwhile, the handling of occluded areas (such as filling in the mean) may introduce biases that do not match the true test distribution. To overcome the above limitations, the SFS module elevates this regularization idea to the feature space, and its core insight is that the overactivated regions in the feature map are the root cause of the model’s “lazy” dependencies. Therefore, this module actively identifies and suppresses these “most significant” feature regions during the training process, forcing the network to deactivate and utilize those less significant but equally discriminative features, thereby guiding the model to learn more comprehensive and robust feature representations.

As shown in Figure 4c, given the intermediate layer feature map, 𝑋∈𝑅𝐵×𝐻×𝑊×𝐶X∈RB×H×W×C, the SFS module operation process is as follows:

(1)

Calculate spatial significance distribution: Firstly, the feature activation intensity, 𝑆 = ∣𝑋∣S = ∣X∣, is obtained through absolute values and then averaged along the channel dimension to obtain the spatial significance map, “spatial” ∈𝑅𝐵×𝐻×𝑊×1∈RB×H×W×1, which comprehensively reflects the overall activation level of each spatial position.

(2)

Identify and suppress the most significant regions: Based on the preset inhibition ratio, 𝛼α (default 𝛼=0.2α=0.2), calculate the number of significant positions to be suppressed in each image, 𝑘=⌊𝛼⋅𝐻⋅𝑊⌋k=⌊α⋅H⋅W⌋. Select the top 𝑘 k  positions with the highest activation values on “spatial” to form the set 𝑃topkPtopk. To encourage network exploration of context, inhibition will be applied to each salient position and its (2𝑅+1)×(2𝑅+1)(2R+1)×(2R+1) local neighborhood (default inhibition radius, 𝑅=1R=1).

(3)

Build and apply suppression mask: Create a mask, 𝑀M, that is the same shape as 𝑋X, with an initial value of 1. For all positions (𝑚, 𝑛)(m, n) located within the suppression neighborhood, set their mask values to 𝛽β (default 𝛽β = 0.1), which means that only 10% of the original feature values are retained. The feature calculation after suppression is 𝑋̃=𝑋⊙𝑀X~=X⊙M, where ⊙⊙ represents element-wise multiplication.

(4)

Residual output and gradient separation: The final output is generated by residual connection: 𝑋out=𝑋+(𝑋̃−𝑋).detach ()Xout=X+(X~−X).detach (). The use of the “. detach ()detach ()” operation here aims to prevent gradient backpropagation through suppression paths, ensuring that SFS serves as a forward regularization method that does not affect the updating of backbone parameters, thereby maintaining the stability of training.

This module is placed after the SS2D operation in each VSSBlock, and by controlling the suppression of overly prominent features it forces the model to abandon its dependence on locally strongly activated regions and instead explore and integrate more comprehensive and diverse discriminative feature clues on the leaves. This mechanism significantly improves the model’s generalization ability and robustness to diverse disease patterns in complex and noisy field scenes.

2.5.3. Cross-Layer Cross-Attention (CLCA)

To effectively integrate multi-scale features and overcome the limitations of insufficient semantic interaction between layers in the basic VMamba architecture, we designed a cross-layer cross-attention module. Unlike methods such as CrossViT [5] that require the construction of additional parallel branches, this module utilizes the network’s own feature pyramid in an endogenous manner. Through lightweight and switchable cross-attention operations, it directly establishes semantic dialogues between adjacent hierarchical features, achieving efficient multi-scale context fusion with almost no additional architectural overhead.

As shown in Figure 4d, in the hierarchical architecture of VMamba-FCS, shallow features contain rich spatial details (such as leaf vein texture and lesion edges), while deep features encode higher-order semantic abstractions (such as the overall morphology and category concepts of lesions). The CLCA module aims to utilize the deep semantic features of the previous layer as context to guide and enhance the feature learning of the current layer.

Given the current layer feature, 𝑋𝑐𝑢𝑟∈𝑅𝐵×𝐻×𝑊×𝐶Xcur∈RB×H×W×C, and the contextual feature, 𝑋𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑣∈𝑅𝐵×𝐻𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑣×𝑊𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑣×𝐶Xprev∈RB×Hprev×Wprev×C, from the previous layer, first perform spatial alignment on 𝑋prevXprev, scale its resolution to match 𝑋curXcur through dimension permutation and bilinear interpolation, and obtain the aligned feature, 𝑋𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑣-𝑎𝑙𝑖𝑔𝑛∈𝑅𝐵×𝐻×𝑊×𝐶Xprev-align∈RB×H×W×C.

To improve parallel computing efficiency and representation capability, the module adopts a four-head attention mechanism. The query vector (𝑄Q) and key value vector (𝐾, 𝑉)(K, V) are obtained through linear projection and multi-head splitting:

𝑄=Split(𝑊𝑞𝑋cur),  (𝐾,𝑉)=Split(𝑊kv𝑋prev-align)Q=Split(WqXcur),  (K,V)=Split(WkvXprev-align)

(8)

where 𝑊𝑞∈𝑅𝐶×𝐶Wq∈RC×C and 𝑊𝑘𝑣∈𝑅𝐶×2𝐶Wkv∈RC×2C are learnable projection weights. The “𝑆𝑝𝑙𝑖𝑡(⋅)Split(⋅)” operation splits the features into 4 heads and flattens the spatial dimension, resulting in 𝑄,𝐾,𝑉∈𝑅𝐵×𝑛𝑢𝑚_ℎ𝑒𝑎𝑑𝑠×(𝐻×𝑊)×ℎ𝑒𝑎𝑑_𝑑𝑖𝑚Q,K,V∈RB×num_heads×(H×W)×head_dim.

The calculation of attention weights follows the standard scaled dot-product attention formula:

(9)

where 𝑑𝑘dk is the dimension of the key vector. The calculated multi-head attention output is concatenated and linearly projected to obtain the fused feature 𝑋crossXcross, which is then added to the current layer feature as a residual:

(10)

This design abandons complex parallel branch structures and achieves efficient interaction between deep and shallow features with minimal computational overhead by reusing the network’s own features. The four-head attention mechanism combines a Dropout operation to further enhance generalization while balancing computational cost and expressive power. This module is only enabled in the last VSSBlock of each feature stage, using the previous layer’s features as context to guide the current layer to fuse richer semantic information. It plays a key role in distinguishing diseases with similar morphologies but different details, such as early and late diseases.

2.6. Experimental Environment and Training Parameters

2.6.1. Experimental Environment

All experiments were conducted in the following hardware and software environments: the processor was an Intel Xeon Platinum 8352V CPU (2.1 GHz, 36 cores), the graphics card was an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB GDDR6X memory), the system memory was 120 GB DDR4-3200, and the operating system was Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. The code was developed based on Python 3.9 and the PyTorch 2.1.0 framework, and GPU accelerated computing was implemented through CUDA 12.1.

2.6.2. Training Parameters and Strategies

The model training used the AdamW optimizer, with an initial learning rate set to 1×10−51×10−5 and the weight decay set to 1×10−41×10−4. The learning rate scheduling adopted a cosine annealing strategy, which was dynamically adjusted during the training process according to the following formula:

𝑙𝑟 = 𝑙𝑟0×0.5×(1+cos(𝜋×epoch𝑇𝑡𝑜𝑡𝑎𝑙))lr = lr0×0.5×(1+cos(π×epochTtotal))

(11)

where 𝑙𝑟0lr0 is the initial learning rate and 𝑇𝑡𝑜𝑡𝑎𝑙Ttotal is the total number of training cycles. This scheduling strategy kept the learning rate relatively stable in the early stages of training and gradually decreased to near zero in the later stages, which helped the model to adjust parameters more finely in the convergence stage and obtain better local optima. The loss function adopted CrossEntropy Loss and set gradient clipping (maximum norm of 3.0) to prevent numerical instability caused by excessive gradient amplitude during training. To further improve training efficiency and balance computational accuracy, automatic mixed precision training was used throughout the experiment.

The experiment consisted of 100 training cycles, with a batch size of 4. In each round of training, the model parameters were updated based on the loss on the training set. The validation set was used to monitor the performance of the model and implement an early stop strategy based on its loss changes. When the validation loss did not decrease for 10 consecutive cycles, the training was terminated to avoid overfitting. The test set did not participate in parameter updates or model selection during the training period, and it was only used for objective evaluation and comparative analysis of the final model performance.

3. Results and Analysis

3.1. Ablation Experiment

3.1.1. Quantitative Analysis of Module Effectiveness

We conducted detailed ablation experiments on the Tomato View dataset to evaluate the independent contributions and synergistic effects of the proposed frequency-domain channel attention (F), cross-layer cross-attention (C), and salient feature suppression (S) modules on model performance. Based on the basic VMamba model, the experiment introduced the above modules separately or in combination and constructed eight model variants, including VMamba, VMamba+S, VMamba+C, VMamba+F, VMamba+C+S, VMamba+F+S, VMamba+F+C, and the complete VMamba-FCS. All comparisons were conducted under the same training strategy and evaluation system to ensure the comparability of the results and the reliability of the conclusions. As shown in Table 1, the benchmark model VMamba achieved an accuracy of 86.14% and a Macro-F1 value of 82.56%, demonstrating its basic recognition ability in complex field scenes. However, the model still exhibits significant recall bottlenecks in specific categories such as early blight (Eb) and healthy leaves (H), revealing its inherent limitations in fine-grained feature perception, multi-scale semantic fusion, and resistance to background interference. The gradual introduction of subsequent modules clearly demonstrates how various mechanisms can effectively address these shortcomings and ultimately achieve significant performance improvements through synergistic effects.

Overall performance comparison of different module combinations.

In terms of the impact of a single module, each enhancement mechanism shows a clear performance gain. The Vmamba+S model with the significant feature suppression module (S) showed improved accuracy and Macro-F1 values of 87.35% and 84.02%, respectively. More importantly, this module shows a breakthrough improvement in the recognition of early diseases (Eb), with its recall rate significantly increasing from 69.00% of the benchmark model to 93.00%. This indicates that by actively suppressing the most prominent regions in the feature map, the S module effectively forces the network to break free from its “lazy” dependence on local strong features and instead explore and utilize previously overlooked and dispersed fine-grained disease patterns. The addition of the cross-layer cross-attention module (C) resulted in an accuracy of 86.36% and a Macro-F1 value of 83.91% for the Vmamba+C model. Although the overall improvement is relatively mild, it validates the positive role of integrating deep semantic context in understanding the overall structure of leaves and distinguishing similar morphological categories, endowing the model with better multi-scale understanding ability. In contrast, the frequency-domain channel attention module (F) showed the most significant performance improvement when introduced separately, with the accuracy and Macro-F1 value of the Vmamba+F model reaching 89.11% and 85.91%, respectively. This result strongly indicates that in real field tomato disease datasets, high-frequency texture information related to disease spots is the core feature for accurate differentiation, and the F module significantly enhances the model’s perception of such key details through the frequency-domain recalibration mechanism.

When different modules are combined, their synergistic effect brings a performance leap beyond simple summation. The Vmamba+F+C model, which combines frequency-domain attention and cross-layer attention, has achieved significant improvements, with accuracy and Macro-F1 values jumping to 90.65% and 89.28%, respectively. This combination has effectively overcome the bottleneck of the identification of the healthy blade (H) category, and its accuracy rate has significantly increased from about 58% under a single module to 76.32%. This proves that the calibration of texture features (F) and the fusion of multi-scale semantics (C) have a profound synergistic effect, enabling the model to simultaneously grasp local details and global context, thereby more reliably distinguishing healthy leaves from diseased leaves with early mild symptoms. In the end, the complete VMamba-FCS model integrating all three modules achieved optimal performance, with accuracy and Macro-F1 values reaching 93.62% and 90.28%, respectively. Compared to the base VMamba, the weighted F1 score improved by over 6%. The final introduced S module plays a role in “refinement” and “robustness” here. Based on the rich and accurate features provided by the preceding F and C modules, it further suppresses possible background noise interference and promotes the network to focus on the most discriminative combination of pathological features, thereby ensuring the high accuracy and strong generalization ability of the model in complex field environments.

After further integrating the cross-layer cross-attention module (C), the heatmap of the Vmamba+F+C model (Figure 7d) shows a wider and more complete coverage of lesions. The model not only focuses on the core area of a lesion but also perceives peripheral tissue abnormalities related to the lesion (such as halos and spreading areas) based on deep semantic context, reflecting the advantage of multi-scale feature fusion. However, there is still a small amount of weak false activation of shadow areas at the edges of the heatmap, indicating that there is still room for improvement in the robustness of the model to complex background interference.

In the end, the complete VMamba-FCS model generated the most accurate and clean heatmap (Figure 7e). Its attention is highly constrained within the real pathological area, and the activation of the background and healthy parts is almost completely suppressed. The salient feature suppression module (S) plays a crucial role in “attention purification” by suppressing residual non-discriminative activation regions in the preceding stage (Figure 7d), forcing the model to rely entirely on the most discriminative pathological features for decision-making. This visualization result strongly confirms that VMamba-FCS has successfully achieved efficient learning and focusing on the essential characteristics of diseases through multi-mechanism collaboration, effectively eliminating dependence on background interference, and provides a visual explanation for its high-precision and robust classification performance.

All models were trained and tested on the same Tomato-Village dataset and experimental settings to ensure fairness in comparison. The overall performance indicators are summarized in Table 3. As shown in Table 3, VMamba-FCS achieved the optimal balance between accuracy and efficiency among all compared methods.

Overall performance comparison with mainstream lightweight models.

As shown in the table above, VMamba-FCS achieved a significant lead in classification accuracy. Its top-1 accuracy reached 93.62%, and its macro-F1 score reached 90.28%. Compared to the classic CNN benchmark ResNet-50 (accuracy 72.06%), VMamba-FCS achieved an absolute improvement of 21.56 percentage points. This excellent balance achieved in three dimensions of high precision, extreme lightweightness, and real-time capability fully confirms that VMamba-FCS is very suitable for deployment on agricultural edge devices (such as embedded systems and mobile terminals) with limited computing resources, storage space, and power consumption and can meet the complex and stringent requirements of accuracy and response speed for actual field disease diagnosis.

3.3. Cross-Dataset Testing

To scientifically evaluate the generalization ability and robustness of the VMamba-FCS model when there are significant changes in data distribution, we have designed rigorous cross-dataset testing. This test aims to simulate a key real-world challenge, the question being whether the model can be directly applied to complex and ever-changing real field environments when trained on controlled laboratory-standard images. This test is not only a touchstone for measuring whether the model’s feature learning overly relies on specific data distributions (i.e., overfitting) but also provides empirical evidence for understanding the actual deployment boundaries of the model.

The testing strictly follows the principle of “controlling variables” to ensure fairness in the comparison and the validity of the conclusions. We trained various models on the Tomato-Village dataset and applied them to the PlantDoc dataset [20] for testing. Due to the differences in disease category systems between the two datasets, in order to eliminate the bias caused by mismatched evaluation results, we first performed a systematic category alignment process on the two datasets. By comparing the category labels and visual features of two datasets in detail, common disease categories with consistent pathological definitions and highly comparable visual diagnostic features were selected. Based on this filtering criterion, we extracted images corresponding to these common categories from Tomato View and PlantDoc and constructed training and testing subsets that were fully aligned with each category. All models participating in the comparison, including VMamba-FCS, base VMamba, and mainstream models such as EdgeNeXt Small, RepViT, DilatedFormer, and ResNet-50, were retrained on the alignment training subset of Tomato-Village and subsequently evaluated directly on the alignment testing subset of PlantDoc without any fine-tuning. The results of the cross-dataset testing are shown in Table 5.

Table 5.

The results in Table 5 show that VMamba-FCS outperforms all comparison models in key metrics such as accuracy, macro-average F1 score, and weighted F1 score, which validates the model’s good generalization performance in the face of domain bias. These factors collectively contribute to the performance degradation of the model when transitioning from one field environment to another. However, VMamba-FCS showed the relatively smallest performance degradation among all compared models, further confirming the effectiveness of its architecture design in enhancing model generalization ability and providing strong support for the deployment of the model in different field environments.

4. Discussion

4.1. The Adaptability and Limitations of the Visual Mamba Architecture in Complex Agricultural Scenes

The core starting point of this study is the recognition that although the visual Mamba architecture based on the state-space model (SSM) performs well in general visual tasks due to its linear computational complexity and global receptive field, it still has limitations when directly applied to unstructured and textually complex field plant disease recognition scenarios without targeted inductive bias. The experimental results show that the basic VMamba model achieved an accuracy of 86.14% on the Tomato-Village dataset, which is better than that of the traditional CNN model. However, when dealing with disease categories with subtle texture features (such as “early disease (Eb)”), the recall rate is only 69.00%, indicating that it has significant shortcomings in capturing local high-frequency details.

This discovery is consistent with recent related research. Zhu et al. [21] pointed out in their study on Vision Mamba (Vim) that although the bidirectional scanning mechanism of the SSM can effectively model long-range dependencies, its ability to model local correlations in the two-dimensional space of images is still weaker than that of convolutional networks. In the field of agricultural vision, Zhang and Mu [22] also observed a similar phenomenon in GMamba, finding that a simple SSM structure can easily lose edge and texture details when processing fine-grained lesion segmentation of grape leaves without the assistance of convolutional local features. The VMamba-FCS model proposed in this article introduces three collaborative mechanisms: frequency-domain channel attention, cross-layer cross-attention, and salient feature suppression. Essentially, it systematically enhances local texture perception, multi-scale semantic fusion, and feature robustness on the global modeling skeleton of the SSM. While maintaining the advantages of efficient long-range modeling of the SSM, it effectively compensates for its shortcomings in fine-grained feature extraction and complex background robustness, providing a new technical path for improving the adaptability of visual Mamba models in complex agricultural scenes.

4.2. Mechanism Analysis of Frequency-Domain Perception and Multi-Scale Interaction

The performance improvement achieved by VMamba-FCS (accuracy of 93.62%) is mainly due to the collaborative enhancement mechanism of frequency-domain channel attention (F module) and cross-layer interaction (C module).

The F module performs frequency-domain recalibration on features through the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) or discrete cosine transform (DCT), significantly enhancing the model’s sensitivity to high-frequency textures related to diseases. This design is consistent with recent research trends in frequency-domain visual representation. For example, Patro et al. [23] theoretically demonstrated in SpectFormer that frequency-domain representation can effectively capture periodic texture patterns that are difficult to perceive in the spatial domain, which is of great significance for identifying subtle patterns such as leaf vein fading and spot distribution in early diseases. In addition, Wang et al. [24] introduced a wavelet transform in WCG VMamba to reduce image noise and enhance discriminative features, further supporting the effectiveness of introducing frequency-domain priors in agricultural vision tasks. In the experiment of this study, the F module alone brought an accuracy improvement of nearly 3%, which strongly verified that frequency-domain analysis can help the model better distinguish disease features from interference information, such as lighting and background in real complex field scenes, and improve the discriminative power of features.

Field environments often suffer from leaf obstruction and uneven lighting, which can easily lead to visual ambiguity, such as mistaking early disease symptoms for healthy leaves. The C module effectively alleviates such problems by establishing the interaction between shallow detail features and deep semantic features. This is consistent with the research findings on multi-scale feature fusion. Rong et al. [25] found in improving CoatNet that relying solely on deep features meant losing key spatial location information, while fusing multi-scale features helps better distinguish disease categories with similar morphologies but different textures. The C module of this study utilizes a lightweight cross-attention mechanism to achieve efficient fusion of cross-layer features, enabling the model to simultaneously utilize shallow detail information (such as edges and fine textures) and high-level semantic context (such as the overall morphology of lesions), thereby improving its ability to distinguish visually similar categories, especially in distinguishing healthy leaves from early diseases, showing significant improvement.

In summary, the F module and C module are enhanced from the perspectives of feature representation and feature fusion, respectively. The two work together to enable VMamba-FCS to more comprehensively and robustly model disease visual patterns in complex field environments. This is an important mechanism for its significantly better performance than the basic VMamba and other comparative models.

4.3. Significant Feature Suppression and Anti-Interference Robustness

The introduction of the significant feature suppression (S) module aims to specifically alleviate the “shortcut learning” problem that deep learning models are prone to in agricultural small-sample data environments, where the model overly relies on local, significant but possibly non-essential features of the data for decision-making, thereby affecting its generalization ability in complex real-world scenarios.

Some current studies enhance the attention of models to key regions by increasing the feature weights of salient regions, such as Liu et al. [26] using the SimAM attention mechanism [27] in YOLO BSMamba to enhance the feature response of lesion regions. Different from this approach, the S module proposed in this work adopts an “adversarial suppression” strategy. This strategy does not reinforce salient regions but actively identifies and suppresses overactivated regions in the feature map during training, forcing the network to learn less salient but potentially more discriminative pathological features, such as halos around lesions and yellowing changes at leaf edges. This approach has inherent consistency with methods in the field of fine-grained recognition, such as TransFG [28], which guides the model to focus on diverse discriminative regions to obtain more comprehensive feature representations. The contribution of this work is to embed this idea for the first time in a lightweight, forward regularized manner into an SSM-based visual architecture.

The experimental results verified the effectiveness of the mechanism: after adding the S module, the recall rate of the model in early disease categories increased by 24 percentage points. This indicates that the model no longer relies solely on the most obvious lesion area for judgment but has learned to comprehensively utilize richer and more comprehensive pathological features of leaves for decision-making. Therefore, under non-ideal conditions such as uneven lighting, blade occlusion, or morphological variation, VMamba-FCS exhibits stronger robustness and generalization ability, effectively reducing misjudgments caused by overfitting of local features.

4.4. Computational Efficiency and Edge Deployment Potential

In the practical application of smart agriculture, the lightweighting and real-time reasoning ability of a model are the core factors that determine whether it can be deployed on the ground. Wang et al. [29] pointed out in LeafMamba that although some lightweight Transformer models (such as MobileViT [30]) reduce the number of parameters through structural design, the inherent quadratic computational complexity of their attention mechanism still restricts their real-time performance on IoT devices with limited computing resources.

The VMamba-FCS model proposed by our research institute performs outstandingly in terms of computational efficiency, with only 1.20 M parameters and an inference speed of 126.5 FPS in the experimental environment. This efficiency indicator is significantly better than those of current advanced lightweight models of the same type. Compared with the EfficientVMamba proposed by Pei et al. [31], VMamba-FCS further optimizes computational overhead and memory usage through a bottleneck MLP for frequency-domain attention, a switchable mechanism across layers, and a forward lightweight feature suppression module, while maintaining the global receptive field and feature richness of the model.

This efficient performance demonstrates that VMamba-FCS has the potential to be directly deployed on agricultural edge devices such as drones, handheld detectors, and embedded platforms like Jetson Nano. It can support real-time disease identification and monitoring tasks in field scenarios, reduce reliance on cloud computing, lower system latency and communication costs, and better meet the practical needs of modern agriculture for real-time response and on-site decision-making.

5. Conclusions

This study proposes a novel lightweight architecture VMamba-FCS based on a visual state-space model to address the practical challenges of tomato leaf disease recognition in real field environments. Through in-depth analysis of the limitations of the SSM in processing unstructured agricultural images, we innovatively integrated three collaborative enhancement mechanisms, frequency-domain channel attention, cross-layer cross-attention, and salient feature suppression, to construct an efficient and robust disease recognition model. The main conclusions of this article are as follows:

(1)

VMamba-FCS effectively addresses the shortcomings of the basic VMamba model, such as insufficient high-frequency texture perception, limited multi-scale feature fusion, and excessive reliance on local features. It achieves a recognition accuracy of 93.62% on the Tomato-Village real-world field dataset and significantly outperforms other models in the cross-dataset (PlantDoc) generalization test with an accuracy of 71.3%, demonstrating both high precision and strong generalization ability, thus setting a new performance benchmark for lightweight disease classification models.

(2)

The ablation experiment and feature visualization analysis of the system show that the frequency-domain attention mechanism significantly enhances the perception ability of subtle textures in early diseases. Cross-layer attention promotes efficient fusion of multi-scale semantics. The feature suppression mechanism effectively improves the robustness of the model under complex background interference. The synergy among the three constitutes the key to performance improvement.

(3)

The model only contains 1.20 M parameters and has an inference speed of up to 126.5 FPS in standard testing environments, achieving an excellent balance between accuracy and efficiency. It fully meets the strict requirements of precision agriculture for lightweight, low-power, and real-time edge devices and has good prospects for practical deployment in the field.

Although the current model performs well on RGB images, there is still room for further improvement. Future research work will mainly focus on two directions: first, exploring the introduction of multimodal data (such as hyperspectral, near-infrared, or environmental text information) to enhance the discriminative ability and adaptability of models in complex scenarios such as extreme lighting and severe occlusion; second, combining this classification model with efficient object detection frameworks (such as the YOLO series), expanding the recognition range from single-leaf to canopy scale, to achieve disease localization and monitoring in field environments, thereby promoting agricultural artificial intelligence technology from laboratory research to large-scale and practical field applications.
