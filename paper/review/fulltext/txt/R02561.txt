Abstract
Deep convolutional networks now classify leaf images on curated benchmarks such as PlantVillage with
accuracies close to the measurement ceiling of those datasets, which has shifted the open questions
away from raw accuracy toward two under-reported issues: which components of a composite pipeline
actually cause the result, and whether the components that matter on laboratory images are the same
ones that matter on field photographs. We address both with a classification framework evaluated under
a protocol that fixes every development decision before the independent test data are read. A Mask R-
CNN stage localizes the dominant leaf, the accepted box is expanded by a validation-selected 5% margin
and resized to a shared 224 by 224 input, and the same localized image is passed to ResNet50,
InceptionV3, and MobileNetV2 together with an extractor that produces eighteen colour and shape
descriptors. The forty-five class probabilities and eighteen descriptors form a sixty-three-dimensional
input to a neural meta-classifier developed by three repetitions of stratified five-fold cross-validation. On
a locked 3,101-image PlantVillage test partition of fifteen classes the framework reached 99.77%
accuracy and 99.75% macro F1 with seven misclassifications, and a one-component-at-a-time ablation
confirmed that every stage contributed. The same design was then trained and evaluated entirely within
a separate thirteen-class PlantDoc field-image dataset, where it reached 90.03% accuracy and 89.40%
macro F1. This is a within-PlantDoc experiment and not a controlled-to-field transfer test, so the figure
measures how the pipeline behaves on field imagery rather than how a PlantVillage-trained model
survives a domain shift. The central finding comes from running the identical ablation on both datasets:
on clean images the ensemble breadth and descriptors provide the incremental gains, but under field
conditions the ordering changes, and leaf localization and learned fusion become the decisive
components. Removing localization cost 2.83 accuracy points and replacing the neural fusion with soft
voting cost a further 2.08 points, the two largest effects on PlantDoc. The contribution is a controlled
and transparent account of where each component of a localization-aware plant disease classifier earns
its place, and of how that ordering shifts between laboratory and field acquisition.


1. Introduction
Plant diseases lower both the quantity and the quality of agricultural output, and their early recognition
remains one of the practical bottlenecks in crop management. Field diagnosis still depends heavily on
visual inspection by trained personnel, which is fast but subjective, or on laboratory confirmation, which
is reliable but slow and difficult to scale. Automated image-based diagnosis has been studied for more
than a decade as a way to narrow the gap between these two extremes, and the release of large
annotated collections such as PlantVillage turned the problem into a standard supervised recognition
task that deep convolutional networks handle well.

The consequence of that success is a saturation effect. On the PlantVillage-derived subsets that
dominate the literature, reported accuracies now cluster in a narrow band close to 99%, and differences
between competing methods often fall within the range of run-to-run variation. When several
architectures all report values near the ceiling, a further tenth of a percentage point on the same clean
                                                   Page 2/22
benchmark is weak evidence of a better method. Two limitations of this line of work matter more than
the headline number. The first is that many studies report a single accuracy without a controlled account
of which components of a composite pipeline actually caused the result. The second is that models
trained and tested on laboratory images with uniform backgrounds do not necessarily hold up on
photographs taken in the field, where background clutter, variable illumination, occlusion, and pose are
the rule rather than the exception.

Transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained convolutional networks became the default approach to
plant disease classification after early demonstrations that fine-tuned deep models outperform
handcrafted pipelines on PlantVillage [1, 2]. Comparative studies established that no single backbone is
uniformly best across crops and conditions, and that residual, inception, and lightweight mobile families
carry different inductive biases [3], which motivates ensembles that read several backbones rather than
committing to one. Recent ensemble studies confirm that combining complementary networks improves
accuracy over the best single model [4, 5], although many fuse features or average logits rather than
training an explicit meta-learner. Stacked generalization offers the alternative of training a second-level
model on the outputs of first-level learners [6, 7], and a neural meta-classifier can accept heterogeneous
inputs, which allows deep probabilities and handcrafted descriptors to be fused in one model.

Colour, shape, and texture descriptors were the standard representation before deep learning and retain
value as compact, interpretable signals. Vegetation indices such as Excess Green [8, 9] separate leaf
tissue from background, and recent work shows that pairing handcrafted measurements with deep
features can improve tomato leaf disease classification [10]. Restricting the classifier to the leaf region
reduces the influence of background. Mask R-CNN [11] supplies an instance-level region that can be
cropped and passed to a downstream classifier, as applied to apple leaf disease by Rehman et al. [12],
and lesion-level analysis has been used to the same end [13]. On clean laboratory images the benefit of
localization is modest because the background is already uniform, but its value is expected to grow on
field images where the background carries most of the nuisance variation.

A growing body of evidence shows that high accuracy on controlled datasets does not transfer cleanly to
field imagery. Xiang et al. [14] report that a PlantVillage-trained ResNet50 loses 67.7 accuracy points
when it is applied directly to PlantDoc, and that standard mitigation techniques recover only part of that
loss. PlantDoc was assembled to expose exactly this gap, since its images come from the open web
with natural backgrounds [15]. The present work does not attempt that direct transfer. Instead it trains
and evaluates the same pipeline design entirely within PlantDoc, and runs the same component ablation
on both datasets, so that the comparison is between which components matter on clean images and
which matter on field images, rather than between two absolute accuracies obtained under a domain
shift.

This paper addresses both limitations directly. We build a classification framework that combines leaf
localization, three heterogeneous convolutional backbones, an interpretable handcrafted branch,
probability calibration, and a neural meta-classifier, and we evaluate it under a protocol that separates

                                                  Page 3/22
every development decision from the independent test data. The framework is assessed in two settings.
The first is the standard fifteen-class PlantVillage subset, where the question is how far the complete
design can be pushed and how much each stage contributes on clean images. The second is a thirteen-
class experiment trained and evaluated entirely within the PlantDoc field-image dataset, where the same
components are examined under natural acquisition conditions rather than transferred from PlantVillage.
Running the same ablation logic on both datasets makes it possible to state not just the final accuracy
but where each component earns its place. The present study concerns the classification formulation of
the problem; an object-detection formulation of tomato leaf disease diagnosis was addressed separately
by the authors [16].

The specific contributions are as follows.

A localization-aware classification pipeline that supplies one validation-selected leaf-focused input to all
deep and handcrafted branches, so that background variation is reduced before any feature is
computed. The 5% crop margin and the 224 by 224 shared input size were chosen on validation data
alone.

A sixty-three-input neural meta-classifier that fuses forty-five deep probabilities with eighteen colour and
shape descriptors, developed through repeated stratified out-of-fold cross-validation with fold-internal
feature scaling and evaluated once on a locked 3,101-image test partition.

A controlled component study on both datasets. On PlantVillage the study removes calibration, the
handcrafted branch, and each backbone in turn. On PlantDoc it also removes the localization stage and
replaces the neural fusion with soft voting, which lets the roles of localization and learned fusion be
measured under field conditions.

A transparent comparison with recent work on the same subset that separates studies sharing the
present locked-test protocol from studies whose evaluation design differs, so that raw accuracy is not
treated as a single universal ranking.


2. Materials and Methods
The framework is a four-stage pipeline, shown in Fig. 1. A Mask R-CNN stage localizes the dominant leaf,
the accepted box is expanded by a validation-selected margin and resized to a shared input, and the
same localized image is passed to three convolutional backbones and to a handcrafted descriptor
extractor. The forty-five deep probabilities and eighteen descriptors are concatenated into a sixty-three-
dimensional vector and passed to a neural meta-classifier that returns the final class probabilities. Each
stage is described in turn below, after the datasets and the fixed data split.

2.2 Fixed data split and balanced training set
The 20,638 classification images were divided once with a stratified 70:15:15 ratio into 14,440 training,
3,097 validation, and 3,101 test images. The validation partition was then split further into 1,548
validation-selection images and 1,549 validation-meta images. The first subset was reserved for crop-
padding selection, image-size selection, and backbone checkpoint selection. The second supported
repeated out-of-fold development of the meta-classifier. The 3,101-image test partition was not read until
every choice had been fixed.

Because the classes are unbalanced, a balanced training manifest of 2,000 effective entries per class
was constructed. Classes below the target kept their original images and received deterministic
augmented replicas, and the single class above the target was reduced without replacement. The final
manifest contained 14,195 original entries and 15,805 augmented replicas, giving 30,000 effective
training entries drawn from 14,195 unique source images. Augmentation was applied to the training
manifest only, so no augmented view of a validation or test image ever entered training.
2.3 Stage I: Mask R-CNN leaf localization
Mask R-CNN [11] localizes the dominant leaf in each image. At a fixed acceptance confidence of 0.50
the localizer returned a box for all 20,638 classification images, so the full-image fallback rule was never
triggered. Accepted-box confidences concentrated near one, with a mean of 0.99992 and a minimum
accepted score of 0.56120. The selected box covered on average 72.26% of the original image area, with
the middle half of images between 63.63% and 83.29% coverage, which indicates that localization
removed peripheral background while retaining most of the visible leaf (Fig. 2). The accepted box was


                                                  Page 5/22
expanded by the validation-selected 5% margin and resized to the shared input size before any feature
was computed. The instance mask itself is not used by any later stage.

2.4 Selection of crop margin and shared input size
The crop margin and the shared input size were both chosen on the validation-selection subset alone.
Four margins were screened at a common 128 by 128 input. Accuracy was identical at 0% and 5%
margin, but macro F1 rose from 91.748% to 92.586% with the 5% margin, and larger margins reduced
both measures, so 5% was retained. With the margin fixed, three input sizes were compared. Ensemble
macro F1 rose from 92.586% at 128 pixels to 94.220% at 160 pixels and 96.665% at 224 pixels, and
accuracy followed the same pattern to 97.222% at 224 pixels, so all branches used 224 by 224 localized
images. Because these decisions used only validation data, the locked test result remains
uncontaminated by hyperparameter search.

2.5 Stage II: convolutional probability branches
Three ImageNet-pretrained backbones were fine-tuned independently on the 30,000-entry balanced
manifest: ResNet50 [20], InceptionV3 [21], and MobileNetV2 [22]. The three were chosen because they
occupy different points on the accuracy-cost curve and carry different architectural priors, a residual
design, a multi-branch inception design, and an inverted-residual lightweight design, which is the
property an ensemble exploits. Each backbone received a task head of global average pooling, dropout
of 0.30, and a fifteen-unit softmax. Training used a three-epoch frozen warm-up at learning rate 1e-3
followed by full fine-tuning for up to twelve epochs at 1e-5, with AdamW [23] and weight decay 1e-4. The
best checkpoint was selected by validation macro F1, and validation loss governed early stopping and
learning-rate reduction. Each backbone outputs a fifteen-class probability vector, so the three together
contribute forty-five probability features.

2.6 Stage III: handcrafted colour and shape descriptors
The handcrafted branch operated on the same localized 224 by 224 image (Fig. 3). Fourteen colour
descriptors were formed from the means and standard deviations of the R, G, B, H, S, V channels and the
Excess Green vegetation index. Otsu thresholding [24] was applied to the Excess Green response to form
a foreground mask, and the largest external contour was retained, from which solidity, circularity, aspect
ratio, and area ratio were computed as four shape descriptors. The mask is an intermediate
measurement rather than a learned segmentation output. Table 2 lists the eighteen-dimensional
composition.

2.7 Stage IV: neural meta-classifier
The forty-five deep probabilities and eighteen handcrafted descriptors were concatenated into a sixty-
three-dimensional vector and passed to a compact neural meta-classifier, shown in Fig. 4. The network
used a first block of 64 units with ReLU, batch normalization, and dropout 0.20, a second block of 32
units with ReLU, batch normalization, and dropout 0.10, and a fifteen-unit softmax output. Optimization
used AdamW at learning rate 1e-3 with weight decay 1e-4. Development followed three repetitions of
stratified five-fold cross-validation, giving fifteen out-of-fold rounds, and fold-specific feature scaling was
fitted on each training portion only. After the architecture and settings were fixed, the system was
evaluated once on the locked 3,101-image test partition. This out-of-fold protocol prevents the meta-
classifier from ever fitting to predictions associated with the test images. Table 3 summarizes the
settings.

2.8 Ablation design and evaluation protocol

                                                      Page 7/22
The contribution of each stage was measured with a one-component-at-a-time ablation. On PlantVillage
the study removed probability calibration, the handcrafted branch, and each backbone in turn, retraining
each reduced variant under the same protocol and evaluating it on the same locked test partition, so that
any difference reflects only the removed component. On PlantDoc the same logic was extended to
remove the Mask R-CNN localization stage and to replace the neural fusion with soft voting, and the
three backbones were also evaluated individually. The reporting used aggregate accuracy and balanced
accuracy together with macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1, because macro measures reveal
whether the aggregate is carried by a single large class. The PlantDoc analysis was developed and
evaluated within its own protocol rather than by transferring the PlantVillage-trained model, so it is a
within-PlantDoc evaluation and not a direct cross-dataset transfer experiment.


3. Results
3.1 Individual backbone performance on PlantVillage
The three backbones converged quickly during fine-tuning and reached similar validation accuracies
(Fig. 5). On the independent test partition ResNet50 was the strongest single model at 98.613% accuracy
and 98.583% macro F1 with 43 errors. MobileNetV2 and InceptionV3 followed at 97.872% and 97.646%
accuracy with 66 and 73 errors. MobileNetV2 reached this level with 2.28 million parameters, roughly
one tenth of the count of the two heavier networks, which supports its inclusion as an efficient
complementary branch even though it is not strong enough to carry the task alone. Table 4 reports the
individual results.

3.2 Full meta-fusion model on PlantVillage
The complete sixty-three-input meta-classifier reached 99.77% accuracy on the locked test partition,
correctly classifying 3,094 of 3,101 images with seven errors. Balanced accuracy was 99.72%, macro
precision 99.79%, macro recall 99.72%, macro F1 99.75%, and weighted F1 99.77%. The close agreement
between overall accuracy and weighted F1 indicates that the result was not driven by a single large
class, and the macro measures confirm consistent performance across the fifteen categories. The
fusion improved on the best single backbone by more than one accuracy point, which reduced the error
count from 43 to 7. Table 5 reports the aggregate measures.

3.3 Class-wise behaviour
The class-wise report showed near-ceiling performance across all fifteen categories. Several classes
were classified without error, including pepper healthy, potato healthy, tomato mosaic virus, tomato
yellow leaf curl virus, and tomato healthy. The smallest class, potato healthy with 23 test images,
retained perfect classification despite its limited support. The seven residual errors were isolated rather
than concentrated in a single category, and each class-level F1 remained close to the upper end of the
scale, which is consistent with the macro F1 of 99.75%. The confusion analysis in Figures 6 and 7
confirmed that off-diagonal mass was sparse and confined to a few visually similar disease pairs, with
no broad systematic failure.



3.4 PlantVillage ablation study
Every removed component reduced accuracy and macro F1, which supports the complete design over
any single stage. Among the backbones, removing ResNet50 caused the largest decline, 0.38 accuracy
points and 0.40 macro F1 points, consistent with its status as the strongest individual model. Removing
InceptionV3 and MobileNetV2 caused smaller but consistent drops, which indicates that the three
backbones carry complementary probability information rather than redundant signals. Removing the
handcrafted branch cost 0.16 accuracy points, direct evidence of a modest but measurable contribution
from the explicit colour and shape representation. Calibration produced the smallest single gain. These
magnitudes are small in absolute terms: on a 3,101-image test set a difference of 0.10 accuracy points
corresponds to roughly three images, and the full ablation spans about twelve images between the
strongest and the weakest reduced variant. Because each configuration was trained under a single seed,
the individual gaps on PlantVillage should be read as a consistent ranking of contributions rather than as

precisely estimated effect sizes; the ranking is stable and every component helps, but the exact point

3.5 Comparison with recent studies on the same subset
To position the result, the closest-protocol comparison was restricted to peer-reviewed studies that used
the same fifteen-class PlantVillage subset of 20,638 images. Under this restriction the framework
reached 99.77% accuracy, above the 99.64% reported by Hasani et al. [25] and the 98.41% reported by
Tiwari et al. [26]. These differences are small and should be read with care, because architecture,
checkpoint selection, augmentation, repeated-run reporting, and test construction all differ across
studies. The comparison is deliberately restricted to studies that share the same fifteen-class subset
and a comparable held-out test protocol, so that the numbers are placed on a common footing rather
than ranked across incompatible designs. Several other works on the same subset used materially
different designs, including two 80/20 splits [5, 27], a validation accuracy reported in place of a test
accuracy [28], federated client evaluation [29], and external validation after training on a different dataset
[30], so their reported values are treated as contextual benchmarks rather than as a direct ranking. What
distinguishes the present evaluation is the locked 3,101-image test partition, the complete class-wise
reporting, and the two-dataset ablation, all of which make the reported figures traceable to a stated

3.6 Within-PlantDoc field-image evaluation
The controlled benchmark measures how far the design can be pushed, but it cannot show whether the
same design survives field acquisition. To test this, the framework was evaluated within a separate
thirteen-class PlantDoc experiment on 1,097 field images covering two pepper classes, two potato
classes, and nine tomato classes. Unlike the uniform PlantVillage images, these photographs carry
cluttered natural backgrounds, variable illumination, occlusion, scale changes, and heterogeneous leaf
presentation, and their class distribution is highly uneven, ranging from 2 images for tomato spider mites
to 150 for tomato Septoria leaf spot. Fig. 8 shows one representative image from each of the thirteen
shared classes and makes the domain difference visible. The PlantDoc images were never merged with
PlantVillage training data, so the experiment is a within-PlantDoc evaluation rather than a direct
PlantVillage-to-PlantDoc transfer. The extreme imbalance should be kept in mind when reading the
macro measures on this dataset: the smallest classes are supported by only a handful of test images, so
a single prediction can move a per-class F1 by a large amount, and the PlantDoc macro figures carry
correspondingly wide uncertainty.

The complete model reached 90.03% accuracy and 89.40% macro F1 on this field material. The absolute
accuracy is about ten points below the PlantVillage level, but the two numbers are not directly
comparable, because the PlantDoc model was trained on PlantDoc rather than transferred from
PlantVillage. The gap therefore reflects the intrinsic difficulty of field images under a matched training
regime, and it is much smaller than the collapse reported for direct controlled-to-field transfer, where
Xiang et al. [14] measured a 67.7-point loss for a PlantVillage-trained ResNet50 applied to PlantDoc. The
within-PlantDoc figure and the transfer figure answer different questions and should not be read on the
same scale. The neural fusion again improved on every individual backbone, exceeding ResNet50,
InceptionV3, and MobileNetV2 by 2.19, 3.08, and 3.72 accuracy points, a wider margin than on the clean
benchmark, which is the first sign that fusion contributes more when the inputs are noisier. Table 8
reports the individual and fused accuracies.

4. Discussion
The two evaluations answer different questions. On PlantVillage the framework reaches 99.77%
accuracy with seven errors, which places it among the strongest reported results on that subset while
remaining fully traceable to a locked protocol. Because the whole field operates near the ceiling of this
benchmark, the more informative outcome is the ablation, which shows a consistent ordering in which
ResNet50 contributes most among the backbones and the handcrafted branch and calibration add
smaller but real gains. The value of the clean-benchmark result is therefore as much in the controlled
attribution as in the accuracy figure.

The PlantDoc result is where the design earns its structure. Accuracy falls to 90.03%, and the ablation
reorders the components so that leaf localization and learned fusion become the two largest
contributors. This is the expected consequence of moving from uniform laboratory backgrounds to field

                                                    Page 12/22
photographs. When the background is clean, localization removes little useful nuisance and the
ensemble does most of the work. When the background is cluttered, localization removes the dominant
source of variation before any feature is computed, and a learned meta-classifier is better than a fixed
average at discounting a branch that has been misled by the scene. The same reordering is what the
cross-domain literature would predict, since the field setting is where background and acquisition, rather
than the choice of backbone, dominate the error [14].

Several limitations bound these claims. The test partitions are small in absolute terms, 3,101 images for
the clean benchmark and 1,097 for the field experiment, so on PlantVillage a difference below roughly
one third of a percentage point corresponds to only a handful of images and is reported as part of a
consistent ranking rather than as a precisely estimated effect. Each configuration was trained under a
single random seed, so the exact ablation magnitudes are not accompanied by run-to-run variance; the
ordering of the components is stable across the two datasets, and it is the ordering, not the third-decimal
value, that carries the argument. The PlantDoc analysis was trained and evaluated entirely within
PlantDoc, so it characterizes how the pipeline behaves on field images but does not establish direct
transfer of the PlantVillage-trained model to a new domain; the collapse measured for that direct transfer
by Xiang et al. [14] is far larger than any gap reported here and marks the separate problem that this
study does not attempt to solve. Efficiency is discussed only through parameter counts, and no latency,
memory, or energy measurement on target hardware was carried out, so no real-time claim is made.
Finally, the datasets are public and the class definitions were taken from their providers, which fixes the
label space and its imperfections.


5. Conclusion
This paper presented a localization-aware classification framework that combines Mask R-CNN leaf
localization, three heterogeneous convolutional backbones, an eighteen-descriptor handcrafted branch,
probability calibration, and a sixty-three-input neural meta-classifier, and evaluated it under a protocol
that fixes every decision before the independent test data are read. On the fifteen-class PlantVillage
subset the framework reached 99.77% accuracy and 99.75% macro F1 with seven errors, and a
controlled ablation confirmed that every stage contributed, with ResNet50 the largest among the
backbones. On a separate thirteen-class PlantDoc field-image experiment the same design reached
90.03% accuracy, and its ablation showed that leaf localization and learned fusion, rather than the
ensemble breadth or the descriptors, are the components that hold performance together under natural
acquisition conditions. The practical message is that the parts of a composite pipeline that matter most
depend on the operating conditions, and that a field-image ablation is needed to see this. Future work
will pursue a direct cross-domain transfer evaluation from controlled to field data, a deployment
benchmark that measures latency and memory on target hardware, and an extension of the framework
to additional crops and to a larger and more diverse field dataset.
