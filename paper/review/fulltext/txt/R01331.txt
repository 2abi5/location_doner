19                                                                    ABSTRACT


20              Southern leaf blight (SLB) is a foliar disease of maize (Zea mays L.) caused by the

21    necrotrophic fungal pathogen Cochliobolus heterostrophus. Genetic resistance is the most

22    effective control method for SLB. Developing disease resistant maize lines requires field trials

23    during which disease phenotypes must be visually assessed. Remote sensing using drones is an

24    emerging technology that can be leveraged for high-throughput phenotyping of disease severity

25    that is otherwise labor-intensive and subjective. This project used a deep learning approach to

26    estimate SLB disease severity of single-row maize plots from drone imagery. Over 26,000 plot-

27    level images produced from flights conducted across three growing seasons were labeled with in-

28    field visual scores taken contemporaneously by expert raters. Variation in environmental

29    conditions contributed to a labeled image dataset that reflects the complexity of agronomic field

30    experiments. We assessed the ability of nine deep learning models from three architectural

31    families to estimate disease severity. The best-performing model, EVA-02-B, achieved strong

32    cross year generalization (R2 = 0.697). Error analysis found that performance was more strongly

33    associated with seasonal disease progression and flight-score time offset than with image-level

34    noise. UAV-based deep learning estimated SLB severity with comparable precision to expert

35    raters. This study lays the groundwork for integrating automated phenotypes into genetic studies

36    of disease resistance.

58                                                              1 INTRODUCTION


59              Maize (Zea mays L.) has the highest production by volume of any staple cereal with

60    extremely versatile uses as feed, food, and biofuel (Erenstein et al., 2022). Diseases pose a major

61    threat to maize production by reducing yield, producing toxins that limit grain use, and

62    increasing management costs (Betts et al., 2025). The development of disease resistant maize
   bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.03.729887; this version posted June 6, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
 (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                               available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




63    varieties offers a reliable and cost-effective solution for controlling diseases and lowering

64    production inputs in economically significant plant pathosystems (Nelson et al., 2018; Wisser et

65    al., 2006). Quantitative disease resistance (QDR) is a preferred management strategy because of

66    its durability, non-race-specific effects, and efficacy against necrotrophic pathogens (Poland et

67    al., 2009; Yang et al., 2017a). However, the moderate effect of multiple genetic loci and strong

68    environmental influence complicate genetic dissection and integration of QDR into elite maize

69    hybrids (Nelson et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2017a). Breeding for QDR requires substantial effort

70    toward in-field disease severity assessments, imposing limits to the number of genotypes,

71    locations, and developmental stages that can be observed. Visual scores are imperfect labels that

72    include both biological signal and rater variation, reducing phenotypic precision and affecting

73    downstream estimates of resistance (Poland & Nelson, 2011). These barriers can be addressed

74    through the development of automated in-field methods for maize field trials that offer higher

75    throughput and greater consistency.


76              Southern leaf blight (SLB) is a foliar disease in maize caused by the necrotrophic fungal

77    pathogen Cochliobolus heterostrophus. SLB is infamous in the United States for the 1970-1971

78    epidemic when U.S. corn yields suffered an estimated 16% loss due to the widespread adoption

79    of a gene that was key to hybrid seed production at the time and, unfortunately, also a source of

80    susceptibility to a novel race of C. heterostophus (Balint-Kurti & Pataky, 2024). SLB infection

81    causes the formation of necrotic lesions beginning on the lower leaves that have variable shape,

82    size, and color influenced by genetic backgrounds among inbreds and hybrids (Munkvold &

83    White, 2016). Resistance to SLB is largely quantitative and relies on a variety of mechanisms

84    (Belcher et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2023; Holley, 1989; Kump et al., 2011; Lim & Hooker, 1976;

85    Yang et al., 2017b). While resistant germplasm has significantly reduced yield loss in the United
    bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.03.729887; this version posted June 6, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
  (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

 86    States, SLB continues to be a significant constraint to maize production in tropical and

 87    subtropical regions (Munkvold & White, 2016).


 88              Molecular and computational innovations continuously improve the accessibility and

 89    affordability of genetic data collection and analysis. However, the implementation of these

 90    powerful methodologies in genetic mapping studies and breeding programs is impeded by the

 91    quality and quantity of the phenotypic data and availability of suitable mapping populations

 92    (Araus & Cairns, 2014; Gage et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2020). Several studies

 93    have successfully identified quantitative trait loci (QTL) associated with SLB resistance using

 94    phenotypes derived from in-field visual scores recorded multiple times per growing season

 95    (Balint-Kurti et al., 2007; Bian et al., 2014; Carson et al., 2004; Chen et al., 2023; Kump et al.,

 96    2011; Li et al., 2018; Martins et al., 2019; Negeri et al., 2011; Yang et al., 2017b; Zwonitzer et

 97    al., 2009). This current standard of visual scoring for SLB severity has limited throughput,

 98    precision, and accuracy due to the inherent qualities of the in-field severity measurements (Bock

 99    et al., 2020). Improving SLB severity phenotyping has the potential to increase the statistical

100    power and reproducibility of genetic mapping studies for QDR and provide the throughput

101    needed by modern breeding programs (Gage et al., 2018; Poland & Nelson, 2011).


102               Recent advancements in sensor technology and artificial intelligence (AI) enable the

103    development of high-throughput phenotyping (HTP) methods, offering a non-destructive,

104    scalable and automated alternative to traditional assessment with visual scoring (Wang et al.,

105    2026). While HTP of disease severity is achievable with remote sensing, it often necessitates

106    time-consuming image evaluation or extensive validation (Bock et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2016;

107    Wu et al., 2019). An increasingly popular HTP modality is imagery captured from unmanned
    bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.03.729887; this version posted June 6, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint

108    aerial vehicles (UAVs) mounted with red-green-blue (RGB) cameras. Feature engineering to

109    extract spectral or textural information from UAV images has been widely adopted, providing

110    data that can be directly used by classical machine learning models. However, this approach

111    overlooks key spatial patterns and suffers from environmental interference. Deep learning (DL),

112    a branch of machine learning inspired by biological neural networks, can learn spatial patterns

113    directly and avoid hand-crafted features with models specialized for image analysis such as

114    convolutional neural networks (CNN) and vision transformers (ViT). This compliments the high-

115    quality RGB image data captured from UAV platforms and facilitates straightforward validation

116    of automated disease severity assessment with disease signals observable to the human eye.


117              Currently, the use of AI in plant pathology has largely been limited by the availability of

118    sizable and accurately labeled datasets (Jeger et al., 2024). Previous studies that used deep

119    learning to assess maize foliar disease from RGB imagery have focused on object detection or

120    pixel segmentation, typically at the leaf level (Craze et al., 2022; DeChant et al., 2017; Green et

121    al., 2012; Haque et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2022; Stewart et al., 2019; Wiesner-

122    Hanks et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2022). Other limitations of current methods

123    include low-throughput handheld imaging devices, destructive leaf sampling, lesion annotations,

124    single leaf or plant targets, and uncoupling from field observations. In this study, we built a

125    three-year dataset of over 26,000 plot-level images labeled with in-field visual scores provided

126    by expert raters. We evaluated the capabilities of nine pre-trained supervised deep learning

127    models across three architecture families (CNN, ViT, hybrid) to estimate SLB severity from our

128    dataset with an agronomically relevant cross-year test evaluation to mirror real-world estimation

129    of unlabeled fields in unseen environments. Comparing visual scores from multiple expert raters

130    to imaged-based scores revealed similar performance between the visual and image-based

136                                               2 METHODS AND MATERIALS


137                                           2.1 Experimental Design and Field Assessment


138              2.1.1 Field Design and Inoculation


139              All data were collected in fields at the Central Crops Research Station (CCRS) in

140    Clayton, North Carolina during the 2023, 2024, and 2025 growing seasons. Two separate fields

141    within the CCRS were planted each year with 2.43 meter (8 foot) single-row plots with 0.97

142    meter (38 inch) row spacing. All blocks were planted with a single row of border plots and check

143    lines. Fields were inoculated with isolates 2-16Bm and Hm540 inoculum of Cochliobolus

144    heterostrophus, the causal agent of SLB (Carson, 1998). The preparation of fungal inocula and

145    field inoculation were as described in Sermons and Balint-Kurti (Sermons & Balint-Kurti, 2018).


146              2.1.2 Visual Scoring

148    as the ‘ground truth’ values. Scores from a total of 29 dates were used, averaging roughly 10 per

161                                                          2.2 RGB SENSOR DATA


162              2.2.1 Image Data Collection


163              A DJI Matrice 300 RTK quadcopter and the DJI Zenmuse P1 35 mm full-frame sensor

164    (SZ DJI Technology Co. Ltd.) collected all images. Sensors were selected to maximize

165    resolution, improving the quality of processing and analysis further down the workflow. Flight

166    missions were planned on the DJI Pilot 2 application and were conducted with the following

167    parameters: 80% forward overlap, 80% side overlap, 12 meters (40 feet) of elevation, flight

168    speed of 5.6 kilometers per hour (3.5 miles per hour), and a shutter interval of 1.0 second. These

169    parameters were chosen to balance high ground sampling distance with low flight disturbances

170    (changes in lighting conditions, battery changes). The elevation was the lowest setting allowable

171    in the DJI Pilot 2 application and propeller downdrafts were shown to not significantly affect

172    canopy movement (Tang et al., 2023). Flights were conducted post-anthesis when plants had

173    reached terminal growth.

204                                                    2.3 Model training and evaluation


205              2.3.1 Model selection


206              Three families of pre-trained deep learning model architectures were evaluated: vision

207    transforms (ViT), convolutional neural networks (CNN), and hybrids (CNN + ViT). Choices

208    were based on image analysis performance described in the model’s source publication. The

209    three CNNs evaluated were EfficientNet V2-S (Tan & Le, 2021) and two sizes of ConvNeXt V2-

210    B and V2-L (Woo et al., 2023). We evaluated three ViTs: two sizes of DIVOv2 ViT-B/14 and

211    ViT-S/14 (Oquab et al., 2024), and EVA-02-B (Fang et al., 2024a). The hybrid models we

212    evaluated were MaxViT-S (Tu et al., 2022), SwinV2-B (Liu et al., 2022) and CoAtNet2 (Dai et

213    al., 2021). Hyperparameters were manually tuned based on the published findings from each

214    architecture, the nature of our dataset, and available computing capabilities rather than via grid

215    search or other optimization methods.

242    2.3.3 Sources of error


243              The circumstances surrounding in-field image collection and visual scoring create diverse

244    sources of error. We categorized sources of error into three levels based on whether they impact

245    an entire flight, a particular image, or an assigned visual score. Flight-level sources of error were

246    conditions affecting spatial and temporal attributes that were maintained throughout the duration

247    of each flight. The image-level sources of error consisted of unique characteristics dependent on

248    location with a field and genotype, making them variable within a given flight and thus measured

249    on a per-image basis. Finally, visual scores themselves were also considered for their

250    contribution to model error due to the tradeoff between dataset size and label noise. The code for

251    organizing and extracting these datasets features can be accessed at

252    https://github.com/gagelab/uav4slb.git (in process; to be finalized before publication).


253              We explored three types of flight level sources of error: lighting conditions, solar

254    geometry, and crop or disease progression at the time of imaging. Using one-minute radiation

255    observations measured in watts per meter squared (W/m2) by the North Carolina Environment

256    and Climate Observing Network station (ECONet) at CCRS (35.66974°N, 78.4926°W), we

257    calculated the irradiance mean and coefficient of variance over the course of all flights (Cardinal

264              For image-level error quantification, we estimated weed pressure, shadows, brightness,

265    and contrast. To measure per-image weed quantity, we classified all pixels as weeds using a

266    canopy height model to calculate the proportion of each image that was ground (height = 0 feet),

267    maize canopy (height > 2.46 feet [0.75 meters]), and weeds (0 < height ≤ 2.46 feet [0.75

268    meters]). For per-image shadow fraction, image pixels were converted from the RGB (Red -

269    green - blue) to HSV (Hue - saturation - value) color space and classified using a qualitatively

270    derived V-channel (brightness) threshold, normalized to the 95% percentile of whole-image

271    value. Mean image brightness was calculated for each image by averaging the V-channel for all

272    pixels in the HSV color space to estimate the effect of overall light intensity on a per image

273    basis. Contrast root mean square (RMS) was calculated as the standard deviation of pixel

274    intensity after converting images to grayscale to capture the variation of perceptually weighted

275    luminance.

282                                                       3 RESULTS & DISCUSSION


283                                                        3.1 Dataset Characterization

284               3.1.1 Image Count Distributions


285               The visual scores remaining after filtering corresponded to 26,071 plot images and

286    covered the full one-to-nine range of SLB severity (Figure 2A). Interestingly, the proportion of

287    whole integer scores used was 63.6% while only 36.4% of the scores assigned were half-integer

288    values (Figure 2A). We fit a kernel density to the visual scores to provide a corrected, non-

289    uniform expected count for each of the 17 scores that accurately reflects the underlying

290    distribution of observed severity. Integration of the kernel density estimate with ±0.25 windows

291    lead to a ~50% proportion of half to whole integer scores for any smooth distribution over the

292    one-to-nine SLB scoring scale. The observed proportion of half-integer scores fell well below the

293    expected proportion and a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test confirmed systematic departure across

294    all 17 values (χ²(16) = 1,915.44, p < 0.001). This is consistent with the tendency for raters to

295    assign the scores pictured in the rating protocol, a behavior known as “knotting” (Bock et al.,

296    2008; Schwanck & Del Ponte, 2014; Sherwood, 1983). This phenomenon can be considered a

297    tradeoff between ease of use and score resolution. The familiarity granted by a visual guide such

298    as Figure 1 increases the convenience and perceived speed of rating (Madden et al., 2017). On

299    the other hand, the challenge of interpolation limits scorers from assigning intermediate values

300    (Large, 1966). These findings motivate image-based automation as a route to regain resolution in

301    disease severity assessment.

318                                              3.2 Model Performance Evaluation


319    3.2.1 Year-To-Year Generalizability


320              One of this study’s objectives was to evaluate a variety of deep learning models for

321    applicability in prediction problems pertinent to maize genetics and breeding programs. Nine

322    models were selected from three architecture families based on ability to predict from fine-scale

323    image features. Our model selection process was rooted in the biology of the trait of interest,

324    aiming to stimulate the discernment of disease characteristics without explicit annotation. The

325    systematic differences between each year’s imaged experiments influence both this biology and

326    perception of disease severity through variation in conditions such as soil type, weather

327    conditions, germplasm, and weed pressure (Ullstrup, 1972). The actual feasibility of an image-

328    based pipeline to alleviate the labor requirements of SLB scoring necessitates performance

329    evaluation on field experiments that were not used during model training.
    bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.03.729887; this version posted June 6, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
  (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

330              We implemented a 3-fold cross-year evaluation test by allocating two years of plot

331    images and visual scores for model training and one year for model testing (Table 1). Such

332    evaluation simulates a major barrier to the deployment of image-based agronomic models:

333    certainty that test scores are derived from features relevant to the target rather than those distinct

334    to a particular environment (Jarquín et al., 2017). For each fold, models were trained on two

335    years then used to estimate disease severity in plot images from the target left-out year to test

336    year-to-year generalizability. By isolating the left-out year’s unique growing and scoring

337    conditions from the training data, we aimed to reduce inflation of model test performance due to

338    shared conditions in both the training and testing data. Additionally, this approach emulates

339    domain generalization, a technique that improves performance reliability on test data by training

340    on multiple source distributions (Hu et al., 2025). Models with high test performance are

341    assumed to have learned the image features relevant to disease severity despite the different

342    growing environments presented during training versus testing.


343              We tested nine different deep learning models, each of which had distinct combinations

344    of architecture and pre-training datasets. We selected R2, MAE, and RMSE as performance

345    metrics and averaged these across all three folds during model comparison. Across all metrics,

346    EVA-02-B, a pre-trained vision transformer with a DINOv2 backbone, achieved the highest

347    performance (Fang et al., 2024b) (Figure 3A-C). While EVA-02-B achieved only the third

348    highest R2 when 2023 was withheld as a test set, improved performance on the 2024 and 2025

349    test sets compensated for this deficiency (Figure S6). One possible contribution to lower

350    performance on the 2023 test set was the unbalanced nature of the dataset as described above.

351    The number of images in the 2023 test set (2,601) was 5,689 fewer images than 2024 and 12,579

352    fewer than 2025. With less image feature variation captured in a smaller test set, the complexity

388              3.2.2 Comparison of rater-to-rater and model-to-rater correlations


389              Variability between expert human raters scoring the same plots under identical conditions

390    can lead to discrepancies in the identification of small effect QTL (Poland & Nelson, 2011). In

391    our SLB visual scoring protocol, one rater is encouraged to collect all ratings for a particular

392    study which improves consistency within an experiment. Where visual scoring may be lacking is

393    reproducibility across multiple experiments. The plot-level images and scores used in training

394    and testing were taken from multiple experiments, resulting in visual scores from four different

395    raters (listed in the Acknowledgements section). In this way, we aimed to develop a model that

396    can address this drawback of visual scoring by uniformly estimating severity across multiple

397    experiments with an automated approach.


398              To test this, we had the four raters from model training plus one additional rater score

399    SLB severity for 100 plots on the same day, immediately followed by image collection to

400    generate image-based test scores that we used to evaluate whether the model agrees with raters

401    as well as raters agree with each other. A pairwise Pearson r was calculated for all rater-rater

402    combinations (n = 10) to estimate rater-to-rater variability. We then calculated pairwise Pearson r

403    for all model-rater combinations (n = 5) to assess model-to-rater variability, essentially treating

404    the model as a sixth rater. The mean of rater-to-rater correlations was 0.80, ranging from 0.69 to

405    0.86 (Figure 4). The mean of the model-to-rater correlations was 0.75, ranging from 0.70 to

406    0.80. The model-to-rater correlations were on average lower than the rater-to-rater correlations

407    by 0.05. The greater range in rater-to-rater correlations (0.17) than model-to-rater correlations

408    (0.10) indicated greater variability from visual scoring. Raters who assigned similar versus

409    dissimilar scores have the correlations at the higher and lower ends of the range. This was driven
    bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.03.729887; this version posted June 6, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
  (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

410    by rater bias in the visual scores, indicating that model derived scores may offer comparable

423    3.3.1 Flight conditions explain model error


424              Variations in lighting conditions, solar geometry, and time of year represent powerful

425    domain shifts influencing deep learning prediction outcomes in agriculture imagery

426    (Chakhvashvili et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025; Spisak, 2022; Valencia-Ortiz et al., 2021; Zhu et al.,

427    2024). These features introduce inconsistencies that are intrinsic components of passive remote

428    sensing, adding complexity to machine learning tasks. We evaluated the robustness of EVA-02-

429    B to known and hypothesized sources of error from flight-level conditions by computing

430    Spearman correlations () to characterize associations to EVA-02-B per flight MAE;  = 0.05

431    was used as a significance threshold given the small sample size (n = 27 flights) and exploratory

432    nature of the analysis (Figure 5). Values mentioned in this section are shown with higher

433    precision in Table S2, along with the mean and standard deviation of each flight characteristic.

434    This analysis dissected the strengths and limitations of our approach, providing insights on both

435    the applicability of current procedures as well as targets for potential improvement.


436              Flight-level lighting conditions are a core consideration for agriculture remote sensing as

437    light intensity and variance have been shown to influence highly informative spectral and

438    textural features of collected images (Bongomin et al., 2024; Chopin et al., 2018). We calculated

476    3.3.2 Model robustness to image noise


477              In remote sensing data, background complexity, illumination variation, and shadow

478    effects contribute noise to each image. We randomly chose the high-error examples from the

479    fourth error quantile (greater than the 75th percentile) for qualitative analysis (Figure 7). We

480    asserted that weeds, shadows, contrast, and brightness may be sources of noise contributing to

481    errors. While there are instances when EVA-02-B made accurate predictions despite high levels

482    of noise, there were consistencies amongst those images with high prediction error that we used

483    to justify further analysis. The amount of weed pixels varied spatially and temporally by

484    timepoint and field while shadows, contrast, and brightness varied within each flight (Figure

485    S4). We aimed to evaluate measurements of these sources of image noise to determine their

486    relationship to per-image error. This analysis could be used to inform strategies to strengthen

487    model performance and generalizability such as field management procedures, flight planning

488    targets, and data stratification methods.

511    3.3.4 Label noise


512              High-quality image labels are critical for training effective models. Expert raters score

513    SLB severity longitudinally to calculate the area under the disease progress curves (AUDPC).

514    This AUDPC value serves as the phenotype for most genetic studies focused on maize disease

515    resistance. In our study, we required no additional time commitment from expert raters to

516    annotate lesions, score images, or any other form of training data labeling. Instead, we

517    appropriated these visual scores for image labels, greatly streamlining the process of generating

518    training data. A drawback of this application of in-field visual scores is the discrepancy in

519    frequency between scoring and imaging (Figure S1). We accepted an offset between scoring and

520    imaging of up to three days as a necessary tradeoff between dataset size and label quality. For

521    instance, the majority of images acquired in 2024 received labels offset by three days. If we

522    reduced the maximum offset to two days rather than three, the 2024 image count would have

523    dropped by 59% and the overall image count by 20% (Figure S5). Disease severity is a

524    phenotype that can progress temporally, and we acknowledge that the three-day span is inexact.
    bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.03.729887; this version posted June 6, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
  (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




525    However, we considered the detrimental effects of the year imbalance and shrunken dataset to be

526    greater than the label noise introduced by a three-day flight-score offset.


527              With this accepted range, plot images generated from one flight may receive visual scores

528    recorded across a window as wide as up to seven days. We hypothesized that this flight-score

529    offset introduced a systematic bias in signed error as images acquired before visual scoring

530    capture plants with less disease relative to those plants observed days later by expert raters,

531    indicating that EVA-02-B perceived disease progression over a maximum of three days in this

532    dataset. The relationship between signed error and flight-score offset was found to be statistically

533    significant with a weak negative correlation (p < 0.001,  = -0.1648). As expected, test scores

534    from images acquired before visual scoring tended to underestimate disease severity and those

535    from images acquired after scoring tended to overestimate disease severity (Figure 8). Over the

536    course of the imaging period, perceived severity increased due to both disease progression (more

537    necrotic lesions) and maize phenology (more senescence). Aerial images captured prior to

538    scoring showed plots with less disease and senescence than was manually observed in the same

539    plot one to three days later. On the other hand, images acquired after scoring contained more

540    disease-relevant details relative to what the expert raters had observed in the days prior. Thus,

541    the test scores produced from RGB aerial imagery by EVA-02-B seemingly accounted for fine-

542    scale changes in disease severity that occurred during the days between image capture and visual

543    scoring.

574                                                             3.4 Conclusion


575              This study investigated the capability of deep learning models to automate the estimation
                                                                                                         n

576    of maize quantitative disease severity from UAV imagery. Over three growing seasons, we

577    developed a dataset of 26,071 plot images labeled with in-field visual scores taken by expert

578    raters. We evaluated nine deep learning models with an agronomically relevant cross-year

579    evaluation to test generalizability onto future growing seasons. By treating visual scores taken on

580    a one-to-nine scale in increments of 0.5 as quantitative and continuous values, we used standard

581    regression model evaluation metrics to determine the highest performing model. We compared

582    correlations amongst expert raters to correlations with image-derived scores and ascertained the

583    model effectively estimated disease resistance with the advantage of comparable accuracy and

584    substantially greater throughput.
    bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.03.729887; this version posted June 6, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
  (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




585              The model errors were analyzed against potential contributing factors from UAV flights,

586    individual plot images, and the visual score labels. The mean absolute error per flight showed

587    weak correlations to solar geometry, lighting conditions, and in-season timing (Figure 6).

588    Absolute error also proved to be robust to image-level features with weak correlations to

589    proportion of weeds, proportion of shadow, mean brightness, and contrast (Figure 8). Label noise

590    introduced by the date misalignment between visual scoring and imaging showed an error pattern

591    indicating our approach achieved fine-scale monitoring of disease progression over time (Figure

592    9). Thus, we conclude that changes in perceived resistance during the days of separation between

593    visual scoring and aerial imaging is a key source of model error from label noise. Our approach

594    to image dataset creation successfully captured the high diversity of field conditions present in

595    agronomic research. When analyzed with pre-trained deep learning models, the outcome was

596    high-quality predictions, shown to be based on disease-relevant details and proven to be robust to

597    various sources of noise.


598              Phenotypic attributes beyond disease resistance can be extracted from the high-resolution

599    RGB image dataset developed for this study. Leveraging the wealth of information available in

600    these images allows additional analysis of phenotypes with known relevance to disease severity,

601    such as leaf angle and plant maturity. Advancements in deep learning algorithms for image

602    analysis empower the discovery of novel variables from our image dataset. Unsupervised

603    learning, a familiar statistical method for revealing hidden patterns in data, can be implemented

604    in the context of our study to discern previously unknown relationships in disease resistance

605    phenotypes. Self-supervision for deep learning image analysis learns rich representations that can

606    both resolve latent phenotypes from image data and provide pretraining for downstream

607    supervised learning.
    bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.03.729887; this version posted June 6, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
  (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.




608              This study found promising results that lay groundwork for improved phenotyping of

609    maize quantitative disease resistance. We assert that our approach can alleviate the barrier to

610    experiment size and consistency imposed by in-field visual scoring for SLB resistance. Since

611    each flight captured images of thousands of plots in less than one hour, disease severity can be

612    estimated over larger fields and with higher frequency than is possible with visual scoring. This

613    increase in scoring frequency paves the way for future studies on the genetic mechanisms

614    underlying variation in the temporal dynamics of SLB disease resistance (Alladassi et al., 2026).

615    The increase in scorable field size has potential to strengthen the statistical power of genetic

616    studies when larger populations and more repetitions can be included. The actual feasibility of

617    our approach could be studied with downstream validation benchmarks such as disease

618    resistance QTL identification with model-derived scores. Such future work would solidify HTP

619    as an effective method for genetic dissection and integration of QDR into elite maize hybrids.

620    Our extraction of underlying mechanistic variables in image processing and analysis provides a

621    procedural basis for improved modeling via multimodal data fusion, informed model-error

622    correction, and guided ensembling (Ispizua Yamati et al., 2026).
