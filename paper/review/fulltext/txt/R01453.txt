Highlights

What are the main findings?

Machine learning models for durum wheat yield achieved over 90% explained variance (R2) under standard random sampling, but this explained variance dropped to approximately 50% on unseen growing seasons.

The integration of coarse meteorological data with high-resolution satellite imagery via a two-stage framework slightly reduced the root mean square error (RMSE) but did not improve the overall explained variance (R2).

What are the implications of the main findings?

Relying exclusively on open-source environmental and satellite data leads to informational saturation, with moderate interannual crop yield predictability.

The development of highly reliable, real-world agricultural forecasting systems requires the integration of static, localized field metadata, such as soil physicochemical profiles and management practices.

Abstract

Accurate crop yield prediction provides essential information for strategic decision-making and precision agriculture, yet achieving reliable forecasts across unseen growing seasons remains challenging. The main objective of this study is to develop a two-stage data assimilation framework that integrates multi-scale environmental data to improve yield predictions and overcome the spatial–temporal autocorrelation limitations of standard machine learning (ML) models. Utilizing an eight-year continuous dataset (2018–2025) of durum wheat fields in Thessaly, Greece, this study integrates high-resolution Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery with macro-scale ERA5-Land meteorological variables. Eight ML algorithms are trained to predict yield at the pixel level. To test model generalization and prevent overfitting, the framework is evaluated using both standard random splitting and leave-one-year-out (LOYO) cross-validation. Concurrently, multiple linear regression (MLR) is utilized to select the most significant meteorological predictors from monthly temperature (maximum and minimum) and precipitation data, which are then integrated into the pixel-level predictions via additive and multiplicative late-fusion assimilation. The results demonstrate that, while standard random splitting produces high explained variance (R2 > 0.90), LOYO validation shows a predictive maximum of approximately 50% explained variance. The late-fusion assimilation slightly reduces interannual offsets, from an RMSE of 966 kg ha−1 to 902 kg ha−1. However, the R2 values remain static due to informational saturation. This study concludes that, while integrating regional climate data improves absolute annual yield magnitudes, securing reliable agricultural forecasts requires the integration of localized agronomic metadata, such as soil properties and field-specific management practices.

Keywords:
precision agriculture; machine learning; Sentinel-2; durum wheat; yield prediction

1. Introduction

1.1. Crop Yield Prediction in Precision Agriculture

Accurate and early crop yield prediction across large agricultural regions is a vital tool for different stakeholders, including producers, processing industries, commodity traders, and policymakers [1,2,3]. At the macroeconomic scale, early yield estimations assist in the stabilization of agricultural markets, the establishment of pricing policies, and the optimization of supply chain logistics [4,5]. Despite its value, accurate pre-harvest yield prediction is inherently challenging because final crop production is a complex, multifactorial outcome [5,6,7]. It is co-regulated by interacting genotypic traits (e.g., crop varieties), localized physicochemical soil properties, agronomic management practices, and environmental conditions [8,9,10,11]. While human interventions and field management significantly determine yield variance, severe interannual climatic variability acts as the most unpredictable and broadscale constraint on this system [7,10,12]. In Mediterranean and semi-arid environments, durum wheat (Triticum durum Desf.) production is vulnerable to erratic rainfall distribution and extreme temperatures, across the crop’s entire developmental cycle [13,14,15]. During the early stages in autumn and winter, extreme cold stress and inadequate temperatures can compromise stand establishment, reduce emergence rates, and restrict tiller development [13,16,17]. On the contrary, elevated temperatures during the reproductive and grain-filling phases induce rapid, forced maturation [13]. These combined stresses induce a non-linear physiological response that reduces the number of grains per spike, individual kernel weight, and carbohydrate assimilation, which masks the crop’s genetic yield potential and eventually leads to interannual yield instability [13,14].

Precision agriculture (PA) addresses these challenges by replacing uniform field practices with targeted management of spatial variability [18,19]. The factors that influence crop growth are highly heterogeneous. For example, topographic variations (e.g., elevation and slope) can contribute to approximately 20% of yield variance, while physicochemical soil properties explain up to an additional 30% [20]. The mapping of this spatial variability helps with optimizing site-specific inputs. Combining remote sensing with variable rate application (VRA) techniques may reduce N applications by 5.8% to 14.2%. This approach keeps total nitrogen use below statutory thresholds while also stabilizing the final yield and protein content across different fertility zones [18]. Similarly, site-specific irrigation based on spatiotemporal data can reduce water use by up to 15% while increasing overall yields [19].

1.2. Remote Sensing and Machine Learning for Yield Estimation

Early approaches to broadscale crop yield prediction relied on legacy satellite platforms, such as the advanced very high resolution radiometer (AVHRR) and the moderate resolution imaging spectroradiometer (MODIS) [4,10]. Even though they were effective for regional macroscopic assessments, their coarse spatial resolutions (ranging from 250 m to over 1 km) introduce mixed-pixel effects, which makes them inadequate for capturing the yield characteristics and within-field heterogeneity of smaller fields [4,8]. To address these spatial limitations, the Landsat program (e.g., Landsat 7 ETM+ and 8 OLI) was a substantial improvement in field-scale mapping with its 30 m spatial resolution [10,11,15,21]. However, Landsat is restricted by its 16-day temporal revisit frequency. This gap, coupled with a high susceptibility to cloud cover during important phenological stages, restricts the creation of the dense, continuous time series required for intra-season yield forecasting [8]. The deployment of the Copernicus Sentinel-2 constellation resolved all these spatiotemporal constraints. Providing a 10 m spatial resolution and a 5-day temporal revisit frequency, Sentinel-2 satisfies the spatiotemporal requirements for precision agricultural monitoring [22,23].

Early high-resolution satellite models relied mainly on multiple linear regression (MLR) and standard vegetation indices (VIs), such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI). However, these linear approaches struggle to capture the complex, non-linear dynamics of the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum [20,24]. Mathematically, MLR is prone to multicollinearity when handling correlated environmental and biophysical variables [9,20]. Physiologically, standard VIs limit spectral analysis to two or three band combinations. This omits red-edge and short-wave infrared (SWIR) data, which are important for detecting the canopy water content, early physiological stress, and crop–environment interactions [8,9]. Lastly, indices like the NDVI often saturate in dense canopies during booting and flowering, which leads to underestimated biomass and nitrogen uptake [12,18].

To overcome the limitations of linear VI-based models, machine learning (ML) algorithms provide alternatives for crop yield estimation. With the use of semi-parametric and non-parametric architectures, ML models map non-linear biological interactions without relying on predefined mathematical functions [20,24]. Rather than depending on simplistic VIs, ML uses all available spectral bands to extract complex, multi-scale features [9,23]. Different ML architectures offer distinct advantages. Tree-based ensembles, such as Random Forest (RF) and Extremely Randomized Trees (Extra Trees), effectively manage multicollinearity. RF constructs multiple decision trees using random subsets of training data and evaluates random features at each node split, which mitigates the influence of correlated variables and makes the model robust to multicollinearity [20,24]. Extra Trees modifies this logic to further stabilize the results and avoid overfitting [9]. Alternatively, gradient boosting algorithms build sequential ensembles of weak models to iteratively minimize residual errors. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) provides scalable solutions for large-scale environmental and genotypic datasets, while LightGBM and CatBoost show high accuracy for complex regression tasks [1,13,25,26]. Lastly, multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), a core architecture of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), resolve non-linear tasks using feed-forward backpropagation. By extracting high-level abstractions across hidden layers, MLPs identify dynamic crop–environment interactions that classical regression misses [4,10,27].

1.3. Model Generalization and Spatial Mismatch

Despite these algorithmic advantages, a major limitation in the current literature is that ML architectures often show high accuracy within single growing seasons or standard random splits, yet their predictive performance frequently degrades when evaluated on unseen years [5,28,29]. Methods like leave-one-year-out (LOYO) cross-validation show a generalization gap that is caused by interannual climate variability. Data-driven ML models are constrained by the variance in their historical training data, thus struggling to predict outcomes during extreme anomalies, such as droughts or heatwaves [28]. This degradation shows an increased squared bias, where the models cannot extrapolate non-linear physiological responses to new environmental stressors [29]. Models that overfit specific years fail to provide reliable forecasts under changing climate conditions [5,28].

To address this generalization problem, predictive frameworks often incorporate regional meteorological variables to calibrate ML models across different seasons. However, the integration of multiple data sources introduces a spatial resolution mismatch [30,31]. Satellite imagery provides high-resolution spectral data (e.g., 10 m to 30 m), whereas climatic variables from global or regional datasets are in much coarser scales (e.g., 4 km to 9 km) [29,30,31]. Traditional methods attempt to integrate these scales by aggregating the high-resolution spectral and yield data to match the coarse meteorological data, usually via mean or weighted mean summaries [32]. This aggregation removes important information. It removes microclimatic variations, local soil moisture stress, and topographical effects [31,32]. As a result, the direct combination of macro-scale climate data with micro-scale satellite imagery creates spatial averaging errors, which leads to spatial bias and pixel-level overfitting [4,31].

Research has identified several advanced integration strategies to bridge this spatial gap and preserve localized variance. While early fusion techniques stack all raw variables into a single input layer and often struggle with scale differences, intermediate-level fusion in Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) extracts features separately before concatenation, offering greater spatial adaptability [33]. In addition, advanced spatially aware data fusion networks use attention mechanisms to adaptively merge multimodal data based on spatiotemporal relevance [34]. Apart from deep learning, data assimilation algorithms like Particle Filtering dynamically integrate real-time remote sensing data into biophysical crop models (e.g., CERES and SPASS). This approach corrects state variables and reduces the uncertainty of coarse climatic inputs [11,35]. Lastly, two-stage hierarchical frameworks accommodate nested data structures without the heavy computational cost of DNNs. By using a first model for high-resolution data and a second model for coarse meteorological variables, this method separates interannual climate variance from local field variability, which minimizes scale-induced overfitting and preserves the integrity of both data sources [36,37].

1.4. Study Objectives

To address the aforementioned generalization limitations and the spatial resolution mismatch in multi-source modeling, this study evaluates a two-stage data assimilation framework. While late-fusion architectures have been successfully applied to separate temporal climatic effects from spatial field variability [38], their efficacy in stabilizing high-resolution predictions across extreme interannual climatic shifts requires a thorough multi-year validation. As a result, the main objective of this study is to explore the effects of combining multi-scale data, assessing how different meteorological integrations can influence the predictive accuracy of high-resolution durum wheat yield data across unseen growing seasons.

To achieve this, this study first evaluates the capacity of various machine learning algorithms to model within-field variability using Sentinel-2 multispectral time-series data. This high-resolution spatial modeling is then integrated with interannual meteorological variables derived from ERA5-Land reanalysis data. By comparing two late-fusion assimilation approaches, this study investigates whether adjusting micro-scale pixel predictions with a macro-scale meteorological baseline can reduce scale-induced overfitting. The generalizability of this integrated approach is evaluated using a LOYO cross-validation framework over an 8-year continuous dataset (2018–2025) in the Thessaly region, Greece.

In summary, the proposed workflow addresses these identified limitations. The use of multi-band machine learning models overcomes the multicollinearity and signal saturation issues of standard linear indices. Additionally, the two-stage late-fusion strategy resolves the spatial resolution mismatch. By separating local field variance from macro-scale climatic effects, this method prevents spatial averaging errors. Lastly, the LOYO cross-validation addresses the generalization gap by providing a realistic assessment of the models across unseen growing seasons.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Study Area

All study sites are located in the Thessaly plain, central Greece. The spatial distribution of the monitored fields within the broader Thessaly region is shown in Figure 1. Monitoring was conducted over eight consecutive growing periods, from 2017–2018 to 2024–2025. In total, 306 fields were utilized, covering a cumulative area of 774.09 hectares, which correspond to 77,409 pixels of 10 m × 10 m resolution. The detailed distribution of the dataset across the growing periods is presented in Table 1.

Figure 1.
Satellite image of the Thessaly plain, Greece. The studied fields across the eight growing periods are indicated in red.

Table 1.
Detailed distribution of the dataset across the eight growing periods.

The selection of the fields was based on two primary criteria: (i) the availability of accurate yield map data and (ii) an adequate field size, scheme, and orientation capable of providing a sufficient number of pure Sentinel-2 pixels. The monitored fields were cultivated with various durum wheat varieties (e.g., Iridae, Meridiano, Normano, Simeto, and Svevo) and belonged to different farmers who applied their own distinct cultivation practices. This diversity in crop management was deliberately included to ensure that the predictive models could be evaluated under varying, real-world conditions, thereby improving their generalizability. Across all fields, sowing occurred during November, and harvesting took place in June of the following year.

2.2. Yield Measurement

Harvest was performed with a John Deere S660i combine harvester (Deere & Company, Moline, IL, USA) equipped with an integrated yield mapping system, which generated geolocated yield data in a point vector format [39]. The spatial resolution of these initial measurements was approximately 1.75 m by 2.5 m, with some variation depending on the harvester’s travel speed during the harvest.

The raw point vector data were further processed in the QGIS software environment (version 3.40.15) [40], as shown in Figure 2. The outliers that result from grain flow delays (typically occurring at the start and end of the field sections) were manually identified and eliminated. Subsequently, the filtered data were interpolated into a continuous raster surface with the use of the QGIS inverse distance weighting (IDW) algorithm. Lastly, the interpolated yield raster maps were resampled to a 10 m × 10 m spatial resolution to align with the pixels of the corresponding Sentinel-2 images.

Figure 2.
The processing workflow of the yield dataset. Image (a) shows the raw point vector yield measurements. Image (b) shows the field boundary delineation. Image (c) presents the continuous raster surface generated via IDW interpolation. Image (d) shows the yield map after it was resampled to the Sentinel-2 10 m grid.

2.3. Satellite Data

A total of 275 cloud-free Sentinel-2 (A, B and C) images, spanning from October to June across the eight growing periods (2017–2025), were acquired from the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem [41]. This study utilized Level-2A Bottom of Atmosphere (BOA) reflectance products, which have been inherently corrected for radiometric and atmospheric effects. The MultiSpectral Instrument (MSI) on board the Sentinel-2 constellation captures data across 13 spectral bands (ranging from 443 to 2190 nm) at a spatial resolution of 10, 20, or 60 m. To ensure spatial uniformity across the dataset, all spectral bands were resampled to a 10 m × 10 m pixel size using the free open-source SNAP (Sentinels Application Platform) software, version 10.0.

Given the temporal inconsistency of satellite image acquisitions across different years, the creation of a temporally aligned and continuous dataset was essential prior to modeling. To achieve this, a simple linear temporal interpolation was applied to the spectral data using the Python programming language (version 3.13.0). The interpolation was executed at a fixed 5-day time step without the application of any supplementary smoothing filters, which produced a dense and complete time series for each growing period. From these continuous time series, 15 representative dates were selected to achieve complete coverage of all important phenological stages of the durum wheat crop. The distribution of these selected dates across the months of the growing season is presented in Table 2.

Table 2.
Distribution of the 15 selected representative dates across the durum wheat-growing season.

2.4. Meteorological Data

Daily meteorological variables were acquired from the ECMWF ERA5-Land reanalysis dataset [42] via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform [43]. ERA5-Land provides a consistent, globally complete dataset at a spatial resolution of 9 km (0.1° × 0.1°), utilizing atmospheric forcing and lapse rate corrections to combine model simulations with observational data. The daily aggregated variables extracted for this study included the maximum and minimum air temperature at 2 m and total precipitation.

The temporal window for data extraction was defined from September 1st of the sowing year to May 31st of the harvest year. Additionally, to reduce the high dimensionality of the daily records and capture seasonal trends, the data were temporally aggregated into monthly intervals (the mean monthly maximum and minimum temperature and total monthly precipitation).

In order to match the 9 km spatial resolution of the ERA5-Land meteorological data with the 10 m × 10 m pixel grid of the satellite and yield data, a bicubic resampling algorithm was applied to the meteorological data. The monthly meteorological variables were then point-sampled at 10 m resolution across the study fields and exported as tabular data for subsequent integration into the machine learning workflow.

2.5. Modeling Process

The modeling process integrates high-resolution spatial modeling with interannual meteorological variables for durum wheat yield prediction (Figure 3). This process consists of spectral data normalization, the training and hyperparameter optimization of the machine learning algorithms, the application of two evaluation strategies, and two-stage data assimilation.

Figure 3.
Schematic overview of the two-stage data assimilation framework. The flowchart illustrates the parallel processing pipelines for the high-resolution Sentinel-2 imagery and macro-scale ERA5-Land variables, the evaluation strategies (random split versus LOYO), and the final late-fusion integration.

2.5.1. Spectral Data Normalization

A relative radiometric normalization (RRN) procedure was implemented for the Sentinel-2 spectral data to homogenize the spectral responses between images acquired at different times. This normalization is considered a useful preprocessing step in multitemporal remote sensing frameworks, as it effectively mitigates the radiometric inconsistencies caused by internal sensor variations and complex external environmental factors, which include atmospheric scattering and topography [44]. Accordingly, the reflectance value of the near-infrared band (NIR–B8) was utilized as a dynamic reference baseline for each pixel in every individual image. The normalized spectral data were formulated as Normalized Difference Indices (NDIs) according to the following equation:

(B8−Bx)(B8 + Bx)(B8−Bx)(B8 + Bx)

(1)

where Bx represents the reflectance value of any other spectral band.

2.5.2. Machine Learning Algorithms and Hyperparameter Optimization

All the modeling and data processing procedures were executed within a Python-based Jupyter Lab environment, with the use of advanced machine learning libraries (e.g., scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost). Eight different machine learning regression algorithms were compared. These algorithms were selected to represent a wide spectrum of machine learning frameworks and algorithmic architectures and to make sure that both linear and complex non-linear patterns within the remote sensing data could be captured effectively. Specifically, the modeling process included linear and latent variable methods, such as ElasticNet and Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR). ElasticNet combines L1 and L2 regularization to improve feature selection and model stability in complex agricultural yield prediction frameworks [45]. Additionally, PLSR was selected due to its well-established capability to extract latent components and effectively handle severe multicollinearity, a frequent complication inherent in continuous multispectral datasets [46].

To address non-linear relationships, tree-based ensemble methods were incorporated. This included bagging algorithms, such as Random Forest (RF) and Extra Trees (ET), which use multiple independent decision trees to reduce prediction variance and prevent overfitting. Specifically, RF is highly reliable in modeling complex, non-linear crop–environment interactions [47], while ET introduces further randomization in node splitting, which can improve generalization and prevent overfitting when handling continuous agricultural datasets [48].

In addition, state-of-the-art boosting ensembles were utilized, such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. These gradient boosting frameworks are specifically designed for high performance, processing speed, and optimal handling of complex tabular datasets. Among them, XGBoost offers strong scalability and algorithmic regularization, LightGBM uses gradient-based selective sampling for extreme training efficiency, and CatBoost uses ordered boosting to effectively mitigate prediction shift [49].

Lastly, the capacity to capture deep, multilayered non-linear interactions was evaluated using an Artificial Neural Network (ANN), specifically a multilayer perceptron (MLP) regressor. MLPs leverage hierarchical representation learning to apply non-linear transformations on high-dimensional data, although their architecture requires thorough hyperparameter optimization to avoid getting stuck in local minima [50].

The hyperparameter optimization was integrated into the training pipeline to establish the optimal model’s performance and to reduce the risk of overfitting. Given the computational complexity of tuning eight different algorithms across a multi-year, high-resolution dataset, an exhaustive grid search was deemed computationally prohibitive. Instead, a randomized search cross-validation approach was implemented. This process samples a fixed number of parameter configurations from specified distributions for computational efficiency. The specific hyperparameter search grids defined for each algorithm are shown in Table 3.

2.5.3. Evaluation Strategies

The modeling process involved two different evaluation strategies. The first strategy, which was the study’s established baseline, was a standard random temporal–spatial split. Following the methodology that was applied in previous related research [51], the entire eight-year dataset was randomly partitioned at the pixel level into an 85% subset for model training and a 15% subset for testing. This partitioning process was iteratively executed five times, with the use of a different random seed for each iteration, thereby ensuring statistical rigor and accounting for the inherent variance of the single random splits. Within the training pipeline of each iteration, an internal randomized 3-fold cross-validation was coupled with the hyperparameter search for reliable model optimization based solely on the training data. Lastly, the models were evaluated on the unseen 15% testing subset. This conventional random sampling approach provides a baseline for the evaluation of the intrinsic learning capacity of the selected algorithms under standard conditions.

However, this standard random sampling in remote sensing datasets often suffers from spatial–temporal autocorrelation, which can lead to overly optimistic accuracy metrics, as models may inadvertently “memorize” specific field or year conditions (data leakage). To address this limitation and simulate a realistic prediction scenario, a leave-one-year-out (LOYO) cross-validation strategy was introduced as the second evaluation approach. In this configuration, the dataset was repeatedly divided based on the eight different growing periods. During each iteration, the data from seven growing years were used to train the algorithms, while the data from the remaining, unseen eighth year were held out for testing. A nested cross-validation architecture was maintained. An internal randomized 3-fold cross-validation was executed within the seven-year training subset to optimize hyperparameters and to avoid any data leakage. Additionally, within both evaluation frameworks, all input features were standardized by utilizing a preprocessing scaling pipeline prior to model fitting. This exhaustive temporal cross-validation process ensured that the models were evaluated only on their capacity to predict durum wheat yield in an entirely unseen future season, which then provided a realistic assessment of their generalizability.

2.5.4. Two-Stage Meteorological Data Integration

The monthly meteorological variables (Tmax, Tmin, and precipitation) spanning from September to May were averaged spatially across all fields. These variables were selected because extreme temperatures and precipitation deficits are the primary, well-documented drivers of interannual yield variability for durum wheat in Mediterranean environments, and utilizing simple variables instead of complex derived indices prevents the curse of dimensionality under limited sample sizes. This produced a single mean value per year for each variable, which was compared with the average annual yield. An exhaustive combinatorial search was then executed using multiple linear regression (MLR) to determine the optimal subset of meteorological predictors of yield at an annual scale. The use of MLR with sequential parameter selection is a well-established technique for identifying dominant meteorological drivers of wheat yield while also maintaining model interpretability [52]. The search algorithm evaluated all possible combinations of two and three meteorological parameters. This three-predictor threshold was used to maintain sufficient degrees of freedom and prevent overfitting under the sample size limitation of N = 8 years. Only the combinations that met the thresholds for statistical significance (p < 0.05) and multicollinearity (variance inflation factor—VIF < 5.0) were retained.

A late-fusion data assimilation strategy integrating the optimal meteorological equations, as determined above, with the top-performing spectral machine learning models was employed. The ML models generated pixel-level yield predictions for each test year. Concurrently, the selected meteorological equations computed a meteorological baseline yield for each year. The divergence between the annual mean of the ML predictions and the meteorological baseline quantified the offset for each growing period. This assimilation was executed via additive and multiplicative methodologies.

The additive approach calibrates the ML prediction of each pixel using the absolute discrepancy (Δ) between the meteorological estimate and the ML mean:

Δ = ŶMeteo − Y−MLΔ = Y^Meteo − Y-ML

This two-stage architecture combines the high spatial resolution of Sentinel-2 spectral data that captures within-field variability with the coarse ERA5-Land meteorological data to calibrate the absolute annual yield magnitudes. Similar two-stage modeling approaches have proven to be effective in agricultural predictive systems, as they separate the temporal climatic effects from spatial field variability, thus preventing the overfitting that often occurs in direct multi-source fusion [38].

2.6. Statistics

All statistical analyses and modeling frameworks were executed in Python 3.13 within a Jupyter Lab (version 4.4.3) environment. Data management, spatial aggregation, and algebraic transformations were performed programmatically within this framework.

For the statistical analysis of the meteorological data, best subset selection was applied through multiple linear regression (MLR) to evaluate the variables. Multicollinearity amongst predictors was controlled by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and applying a variance inflation factor threshold (VIF < 5.0). Variable retention was further determined by the statistical significance of the regression coefficients (p < 0.05), and the stability of these MLR models was assessed using LOYO cross-validation. Separately, the machine learning and data assimilation pipelines managed data standardization, k-fold model cross-validation, and hyperparameter optimization.

The overall performance of both the meteorological MLR and machine learning models was evaluated using a well-known set of metrics. The best-performing models were determined by the adjusted coefficient of determination (adjusted R2) and the standard coefficient of determination (R2). Additionally, model accuracy was quantified using the root mean squared error (RMSE) and by calculating the linear regression slope against the 1:1 fit line.

3. Results

The proposed framework is evaluated in three phases to assess the integration of multi-scale data. First, the ML modeling performance using only Sentinel-2 data is presented. This evaluation is further divided into two steps. The initial step uses a standard random split to provide a baseline for the evaluation of the algorithms’ intrinsic learning capacity under standard conditions. However, because this standard random sampling is prone to spatial–temporal autocorrelation and data leakage, the second step introduces LOYO cross-validation to simulate a realistic prediction scenario. This step allows for an assessment of the models’ generalizability when evaluated on an entire growing season. Next, the most significant meteorological variables are identified, and the optimal multiple linear regression (MLR) models are selected to capture interannual climate variations. Lastly, the two-stage, late-fusion data assimilation strategy is evaluated, assessing how the integration of the meteorological equations affects the performance of the ML predictions.

4. Discussion

The present study developed a predictive framework for durum wheat yield across an 8-year period (2018–2025) in the Thessaly plain, Greece. By evaluating machine learning models utilizing Sentinel-2 imagery alongside late-fusion assimilation of ERA5-Land meteorological data, this research explored the limits of yield forecasting based only on open-source environmental inputs.

4.1. Performance and Generalizability of the Baseline Models

The initial evaluation of the machine learning algorithms using the standard random split showed high performance, particularly for the complex tree-based ensembles. Five out of the eight tested algorithms achieved an R2 above 0.90, with an RMSE of approximately 380–400 kg ha−1. These high performances align with those in previous research that evaluated wheat yield under standard random or cross-validation frameworks. For example, when utilizing multispectral satellite data to predict winter wheat yield, Long Short-Term Memory networks and Random Forest algorithms reached R2 values of 0.930 and 0.886, respectively [23]. Similarly, models incorporating all Sentinel-2 spectral bands in southern Germany explained 84% of the yield variance (R2 = 0.84) with an RMSE of 560 kg ha−1 [53], while Random Forest frameworks that were tested with a standard 80/20 random split in arid environments maintained a maximum R2 of 0.82 [31]. Additionally, specifically for durum wheat in Mediterranean environments, models that relied only on multispectral vegetation indices reached an R2 value of 0.79 for specific modern varieties like “Ciclope” [12].

However, the transition to the leave-one-year-out (LOYO) cross-validation revealed a significant performance degradation. The maximum explained variance dropped to approximately 0.51 (CatBoost and Random Forest), while errors increased to nearly 1000 kg ha−1. This difference reflects the spatial–temporal autocorrelation problem inherent in standard random data splitting, where algorithms tend to memorize specific field or season conditions rather than learning the underlying physiological responses. Fathololoumi et al. [54] demonstrated a similar effect in crop yield modeling. Even though a pixel-based random split achieved an R2 of 0.86 and a low RMSE of 16.5 (standardized units), the spatial cross-validation caused the RMSE to spike to 30.9 due to the elimination of spatial autocorrelation [54]. Furthermore, Cai et al. [28] showed that models evaluated with LOYO validation fail to generalize during extreme unseen weather events, such as severe droughts, because purely data-driven models are constrained by the variance in their historical training records [28].

Algorithm rankings shift significantly depending on the validation strategy. Under random splitting, performance differences among the top tree-based ensembles are marginal. This is expected, as spatial autocorrelation masks true model generalization. For operational use, algorithm selection should rely on prediction error under unseen conditions. Under the LOYO framework, LightGBM and Random Forest demonstrated the highest transferability. While CatBoost retained a slightly higher R2, LightGBM and Random Forest achieved the lowest errors (RMSE of 964 and 966 kg ha−1, respectively). Minimizing this error is of great importance for practical yield prediction. Therefore, LightGBM and Random Forest are the recommended algorithms for operational deployment.

The resulting LOYO performance, which explains approximately 50% of the yield variance, aligns with the performance limits observed in comparable studies that rely exclusively on macro-scale environmental and spectral data. For instance, predictive models in Northeast China that relied on climate and satellite data, without static soil and geographical variables, reported variance maximums of 53%, 44%, and 36% for maize, rice, and soybean, respectively [8]. The current methodology utilizes open-source remote sensing imagery without incorporating field-level metadata, such as soil physicochemical properties, topographic variations, specific durum wheat varieties, or agronomic management practices (e.g., sowing dates and nitrogen fertilization rates). The absence of these static and management variables has been widely documented to limit a model’s generalization capacity across unseen growing seasons. Zare et al. [11] reported a 26% prediction error when detailed management data and soil parameters were omitted, which was subsequently reduced to 15% only after integrating these local variables through data assimilation [11]. In addition, predictive maximums vary significantly based on crop genetics. As noted previously, pure remote sensing approaches can achieve a higher accuracy for modern varieties than ancient varieties [12]. Therefore, given the multifactorial nature of crop production, explaining half of the interannual variance without access to field-specific metadata represents a consistent threshold for remote sensing frameworks, rather than an absolute limit.

4.2. Meteorological Modeling and Interannual Variability

The combinatorial search using the multiple linear regression (MLR) models showed a consistent dependence on specific temporal windows, especially the months of May and November. The frequent selection of May maximum and minimum temperatures within the top-performing equations aligns with the physiological sensitivity of durum wheat during the reproductive and grain-filling phases. In Mediterranean environments, late-spring weather anomalies act as primary drivers of interannual yield variability. For instance, in southern Italy, extreme intra-season climatic variability during anthesis and grain-filling phases, such as May precipitation dropping to 1 mm against a historical average of 38 mm coupled with a mean temperature of 23.1 °C, induced severe physiological stress and required variable-rate nitrogen zoning to avoid yield degradation [36]. Similarly, research in Apulia, Italy, demonstrated that high May temperatures (21.6 °C) and low rainfall (64 mm) reduced the anthesis-to-maturity phase to just 41 days, which accelerated senescence and limited carbohydrate accumulation [22]. On the contrary, abnormally low spring temperatures (19.6 °C) and excess rainfall (81.8 mm) prolonged this developmental phase to 64 days. Even though this induced a “stay-green effect”, it also disrupted carbohydrate translocation to the grains, which resulted in unexpected, localized yield losses [22]. Furthermore, extreme events such as late-spring cloudbursts in Italy have been identified as triggers for severe national yield reductions and spectral response alterations [7].

In addition to the late-spring variables, the frequent inclusion of November minimum temperature reflects the importance of the autumn establishment phase. In Mediterranean and semi-arid environments, the thermal conditions during the sowing window determine the crop’s baseline genetic potential. Sub-optimal conditions and delayed sowing outside the optimal November window can suppress germination and tillering. For example, experimental delays in semi-arid environments from November to January resulted in a notable drop in the average annual yield from 2922.5 kg ha−1 to just 582.5 kg ha−1, with that of vulnerable varieties reducing to a minimum of 170 kg ha−1 [13]. These early season deficits compromise the structural foundation of the crop, and they induce yield reductions that cannot be fully compensated for by favorable spring conditions.

However, the modeling of these macro-scale climate effects is inherently constrained by the limited dataset (N = 8 years) and the restricted geography of the study area, which narrows the overall range of observed climate variability. When fitting predictive algorithms on restricted temporal records, models are prone to capturing non-generalizable, spurious fluctuations. To prevent this overfitting, safeguards were implemented in the MLR feature selection process. Instead of using high-dimensional, complex derived meteorological variables, the methodology intentionally constrained the input space to simple, interpretable monthly variables (max and min temperatures and total precipitation). This simplification is a documented standard to avoid the curse of dimensionality when sample sizes are moderate [29]. Additionally, multicollinearity was controlled by applying a variance inflation factor (VIF) threshold of <5.0 alongside significance criteria (p < 0.05). These parameters follow established safeguards in large-scale yield forecasting, where researchers utilize dual-threshold exclusions (e.g., discarding variables with p > 0.01 and limiting inter-variable Pearson correlations at <0.85) [37,53] or intra-group correlation thresholds of r < 0.5 [28] to ensure that the models represent true physiological responses rather than historical data artifacts. However, even with these statistical controls in place, the inherent limitations of a small sample size cannot be completely eliminated. Any residual overfitting to climatic idiosyncrasies in MLR modeling risks propagating into the final two-stage data assimilation, which renders the data assimilation vulnerable when making predictions under conditions of unprecedented weather extremes.

4.3. Evaluation of the Two-Stage Data Assimilation

The integration of coarse meteorological data with high-resolution satellite imagery introduces a spatial mismatch. In the Thessaly study area, fields experience nearly identical regional weather conditions within any given growing season. Moreover, the dataset exhibits temporal discontinuity, meaning that it lacks a substantial number of continuous, multi-year observations for the exact same fields across the 8-year period. This limitation renders advanced time-series architectures inapplicable. For example, while deep learning networks such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) have achieved exceptionally high predictive accuracies for winter wheat yield (R2 = 0.930; RMSE = 201 kg ha−1) [23], these algorithms mathematically require continuous, multi-year field records to capture dynamic temporal dependencies.

As a result, the assimilation of 9 km ERA5-Land variables into the 10 m pixel-level machine learning models (which is a standard early-fusion approach) was avoided. Directly stacking macro-scale climate predictors with micro-scale spectral data across a geographically concentrated area creates spatial averaging errors, which masks sub-field spatial heterogeneity [8,29]. This early-fusion strategy forces the models to overfit at the pixel level by memorizing broad interannual climatic shifts rather than learning localized, within-field physiological responses. For example, in early-fusion yield forecasting, direct stacking of limited time-series variables resulted in a high RMSE of 685 kg ha−1 due to multicollinearity, whereas structurally decoupling the predictors into a two-stage stack-ensemble reduced the RMSE to 451 kg ha−1 [55].

To bridge this resolution gap, a two-stage late-fusion data assimilation framework was evaluated. This approach separates the localized spatial variability captured by Sentinel-2 from the regional climatic baseline dictated by the MLR equations. Comparable hierarchical decoupling methods have proven highly effective in resolving spatial mismatches in the literature. For example, in Northern China, researchers utilized Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM) to separate these scales for winter wheat forecasting. By using high-resolution spectral indices as first-level predictors for micro-canopy dynamics and coarse meteorological data as second-level predictors to adjust the intercept and slope, this late-fusion method outperformed standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) early-fusion. It achieved a yield R2 of 0.75 with an RMSE of 110 kg ha−1, and it significantly reduced the normalized RMSE for grain protein predictions by almost half, from 13.56% to 6.87% [37]. Similarly, post-simulation Bayesian data fusion approaches in Australian wheat modeling separated historical bias from dynamic forecasting, which reduced the absolute yield bias by more than 85% in May compared to single-source methods [56]. The authors justified this error reduction by explaining that decoupled late-fusion architectures prevent the non-stationary climate biases and spatial spreading errors that inherently corrupt direct early-fusion inputs [56].

The results of the present study demonstrate moderate utility of this decoupled architecture. Under the LOYO cross-validation, the late-fusion assimilation provided a slight, consistent reduction in the absolute prediction error, even though the overall improvements were not drastic. Specifically, when integrating the meteorological baseline from Equation 8 via the multiplicative approach, the mean RMSE for the LightGBM algorithm decreased from 964 kg ha−1 to 908 kg ha−1, while the Random Forest baseline RMSE was reduced from 966 kg ha−1 to 902 kg ha−1. These reductions validate the necessity of climate integration in Mediterranean environments, which are heavily impacted by drought and heat stress. As demonstrated by machine learning frameworks in Morocco [45] and crop modeling assimilation in southern Spain [46], integrating regional climate indices with satellite data systematically improves predictive robustness. This is supported by similar data fusion studies, where integrating meteorological parameters (such as land surface temperature and precipitation) with vegetation indices decreased the RMSE and mean squared error (MSE) by 15% and 30%, respectively, by successfully capturing abiotic heat stress before it manifested as visible canopy degradation [29]. Notably, the total explained variance remained completely static across all integration methods (R2 = 0.489 for LightGBM and R2 = 0.509 for Random Forest). This phenomenon was also observed in the literature when utilizing Particle Filtering to assimilate satellite data into process-based crop models for winter wheat in Germany, where researchers successfully reduced the median prediction bias from 26% to 15%, yet R2 remained static at approximately 0.27 due to unresolvable input uncertainties, such as missing soil and management data [11]. These marginal improvements align with the informational saturation observed in multi-source fusion. By comparing early- and late-season forecasts, studies in China and Australia demonstrated that the incremental R2 benefit of adding climate data to remote sensing features was reduced from 0.40 down to 0.17 [5] and provided a marginal increase of only 0.06 to 0.12 in the late season [28]. The authors attributed this to the fact that peak-value satellite imagery physically absorbs the historical climatic impacts, accounting for up to 85–95% of the variability, leaving no new variance for the algorithm to explain.

This unchanged R2 indicates that, while the assimilation somewhat corrected the absolute annual yield magnitude (shifting the overall prediction baseline), it could not compensate for the unmeasured spatial variance that was constrained by the previously discussed omission of static field metadata. However, maximizing the potential of this multi-source fusion requires granular field data. As demonstrated by Fiorentini et al. [57] for durum wheat in Italy, improving performance requires the mandatory integration of topsoil parameters (e.g., nitrogen) and specific agronomic management metadata alongside the fusion of weather spectra. When missing these static inputs, process-based models that run with highly uncertain soil hydraulic properties and nitrogen baseline rates showed significant prediction biases of 25% and 26%, which could be partially reduced to 15% via dynamic satellite assimilation [11]. Relying only on dynamic variables creates a performance limit. Omitting local static variables introduces systemic bias and prevents the models from transferring across different genotype–environment–management interactions [9,11,37]. This loss of transferability was quantified by Burdett and Wellen (2022) [20], where a Random Forest model built on high-resolution spatial variables achieved an excellent R2 of 0.92, but, when applied to unseen fields with unrecorded agronomic management standards, the predictive capacity collapsed, showing an R2 of 0.06 and proving that algorithms memorize local spatial patterns when management data are omitted. Lastly, the observation that the assimilation framework provided no performance improvement under the random split evaluation further confirms the presence of spatial autocorrelation in standard sampling. This indicates that the late-fusion approach specifically addresses interannual climatic offsets, which offers a realistic, targeted calibration for real-world yield forecasting across unseen growing seasons.

These findings highlight an informational saturation point in predicting yield based on spectral, meteorological, and environmental modeling. To overcome this predictive limit, future research should prioritize the integration of localized static metadata, such as soil physicochemical profiles and specific agronomic practices, and explore hybrid architectures that combine machine learning with process-based crop models.

Beyond resolving this informational saturation, future implementations must also address the temporal requirements of operational deployment. While the proposed two-stage data assimilation framework marginally improves the interannual climatic offsets, the current methodology relies on data spanning the entire growing season (up to May). As a result, this represents an end-of-season estimation rather than a true early-warning forecast. For operational agricultural decision-making, predictive systems must provide reliable estimates at least one to two months prior to harvest. In the current study, the complete seasonal dataset was utilized deliberately to validate the architecture of the data assimilation method. However, as demonstrated in previous research [51,58], satellite inputs can be systematically reduced. To adapt this assimilation framework for true operational early warning, future iterations must restrict the input data to early-season windows (e.g., stopping in March or April) to assess how the assimilation performs without the benefit of late-season data.

Finally, as these predictive frameworks transition toward operational deployment, future iterations could incorporate Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques, such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [59]. Integrating XAI can bridge the gap between algorithmic complexity and practical agronomy by quantifying the exact contribution of specific spectral and meteorological features, which will ultimately help agricultural stakeholders to understand, interpret, and trust the model outputs.

5. Conclusions

This study developed a predictive framework for durum wheat yield in the Thessaly plain, Greece, over an 8-year period (2018–2025). By comparing machine learning models utilizing high-resolution Sentinel-2 imagery against a two-stage late-fusion assimilation framework incorporating regional ERA5-Land meteorological data, the research evaluated the limits of yield forecasting using open-source environmental inputs.

The results demonstrated that, while standard random data splitting achieved high accuracies due to spatial autocorrelation, the leave-one-year-out (LOYO) cross-validation presented a predictive maximum of approximately 50% explained variance. The integration of macro-scale meteorological data via a late-fusion assimilation approach marginally reduced absolute prediction errors, lowering the Random Forest RMSE from 966 kg ha−1 to 902 kg ha−1. However, the total explained variance remained static. This confirms that, while the assimilation of regional climate data improves the overall interannual yield magnitude, it cannot compensate for the unmeasured localized spatial variance within the fields.

These findings highlight an informational saturation point in solely spectral, meteorological, or environmental modeling. To break through this predictive ceiling, future research should integrate localized, static field metadata, such as specific crop varieties, soil physicochemical profiles, and agronomic management practices. In addition, exploring hybrid architectures that couple machine learning algorithms with process-based crop models could provide mechanisms to better interpret the complex interactions between micro-scale canopy dynamics and macro-scale climate variability.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the models across any metric (p < 0.05, Tukey’s HSD post hoc test).
