Abstract:

Corn is the most widely grown crop in the U.S. and makes up a significant part of the American diet. Under the pressure of feeding a growing population, accurate and timely estimation of corn yield before the harvest is of great importance to supply chain management and regional food security. Recently, machine learning (ML) in conjunction with satellite remote sensing has been used for developing corn yield prediction models. Despite the success, a major bottleneck of training a reliable supervised ML model is the need for representative ground truth labels (e.g., yield records), which may be limited or even not available due to financial and manpower reasons. Also, due to domain shift, an ML model trained with labeled data from a label-rich region (i.e., source domain) could experience a significant performance decrease when directly applied to the region of interest (i.e., target domain). To address this issue, we proposed a Bayesian domain adversarial neural network (BDANN) for unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) on county-level corn yield prediction. By applying adversarial learning and Bayesian inference, BDANN was trained to reduce domain shift and accurately predict corn yield by extracting domain-invariant and task-informative features from both source and target domains. Moreover, the results also demonstrated that the BDANN model generalized well on small training sets. Experiments in two ecoregions in the U.S. corn belt have shown the effectiveness of the proposed BDANN and its superiority over other state-of-the-art methods.

SECTION I.

Introduction

Corn is the most produced crop in the U.S. and primarily grown in the Midwestern states, which region is known as the U.S. corn belt. For example, in 2020, more than 360 million metric tons of grain corn were harvested in the U.S. and over 75% were from the Midwestern area [1]. However, under the pressure of environmental stresses and climate change, increasing interyear variability of corn yield was observed [2]. The timely and accurate prediction of corn yield is therefore of great importance to food security monitoring, risk management, and commodity market planning [3].

Traditional yield prediction methods include survey-based methods, which are time-consuming and labor-intensive, and biophysical crop models, which typically require extensive crop-specific and management-related input parameters that are difficult to obtain [4], [5]. Recent progress in satellite remote sensing has enabled large-scale crop yield prediction. Typically, satellite images are acquired to monitor crop growth conditions, and informative features, such as vegetation indices (VIs), are extracted from them and used as inputs for machine learning (ML) model development. Among different ML methods, deep learning (DL) has achieved impressive results due to its strong learning capability to capture the complex relationships between crop yield and multisensor remote sensing data [1]. For example, Khaki and Wang [6] developed a fully connected deep neural network (DNN) for corn yield prediction using weather and soil variables as input predictors. You et al. [7] considered the spatial–temporal information in satellite imagery by including the Gaussian process to a convolutional neural network (CNN) for county-level soybean yield prediction in the U.S. corn belt. Kang et al. [3] comprehensively evaluated the performance of several conventional ML and DL methods based on multisource remote sensing data for corn yield prediction. More recently, Ma et al. [1] developed a Bayesian neural network (BNN) model, which can not only predict the corn yield but also quantify the predictive uncertainty. Chen et al. [8] proposed a spatial disaggregation method based on several ML methods for corn yield prediction in China at the municipal level.

Despite the success, a large number of ground truth data (e.g., yield records) is required for DL model training [9], [10]. However, historical yield records are not always available, especially for regions without an annual agricultural census. Also, the domain shift, which is the difference of data distributions between regions or domains, would introduce biases in model training and thus limit model’s generalizability. Therefore, DL models trained in one region (i.e., source domain) often lose their validity if directly applied to a new region (i.e., target domain). To address this issue, fine-tuning-based transfer learning strategies have been explored, in which a few layers of the pretrained models from the source domain are refined using the available labeled data from the target domain [11], [12]. For example, Russello [12] trained a CNN model with labeled data from one ecoregion and fine-tuned it in another ecoregion for county-level soybean yield prediction in the U.S. However, this type of approach still requires some labeled samples from the target domain, which may not be available for certain regions.

To realize efficient transfer learning for target regions without labeled samples, unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) has been developed, in which labeled data are not needed in the target domain [13], [14], [15]. Among different UDA methods, the domain adversarial neural network (DANN) is a representative approach and has been successfully used in different applications [13]. For example, Ganin et al. [13] proposed DANN to realize effective transfer learning for handwritten digits classification. Ma et al. [14] applied the DANN model for corn yield prediction across two ecological regions in the U.S. corn belt.

From our previous research [14], it was noticed that to train a reliable DANN model, a large number of labeled data are required from the source domain. To reduce the field survey cost for ground-truthing, it is necessary to improve the model’s learning ability and its performance on small training datasets. For this purpose, in this study, we incorporated Bayesian inference into DANN and proposed a Bayesian DANN (BDANN) for county-level corn yield prediction. Instead of making point estimates, BDANN was designed with Bayesian layers, in which variational distributions were used to approximate the posterior for trainable parameters (i.e., weights and biases). In this manner, BDANN was able to simulate multiple possible models based on variational distributions, which could be regarded as a form of ensemble learning [16] that enabled BDANN to be robust to overfitting and learn well from a small training set.

To evaluate the developed BDANN model, two separate ecological regions in the U.S. corn belt were selected as the study area. Remote sensing and weather variables were first collected from multiple data sources. Then, extracted feature variables were paired with reference yield records for model training. Finally, BDANN was evaluated in four testing years 2016–2019 along with three other comparison models. The main contribution of this work is that we proposed a BDANN model for county-level corn yield prediction that has: 1) a strong transferability across different spatial domains and 2) a superior learning capability on small training datasets.

B. Bayesian Domain Adversarial Neural Network

Based on DANN, we developed a BDANN method by incorporating Bayesian inference into DANN to improve the model’s learning ability and its performance on small training sets. In BDANN, each hidden layer was designed as a Bayesian layer, in which variational distributions were used to approximate the posterior for trainable parameters. The architecture of the proposed BDANN was shown in Fig. 1.

Specifically, instead of making point estimates, the posterior distribution p(w|D)
of each trainable parameter w
was estimated via Bayesian inference. For each input sample x
, its predictive distribution can be made by taking the expectation under p(w|D)
. Taking the expectation is equivalent to averaging predictions from an ensemble of neural networks weighted by the posterior distribution p(w|D)
, which enables the model to be robust to overfit and learn better from a small training set [16]. However, it is intractable to directly estimate the posterior distribution. Therefore, we approximated the posterior on the parameters by variational inference [18], which finds the variational parameters θ
of a distribution q(w|θ)
that minimize its Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence with the posterior distribution p(w|D)
.

Besides, similar to DANN, the domain discriminator Gd
and the yield predictor Gy
of BDANN were trained to predict the domain label and the crop yield, respectively. For Gd
, the domain classification loss Ld
was still designed as a binary cross-entropy function [see (5)]. For Gy
, to account for the different data uncertainty levels from county to county, we followed [1] and designed the yield prediction loss Ly

SECTION III.

Experimental Setup

A. Study Area and Data Acquisition

The experiment was conducted on top corn-producing states with abundant historical yield records in the U.S. corn belt (Fig. 2). Counties in the study area were grouped into two ecoregions according to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) [19], including the Great Plain (GP) and the Eastern Temperate Forests (ETFs). GP is characterized by a wide range of weather with hot summers and low precipitation. GP is therefore subject to drought due to high summer temperature and lack of rainfall. ETF, on the other hand, is characterized by a humid and temperate climate with mild and humid summers. The dominant ecosystems in ETF are closed-canopy deciduous forests with high plant biodiversity [20].

Fig. 2.
Corn growing counties in two ecoregions within the study area, including the ETF region and the GP region.

In terms of remote sensing and weather observations, we considered several representative VIs and environmental stress indicators as predictors in this study. Specifically, three complementary VIs, including green chlorophyll index (GCI), enhanced VI (EVI), and normalized difference water index (NDWI), were extracted from the MODIS MCD43A4 product [21]. Besides, we collected daily mean air temperature (Tmean), maximum air temperature (Tmax), and total precipitation (PPT) from the parameter elevation regressions on independent slopes model (PRISM) dataset. Moreover, daytime and nighttime land surface temperature (LSTday and LSTnight) were extracted from the MODIS MYD11A2 product [22].

To extract the corn-specific features, we used the USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS) cropland data layer (CDL) as the crop mask to mask out areas on noncorn lands [23]. Then, time-series remote sensing and weather variables were aggregated spatially to the county level and aggregated temporally to a 16-day interval from middle April to early October to cover the growing season of corn in the study area. Finally, historical yield records were collected from the USDA NASS [23] and paired with the corresponding predictors for model development and evaluation. The total number of data samples in ETF and GP are 5417 and 5544, respectively.

B. Model Training and Evaluation

To evaluate the model’s transferability, the proposed BDANN model was trained using the labeled data from the source domain and evaluated on the target domain. GP and ETF were alternatively selected as the source domain and the target domain. Besides, BDANN was compared to three other models, including RF, DNN, and DANN. Among them, DANN and BDANN were domain adaptation methods, while RF and DNN were trained on the source domain and directly applied to the target domain without domain adaptation. For a fair comparison, the DANN and the BDANN were designed to have a similar structure. They both had a depth of 6. Specifically, the feature extractor had an input layer with 256 neurons and two hidden layers with 128 neurons each. Both the yield predictor and the domain discriminator had two fully connected hidden layers with 128 and 64 neurons and an output layer.

All models were evaluated in four testing years 2016–2019, while data in all preceding years since 2006 were used for model training for each testing year. For instance, when testing in 2016, data samples in 2006–2015 were used for model training. Two metrics, i.e., root mean square error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R2
), were used to evaluate the model performance.

SECTION IV.

Results and Discussion

A. Model Comparison

The evaluation results in each testing year were shown in Table I. The best performer was highlighted in bold for each study case in each testing year. It was observed that RF and DNN had poor performance in the target domain due to the domain shift. By applying domain adaption, DANN had better spatial transferability than RF and DNN and achieved satisfying accuracies in several testing cases. For example, in 2017, DANN achieved an R2
over 0.70 when transferring from ETF to GP. However, since the domain shift changed yearly, DANN’s performance varied in each testing year. For example, when transferring from GP to ETF in 2018, DANN only achieved an R2
of 0.51. In comparison with DANN, the proposed BDANN model performed more robust and achieved the best performance in most cases. For example, when transferring from GP to ETF in 2018, BDANN outperformed DANN by a large margin and achieved an R2
of 0.67. The only case in which BDANN achieved an R2
below 0.60 was in 2019 when transferring from GP to ETF. The reason may be that several ETF states (i.e., Illinois and Indiana) experienced unusual wetness in 2019 [24], which increased the domain discrepancy between ETF and GP. Consequently, it was difficult for all models to adapt to the target domain and modeling the yield variance. Nevertheless, the proposed BDANN model was still the best performing one with a decent accuracy in this abnormal year (Table I).

Furthermore, we showed the agreement of predicted yield and reported yield by combining results in all testing years (Fig. 3). Due to the domain shift, RF and DNN failed to achieve good agreement and tended to either overestimate or underestimate the corn yield in the target domain. For example, when transferring from GP to ETF, both RF and DNN significantly underestimated yield for high-yielding counties in ETF [Fig. 3(a2) and (b2)]. These errors were significantly reduced in DANN and BDANN when domain adaptation was considered and BDANN performed the best. In addition, BDANN further outperformed DANN with a better agreement between predicted yield and reported yield [Fig. 3(d)]. It demonstrated that BDANN had the best spatial transferability.

B. t-SNE Visualization

To investigate the effect of domain adaptation, we further visualized the feature distributions using the t-distributed stochastic embedding (t-SNE) algorithm, which is an unsupervised visualization tool that models each high-dimensional vector by a 2-D point in such a way that similar vectors are modeled by nearby points and dissimilar vectors are modeled by distant points with high probability [25]. Both the original features and the extracted features by DANN and BDANN of testing data in 2019 are shown in Fig. 4. Feature samples from two ecoregions are color-coded, with green representing the ETF and yellow representing the GP.

Fig. 4.
T-SNE visualization results of (a) original features and extracted features by (b) DANN and (c) BDANN in 2019.

Before domain adaptation, it was observed that the original data from ETF and GP were clearly separated [Fig. 4(a)], showing the existence of domain shift between these two ecoregions. Therefore, RF and DNN trained in one region would lose validity when directly applied to the other region. After domain adaptation, both DANN and BDANN were able to largely reduce the domain shift [Fig. 4(b) and (c)]. However, some isolated data samples were observed in the visualization results of DANN [the red ellipse in Fig. 4(b)]. It was found that those isolated data samples were mostly counties near the boundary of two domains, such as from WI and MN. Since those counties were geographically close to each other and tended to have small domain shift, the domain loss calculated based on those data samples was always. large. Therefore, nearby data samples were easily aligned, and the feature extractor of DANN would be mostly updated based on distant data samples. Consequently, DANN learned to separately match nearby data samples and distant data samples but failed to consider aligning the whole source and target domains. On the other hand, BDANN, due to the regularization by the prior distributions [18], could prevent being overtrained on distant data samples and thus learned to extract cross-domain features with high spatial generalizability. Therefore, the t-SNE results showed that BDANN had well aligned the whole source and target domains with no isolated data clusters [Fig. 4(c)].

C. Model Performance With Different Sizes of Training Sets

Furthermore, to compare the learning abilities of DANN and BDANN on small training sets, we evaluated their performances under different sizes of training sets. Specifically, both models were trained on the same training set in 2006–2018 with a decreasing size. Then, the trained models were evaluated on the full testing set in the testing year 2019. GP and ETF were alternatively selected as the source and target domains. In both cases, the size of the training set was gradually decreased from 100% (about 5000 samples) to 10% (about 500 samples). The model performance was shown in Fig. 5.

Fig. 5.

ETF and ETF →

It was observed that the BDANN model had a more stable performance in both transfer experiments (Fig. 5). Specifically, when transferring from GP to ETF, BDANN constantly outperformed DANN regardless of the training size. Also, the DANN model’s performance decreased significantly when the training size was reduced to 10%. On the other hand, when transferring from ETF to GP, both DANN and BDANN performed equally well with a relatively large training set. As the training size decreased, more difference between the two models was observed. The BDANN model was able to achieve an R2
of over 0.60 when using only 10% training data, while the DANN performance dropped significantly to 0.46. These results demonstrated that the BDANN model could generalize well on small training sets.

SECTION V.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a BDANN model to address the domain shift issue for county-level corn yield prediction. By applying adversarial learning and Bayesian inference, BDANN extracted domain-invariant and task-informative features from source and target domains. Experiments in two ecoregions over the U.S. corn belt demonstrated that BDANN had better transferability than RF, DNN, and DANN. Moreover, the results also showed that the BDANN model was able to perform well using relatively small training sets. To further improve the applicability of our model in distant domains with large domain shifts, strategies, such as partial domain adaptation [26] and multisource domain adaptation [27], will be explored to avoid negative transfer effects caused by irrelevant source samples.
