48   Abstract
49
50   Africa’s food security is increasingly threatened by climate change and population growth. High-resolution
51   yield data are vital for precision agriculture and climate adaptation, yet much of the continent lacks sufficient
52   monitoring due to limited ground data.This study presents first high-resolution (250 m), continent-wide
53   maize yield prediction framework for 42 African countries and a novel yield disaggregation method using
54   Net Primary Productivity (NPP) to spatially downscale national-level FAO yield statistics, creating fine-
55   scale training data for supervised learning. A comprehensive feature set of 296 variables was constructed by
56   integrating multi-source Earth observation, climate, and soil data. The framework evaluates multiple
57   machine learning and deep learning models- including XGBoost, LightGBM, a hybrid deep neural network
58   (HDNN), and, for the first time in this context, the Tabular Prior-data Fitted Network (TabPFN), a tabular
59   foundation model. Using an expanding-window temporal cross-validation strategy, XGBoost achieved the
60   highest temporal R² (0.78), while TabPFN demonstrated superior spatial generalization and the lowest mean
61   absolute percentage error (MAPE ≈ 25%). Causal inference and ablation analyses underscored the predictive
62   importance of vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI, NDWI), drought metrics, and soil properties. Model outputs
63   showed strong alignment with FAOSTAT-reported national yields (R² > 0.75; MAPE ≈ 26–28%),
64   highlighting the reliability of the proposed approach. Despite known limitations- such as reliance on proxy-
65   based disaggregation and the use of coarse-resolution climate inputs- this work provides a novel and scalable
66   framework for yield monitoring in data-scarce regions. It also marks the first application of tabular
67   foundation models in continental-scale agricultural prediction, opening new directions for high-resolution,
68   data-efficient crop yield prediction.

83    1     Introduction
 84   Africa has shown significant progress in improving cereal crop yields over the past decades, primarily driven
 85   by increased use of fertilizers, high-yield seeds, and technological advancements. Average yields have
 86   doubled from about 1.1 t ha-1 in the 1960s to 2.2 t ha-1 in the 1990s, further increasing to 4 t ha-1 in 2020
 87   (FAOSTAT, 2021; Le Roux and Cilliers, 2025). Though the increase has been substantial, Africa’s
 88   productivity is still far behind the global average in terms of agricultural land productivity. Although the
 89   general trend in cereal crop yields is upward, there is substantial interannual and spatial variability. This
 90   variability is largely explained by fluctuations in climate (Ray et al., 2015; Lesk et al., 2022), differences in
 91   soil quality, and variations in management practices (Tilman et al., 2011; Mueller et al., 2012; Fan et al.,
 92   2011). Countries like Egypt and South Africa achieve cereal yields exceeding 5 t ha-1, while countries such
 93   as Namibia, Niger, and Mozambique have yields below 1 t ha-1, primarily due to limited irrigation,
 94   agricultural inputs, and access to technology (FAO, 2023). High-resolution crop yield maps are essential for
 95   assessing food security risks, identifying untapped production potential, and understanding land use
 96   dynamics, the impacts of extreme weather events, and the localized effects of climate change (Grassini et
 97   al., 2024). In the absence of comprehensive ground measurements, crop yield datasets are increasingly
 98   generated using dynamic crop models, remote sensing and through machine learning (ML) techniques.
 99
100   ML methods have emerged as powerful tools for crop yield prediction in addition to traditional statistical
101   and process-based modelling approaches (Leng and Hall, 2020; Leroux et al., 2019; Adisa et al., 2019;
102   Asamoah et al., 2024). Particularly, ML has enabled the integration of spatially disaggregated data- satellite
103   imagery, soil maps, and localized climate records- with flexible algorithms to predict yields at
104   unprecedented resolutions (Tamás et al., 2023; Kheir et al., 2024).
105   Studies across Africa demonstrate that tree-based ensemble ML models such as Random Forest and Gradient
106   Boosting excel at capturing nonlinear relationships between environmental variables- such as climate, soil,
107   and remotely sensed vegetation indices- and crop yields, achieving R² values of up to 0.63 in countries like
108   Kenya and Zambia (Kaneko et al., 2019) and root mean square error (RMSE) values as low as 1.76-3.94 t
109   ha-1 in regional potato yield studies (Tamayo-Vera et al., 2025). Random Forest models applied to MODIS
110   NDVI data achieved a correlation of 0.76 in Zambia. In contrast, XGBoost models reduced prediction errors
111   to less than 2 t ha-1 in South Asia (Prodhan et al., 2021) in crops like rice, wheat, and maize.
112
113   More recently, deep learning techniques such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-
114   Term Memory (LSTM) networks have gained increased adoption due to their potential to achieve better
115   performance (Kumar et al., 2023; Srivastava et al., 2022). However, they typically require a larger amount
116   of data than traditional ML models to achieve better performance. This has been a major technical obstacle
117   in data-scarce African countries (Safonova et al., 2023). Transfer learning could help mitigate the limitations
118   posed by small training datasets, enabling robust forecasts even under changing climatic conditions
119   (Morales and Villalobos, 2023). Yet, how well model transferability can be kept under different conditions
120   has still been understudied with deep learning for yield predictions (Ma et al., 2023; Stiller et al., 2024).
121   In recent years, deep learning models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-
122   Term Memory (LSTM) networks have gained traction for yield prediction, offering improved accuracy in
123   some cases (Kaneko et al., 2019; Kumar et al., 2023; Srivastava et al., 2022). However, their high data
124   demands pose a major challenge in data-scarce regions like Africa. Transfer learning has been proposed as
125   a solution, enabling model generalization with limited training data under shifting climatic conditions
126   (Morales and Villalobos, 2023). Yet, the effectiveness of deep learning models in low-data environments
127   and their transferability across agro-ecological zones remains understudied (Ma et al., 2023; Stiller et al.,
128   2024).
                                                                                                             4

129   Foundation models represent a promising new frontier in deep learning. Introduced in 2021, these models
130   are trained on broad, diverse data sets using self-supervised learning and can be adapted to a variety of
131   downstream tasks with minimal additional data (Bommasani et al., 2021). In the tabular domain, TabPFN
132   (Tabular Prior-data Fitted Network) is a recent foundation model capable of few-shot learning for structured
133   data prediction (Hollmann et al., 2025; Hoo et al., 2025). Unlike conventional deep networks, TabPFN is
134   pre-trained on synthetic tasks and can make robust inferences from small, structured datasets. Despite their
135   potential, the use of tabular foundation models in crop yield prediction remains largely unexplored.
136   A comprehensive comparison of conventional and emerging ML techniques- including tree-based
137   ensembles, deep learning, and foundation models- is essential for identifying the most reliable and data-
138   efficient approaches for yield mapping. Such benchmarking can inform the best practices in data-scarce
139   regions and guide the development of interpretable, high-resolution yield prediction frameworks.
140   This work aims to contribute to this research agenda by advancing a scalable, data-driven mapping pipeline
141   to support food security in Africa- particularly in highly vulnerable areas of sub-Saharan Africa where yield
142   variability and data gaps are most pronounced (De Martínez Christiane et al., 2016). While existing datasets
143   such as M3Crops, GAEZ, SPAM, and RAY2012 provide valuable insights, they are limited by coarse
144   resolution, fragmented temporal coverage, or heavy reliance on model-based assumptions (Monfreda et al.,
145   2008; Fischer et al., 2021; Ray et al., 2012; You et al., 2014; Cao et al., 2025). Even more recent hybrid
146   datasets like GDHY, which integrate satellite remote sensing with agricultural census data, remain
147   constrained by their 0.5° spatial resolution (~50 km), limiting their utility for localized agricultural planning
148   (Iizumi and Sakai, 2020). Considering these limitations, there is an urgent need to develop a high-resolution,
149   temporally continuous, and scalable yield prediction framework that can operate effectively in data-
150   constrained settings.
151
152   Accordingly, the main objectives of this study are:
153
154   1) To develop a high-resolution (~250 m) maize yield prediction framework for Africa by integrating Earth
155      Observation (EO) data (climate and soil variables) with advanced ML models trained on spatially
156      disaggregated synthetic yield data derived from Net Primary Productivity (NPP), to generate high-
157      resolution yield maps.
158
159   2) To evaluate and compare the performance of multiple ML and deep learning models, including, tree-
160      based ensemble, traditional deep learning in forecasting maize yield across African countries, using
161      rigorous time-aware cross-validation techniques, and to assess the effectiveness of tabular foundation
162      models in crop yield prediction.
163
164   3) To assess the contribution of different environmental and agronomic variables (e.g., climate, vegetation
165      indices, drought indices, soil properties) to maize yield variability based on causal inference techniques.
166
167   2     Study Area and Datasets

168   2.1   Study area
169
170   Africa, the second largest and second-most populous continent in the world, comprises a diverse array of
171   physical, climatic, and ecological zones that make it highly variable in terms of agricultural potential. Maize
172   is one of the most important staple crops across the continent, both in terms of consumption and cultivation.
173   This study focuses on 42 maize-producing countries in Africa, selected based on their contribution to
174   regional maize output, availability of agricultural data, and geographic representation across different agro-
175   ecological zones. The study area extends longitudinally from approximately 17°W to 51°E and latitudinally
                                                                                                           5

176   from 35°N to 35°S, encompassing countries from North Africa (Algeria, Egypt, Libya, Morocco, Sudan,
177   Mauritania), West Africa (Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Mali, Niger, Nigeria,
178   Senegal, Sierra Leone, Togo), Central Africa (Cameroon, Central African Republic, Chad, Congo,
179   Democratic Republic of the Congo) East Africa (Burundi, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, Somalia,
180   Uganda, United Republic of Tanzania), Southern Africa (Angola, Botswana, Lesotho, Madagascar, Malawi,
181   Mozambique, Namibia, South Africa, Zambia, Zimbabwe). This vast spatial coverage captures the
182   variability in climatic and environmental conditions that influence maize productivity across the continents.
183   Africa’s diverse physiography, with elevations ranging from below 500 m to nearly 6,000 m, significantly
184   influences agricultural patterns. North Africa features mostly low-lying plains and isolated mountain ranges
185   like the Sahara and the Atlas. West and Central Africa consist of low to mid-elevation plains and plateaus,
186   such as the Sahel and Congo Basin. East Africa is marked by high elevations, including the Ethiopian
187   Highlands and Rift Valley, reaching up to 5,895 m. Southern Africa features elevated plateaus and
188   escarpments, including the Drakensberg and Highveld regions. These zones span a wide range of
189   agroclimatic regions, including arid and semi-arid zones, tropical rainforests, savannahs, highlands, and
190   coastal belts. The substantial latitudinal extent of the study area results in marked climatic variation across
191   countries, as reflected in the distribution of Köppen climate zones (Fig. 1a). Soil properties across Africa
192   exhibit significant spatial variability, which influences maize cultivation in terms of root development,
193   nutrient availability, and water retention. North Africa has predominantly sandy to silty loam soil texture,
194   7.5–8.5 pH, <1% organic carbon, and low to moderate water-holding capacity. West Africa has a larger range
195   of topsoil texture (sandy loam to clay), 5.0–7.0 pH, 1–2% organic carbon, and mostly moderate to high
196   water holding capacity. Central Africa has a clay loam to clay soil texture, 4.5–6.0 pH, >2% organic carbon,
197   and high-water holding capacity. East Africa has a sandy loam to clay soil texture, 5.0–7.5 pH, 1–3% organic
198   carbon, and moderate to high water holding capacity. Southern Africa has a sandy loam to clay soil texture,
199   5.0–7.0 pH, 0.5-2% organic carbon, and low to moderate water holding capacity.

210   2.2   Maize yield and crop calendar data
211
212   This study utilizes maize yield data from FAOSTAT for the period 2009–2022, aligning with the availability
213   of Net Primary Productivity (NPP) data, which is employed for spatial disaggregation of crop yields (see
214   Section 3.1). To effectively link yield estimates with phenological phases, we incorporate crop calendar
215   information from (Sacks et al., 2010), which provides global gridded data on planting and harvesting periods.
216   This dataset includes key variables such as the start, end, and duration of both planting and harvesting
217   periods. For our analysis, we focused on the primary maize growing season, labelled as the 'major' season
218   in the dataset. The crop calendar raster was clipped and processed to extract country-level planting and
219   harvesting day-of-year (DOY) values across Africa. Given the substantial spatial variability in cropping
220   practices, we aggregated the raster values using the mode to represent the most dominant planting and
221   harvesting dates within each country. This approach ensures consistent and representative identification of
222   maize growth periods for subsequent analysis with Earth Observation (EO) data.

224   2.3 Earth Observation (EO) datasets
225   In this study, a range of crop yield determinants was derived from open-source climate, soil, and remote
226   sensing data products (refer to Table 1 for the details).
227
228   Climate data:
229   Climate data plays a significant role in crop yield prediction by providing essential information about
230   environmental conditions, including long-term climatic trends and short-term extreme weather events
231   (Chinnasamy and Ashok, 2014). Accordingly, we extracted temperature, precipitation, solar radiation, wind
232   component, and volumetric soil water datasets from the ERA5-Land daily aggregated climate reanalysis
233   dataset, which is hosted on the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform (Muñoz-Sabater et al., 2021).
234   Reference evapotranspiration (ET₀) also serves as a key indicator of crop water demand and serves as an
235   input to the calculation of drought stress indices, thereby contributing to better crop yield estimates (Dong
236   et al., 2024). The ET₀ data utilized in this study were derived from the WAPOR daily reference
237   evapotranspiration dataset, accessible through the GEE platform (Blatchford et al., 2020). The ERA5-Land
238   monthly aggregated dataset was used to derive drought indicators (e.g., SPI-1 and SPI-3).
239
240   Vegetation indices:
241   Multi-spectral remote sensing data provides reliable insights into crop biophysical parameters across various
242   growth stages by capturing distinct spectral signatures (Muruganantham et al., 2022). For instance, the
243   Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is widely used to assess crop productivity and identify
244   physiological stress, while the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) provides valuable insights into
245   plant and soil moisture levels. We derived these indices from the MODIS Terra daily NDVI and NDWI
246   datasets to improve the accuracy of maize yield prediction.
247
248   Soil and agricultural management data:
249   Soil grids data developed by (Poggio et al., 2021), provided spatial characterization of key soil chemical
250   properties such as sand, silt, clay, pH, nitrogen, and soil organic carbon content etc., to a soil depth of a
251   maximum 2m. These properties are critical for accurate crop yield predictions, as they significantly
252   influence spatial yield variability. Irrigation data helps differentiate between rainfed and irrigated cropland
253   regions, leading to more accurate yield estimates. To include this information in our training dataset, we
254   incorporated the Landsat-derived Global Rainfed and Irrigated-Cropland Product (LGRIP) developed by (P.
255   Teluguntla et al., 2023a).
256
257   Spatial disaggregation dataset:
258   To spatially limit the sample extraction process from all EO feature layers only to cropland areas, we applied
259   a cropland mask based on the Africa Cropland Extent 2019 dataset from Digital Earth Africa. Lastly, the
260   disaggregation of crop yield from the country scale to the pixel scale, a unique aspect of this study, is
261   achieved by using Net Primary Productivity (NPP) as a proxy for crop yield at the local scale. NPP quantifies
262   the net primary production of crops, with higher values indicating greater biomass accumulation, which
263   correlates with increased yields and enhances the understanding of agroecosystem dynamics (Zhang et al.,
264   2020). For this purpose, we employed the WAPOR Dekadal Net Primary Production 2.0 dataset, available
265   on the GEE platform, for crop yield disaggregation (FAO, 2018). The crop yield disaggregation
266   methodology is detailed in Section 3, and a comprehensive compilation of all data sources and metadata is
267   provided in Table 1.

273   3     Materials and Methods

274   In this study, we developed a robust, high-resolution maize yield prediction framework spanning 42 African
275   countries from 2009 to 2022- one of the most spatially and temporally comprehensive efforts to date for the
276   continent. Leveraging 296 features- including dekadal remote sensing and climate time-series data aligned
277   with maize phenology, and high-resolution soil variables- we addressed key data sparsity challenges using
278   a spatial disaggregation method that converts FAO national yield statistics into pixel-level labels via Net
279   Primary Productivity (NPP). While based on prior methods (Iizumi et al., 2013), our approach integrates
280   crop calendars, noise-filtered cropland masks, and quality control thresholds to enhance the reliability and
281   generalizability of disaggregated labels. To ensure temporal rigour, we employed an expanding-window
282   cross-validation strategy and trained multiple machine learning and deep learning models using mean
283   squared error (MSE) loss. Performance was evaluated across both pixel and country scales using multiple




284   metrics. This methodological pipeline enables scalable and interpretable yield forecasting, supporting more
285   precise agricultural monitoring across data-constrained regions in Africa. The complete methodological
286   pipeline, including data preprocessing and model development, is detailed in the following section and
287   illustrated in
288   Figure 2. End-to-end framework for maize yield prediction comprises six rigorously designed components:
289   (1) Multi-source data preprocessing, where climate variables, vegetation indices, irrigation layers, and soil
290   properties are harmonized into phenology-aware time-series and static features; (2) Dataset construction
291   with 296 features- 286 time-series variables aggregated at dekadal resolution and 10 static spatial predictors-
292   aligned to crop growth stages using country-specific planting calendars; (3) Label generation via a novel
293   application of NPP-based disaggregation of FAO national yields to pixel level; (4) Model training using a
294   temporally-expanding cross-validation strategy across four forecasting architectures: tree-based (XGBoost,
295   LightGBM), deep learning (Hybrid Deep Neural Network combining LSTM and dense layers), and TabPFN
296   foundation models; (5) Model evaluation through spatial and country-level metrics (MSE, RMSE, MAE,
297   R², MAPE), ensuring robustness and scalability; and (6) Post-hoc interpretability using both ablation
298   analysis and causal inference (backdoor criterion), culminating in the generation of actionable, high-
                                                                                                          10

299   resolution yield maps. This integrated pipeline bridges explainability, generalization, and fine-scale spatial
300   resolution, enabling practical application in data-scarce agricultural regions.
301
302   3.1      Spatial disaggregation of FAO yield using NPP-based modelling
303
304   The disaggregation process began with spatial sampling across verified cropland areas in Africa. To improve
305   label quality, we applied connected component analysis to the cropland extent mask, removing isolated
306   clusters smaller than 100 pixels that were likely misclassified. This filtering step reduced spatial noise and
307   improved land-use reliability as there was a spatial resolution mismatch between the cropland mask layer
308   (~10m) and the NPP data layer (~250m), which resulted in reduced probability of sampling from land use
309   classes other than croplands. We then calculated cropland extent per country and allocated sample points
310   proportionally, yielding a stratified, spatially balanced training set of ~100,000 points across the continent.
311   Countries with fewer than 50 cropland samples- due to limited agricultural area- were excluded to avoid
312   unreliable model training and statistical artefacts. This preprocessing ensured methodological rigour in both
313   spatial representativeness and label integrity for downstream model development.

366   3.2 Data preparation for machine learning model training
367
368   Data preparation is a significant and sensitive step in our modelling framework, as the length of the growth
369   period varies across countries. To account for these variations, we processed the data iteratively for each
370   country, the planting and harvest dates accordingly (refer to section 2.2 for details). Time-series variables
371   (e.g., climate and remote sensing data) were extracted at a decadal interval from 2009 to 2022, aligning with
372   the availability of NPP data. The spatially disaggregated yield estimates, derived from the NPP data, served
373   as training labels for our model.
374   To incorporate edaphic factors, we extracted high-resolution soil attributes from the SoilGrids 250m v2.0
375   database at all sampled locations. Given the multivariate nature of the dataset across various depths, we
376   conducted a Pearson correlation analysis to eliminate multicollinearity and improve model efficiency. We
377   removed 52 highly correlated variables (r > 0.85) and retained seven non-redundant soil predictors with
378   agronomic relevance, including bulk density, cation exchange capacity, clay content, and nitrogen
379   concentration (refer to Table 1). This feature selection was essential for ensuring both computational
380   tractability and interpretability of the model across heterogeneous soil environments.
381   Time-series features- including NDVI, NDWI, and reference evapotranspiration (ET₀)- were compiled at
382   dekadal resolution, covering up to 22 intervals based on the longest observed maize growth cycle across
383   Africa. Missing values, primarily due to remote sensing artefacts, were imputed using linear interpolation
384   to maintain temporal continuity. To enhance the representation of climate extremes, we computed
385   Standardized Precipitation Indices (SPI-1 and SPI-3) over each country’s crop growth period, capturing
386   short- and medium-term drought dynamics. We further incorporated irrigation extent from the LGRIP global
387   database, allowing the models to distinguish between rainfed and irrigated conditions- an often overlooked
388   yet critical factor in large-scale yield forecasting (refer to Table 1).

390   3.3   Expanding window cross-validation and model optimization
391
392   To ensure a robust and generalizable yield forecasting framework, we evaluated four distinct modelling
393   approaches: gradient-boosted tree models (XGBoost, LightGBM), a hybrid deep neural network (HDNN)
394   combining LSTM and dense layers for joint processing of temporal and static inputs, and TabPFN- a
395   transformer-based probabilistic foundation model designed for tabular datasets (see Section 3.4 for each
396   model detailed description). The inclusion of TabPFN is particularly novel in the context of crop yield
397   modelling, offering a pretrained architecture capable of rapid inference with minimal data. Hyperparameter
398   optimization for XGBoost and LightGBM was conducted using the Optuna framework, enabling efficient
399   and reproducible parameter tuning via Bayesian optimization (Akiba et al., 2019).
400   To guard against overfitting and inflated performance estimates- a common pitfall in data-driven models-
401   we implemented an expanding window temporal cross-validation scheme. Unlike conventional random or
402   k-fold approaches, this method respects the temporal structure of agricultural time-series data. Training
403   begins with the earliest year (2009) and sequentially expands to include more historical data, while testing
404   is performed on the immediately following year. For example, Fold 1 trains in 2009 and tests in 2010; Fold
405   2 includes 2009-2010 for training and 2011 for testing, continuing until the final fold evaluates 2022. This
406   setup allows us to rigorously assess model performance under realistic, year-ahead forecasting conditions
407   and inter-temporal yield variability. The full cross-validation structure is depicted in Figure 4.

436   and year to ensure a representative subset, as the number of data points increased with each fold in the actual

571   3.5   Ablation study and causal discovery
572
573   To rigorously assess the influence of different environmental and management variables on yield prediction
574   accuracy, we conducted a structured ablation study on the XGBoost model. Unlike generic feature
575   importance methods, our approach uniquely groups input variables into interpretable thematic categories—
576   soil properties, minimum and maximum temperatures, precipitation, solar radiation, volumetric soil water
577   content, drought indices, reference evapotranspiration (ET₀), NDVI, NDWI, and irrigation—enabling a
578   domain-informed evaluation of their collective contribution. Each group was systematically removed in
579   isolation to quantify its impact on predictive performance, providing actionable insights into which
580   biophysical signals are most critical for accurate yield estimation.
581   To ensure methodological rigour, all ablation experiments were conducted using an identical set of
582   hyperparameters optimized via the Optuna framework and evaluated under the same expanding window
583   cross-validation protocol as the baseline model. This setup yielded 12 controlled training scenarios, each
584   representing the absence of one feature group. Resulting outputs were stored and benchmarked against the
585   full model to determine the marginal loss in accuracy attributable to each exclusion. This systematic
586   evaluation enhances the interpretability and trustworthiness of the model, offering valuable guidance for
587   both scientific understanding and feature selection in operational forecasting systems.
588   To go beyond mere correlation and assess whether the machine learning model captured true causal signals
589   in the data, we implemented a robust causal discovery framework grounded in structural causal theory.
590   Specifically, we applied a backdoor-adjustment-based inference approach to uncover the underlying cause-
591   and-effect relationships between key environmental drivers- such as climate variables, soil properties, and
592   vegetation indices- and both the observed (disaggregated) and predicted crop yields. This dual-path analysis
593   allowed us to not only estimate real-world causal effects but also to test the fidelity of the XGBoost model
594   in preserving these causal structures, a novel and critical step in validating model interpretability and
595   trustworthiness.
596   Guided by domain expertise and exploratory analysis, we identified appropriate confounding variable sets
597   for each treatment variable, then constructed two parallel causal graphs using the DoWhy library
598   (https://www.pywhy.org/dowhy/v0.12/): one targeting the actual yield and the other using the XGBoost-
599   predicted yield as the outcome. By adjusting for confounders satisfying the backdoor criterion, we computed
600   the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) to isolate the marginal causal influence of each treatment. This rigorous
601   approach enables a more principled evaluation of model alignment with real-world agronomic processes,
602   enhancing both the scientific robustness and the practical utility of data-driven decision tools in agriculture.
603   For a given treatment 𝑇, we adjusted for a set of confounders 𝑍 that satisfy the backdoor criterion-i.e., they
604   block all non-causal paths from 𝑇 to crop yield 𝑌 in the causal graph. The causal effect of interest is
605   quantified as the average treatment effect (ATE):
606
607                             ATE = 𝐸[ 𝑌 ∣ 𝑑𝑜(𝑇 = 1) ] − 𝐸[ 𝑌 ∣ 𝑑𝑜(𝑇 = 0) ] (𝐸𝑞. 15)
608   The ATE quantifies the expected change in the outcome (e.g., yield) if we change the treatment from 0 to 1
609   for every data point in the population. The interventional quantity can be estimated from observational data
610   by applying the backdoor adjustment formula:

614   both the observed yields and the XGBoost-predicted yields to assess whether the predictive model preserves

617
618   Commonly used validation metrics for regression tasks–particularly in crop yield prediction–include mean
619   absolute error (MAE), mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), coefficient of
620   determination (R²), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) (Deshmukh et al., 2022). These metrics
621   are essential for evaluating both the performance and accuracy of the predictive models developed in this
622   study. While conceptually related, each metric provides unique insights into different aspects of model
623   behavior and error characteristics.
624   MSE calculates the average squared difference between predicted and actual yield values, placing greater
625   importance on larger errors, however, it is less interpretable due to its squared units. RMSE, the square root
626   of MSE, offers a more interpretable metric by retaining the same units as the original data, but it remains
627   sensitive to outliers. MAE computes the average of absolute differences between predicted and actual values,
628   providing a more intuitive measure and being robust to outliers, though it does not penalize large errors as
629   severely.
630   R² represents the proportion of variance in the actual data that is explained by the model. While it indicates
631   the model's ability to fit the data, it does not always reflect prediction accuracy and can be misleading,
632   particularly in cases of overfitting. MAPE expresses errors as percentages, making it useful for comparing
633   models across different spatial and temporal contexts. However, it requires caution, as small actual values
634   (approaching zero) can result in inflated error rates.

857   To quantify the sensitivity of individual features and establish their causal relationship with yield, we

860   except in Somalia, Zimbabwe, Botswana, and Morocco. The ablation study (Fig. 9) quantifies the sensitivity

865   the data, and the MAPE value increased by 1% when removing NDWI. Other features such as soil properties,
866   wind components, minimum temperature, and volumetric soil water also emerged as crucial parameters for
867   accurate yield estimates. As the model was trained using rigorous cross-validation splits, it gives balanced
868   weight to every individual parameter, avoiding over-reliance on a single feature, which commonly occurs
869   in an overfitting scenario. Therefore, the exclusion of other climatic variables such as precipitation, radiation,
870   and ET₀ leads to minimal or even slightly improved performance, suggesting that their contribution may be
871   either marginal or potentially redundant given the presence of more informative features.

895   5       Discussion
896   5.1   Significance and adaptability of high-resolution maize yield prediction mapping framework
897
898   The ability to generate continent-scale, high-resolution crop yield prediction maps marks a major
899   advancement in agricultural monitoring, especially for regions like Africa where field-level data are scarce
900   and yield heterogeneity is high. In this study, we present a novel framework that combines synthetic yield
901   disaggregation with machine learning and Earth Observation (EO) data, offering an innovative solution to
902   the chronic spatial data gaps in African agriculture. Unlike traditional assessments based on coarse national-
903   level statistics, our method captures the fine-scale spatial and temporal variability critical for identifying
904   yield gaps, evaluating food security risks, and assessing localized climate impacts (Aramburu-Merlos et al.,
905   2024). Our approach generates pixel-level maize yield estimates using a synthetic disaggregation technique
906   driven by Net Primary Productivity (NPP) and multi-source EO data. The resulting high-resolution maize
907   yield maps (Fig. 11) demonstrate strong consistency with officially reported national statistics, validating
908   the method’s effectiveness. By simulating disaggregated training targets, we enable the use of advanced
909   models such as XGBoost and TabPFN, which require large training volumes and show strong performance
910   across diverse agro-climatic zones. Notably, our analysis reveals significant regional differences in model
911   generalization, reinforcing the importance of spatially explicit model evaluation beyond administrative
912   boundaries. Crucially, the model training pipeline was built around crop development phase features and
913   implemented using a rigorous expanding window cross-validation strategy. This approach avoids common
914   pitfalls associated with random or stratified splits that can introduce temporal leakage and artificially inflate
915   performance. By preserving the chronological structure of training and testing data, we simulate realistic
916   forecasting scenarios that better reflect operational decision-making conditions under evolving climatic and
917   environmental pressures. To our knowledge, this is the first study to produce high-resolution yield
918   predictions at a pan-African scale using a fully synthetic yield framework. Most prior efforts have focused
919   on single countries or regions and relied on labor-intensive field yield surveys (Leroux, et al., 2019; Adisa
920   et al., 2019; Asamoah et al., 2024). In contrast, we combine scalable synthetic data generation with a
921   temporally consistent evaluation protocol, retraining models in each fold to prevent overfitting. Our results
922   meet or exceed the accuracies reported in previous studies. For instance, (Leroux, et al., 2019) reported R²
923   values of 0.42–0.53 in West Africa; (Adisa et al., 2019) reported 0.81 in Ghana; and (Asamoah, et al., 2024)
924   achieved 0.67–0.86 in Southern Africa for 2018–2019. However, because these studies use different datasets
925   and timeframes, direct year-to-year comparisons are not meaningful.
926   Instead, our framework provides a more generalizable performance summary across years and countries. As
927   shown in Figure 7, our models explain 78–79% of yield variance continent-wide, with annual performance
928   ranging from 50% to over 90%, depending on climatic conditions and data density. These findings
929   underscore the robustness, scalability, and policy relevance of our yield mapping framework in supporting
930   data-driven agricultural planning, early warning systems, and sustainable intensification efforts across
931   Africa. Performance nevertheless degrades in anomalous seasons, indicating that future work should
932   incorporate hybrid approaches that blend process-based crop models with machine- and deep-learning
933   techniques to better capture extremes.

952   5.2   Importance of vegetation indices and climate signals in yield prediction
953
954   Our findings underscore the critical role of remote sensing variables- particularly vegetation indices- in
955   improving the accuracy, interpretability, and robustness of crop yield predictions across heterogeneous
956   African landscapes. Among these, NDVI and NDWI emerged as the most influential predictors, as
957   demonstrated through both ablation studies and causal effect estimation. When these indices were removed,
958   predictive performance declined noticeably, confirming their essential contribution. These results align with
959   earlier research: for instance, (Xu and Katchova, 2019) showed that NDVI captures nonlinear relationships
960   with soybean yield across phenological stages, while (Tamás et al., 2023) and (Teal et al., 2006)
961   demonstrated its effectiveness in forecasting corn grain yield. Our work builds on these insights by not only
962   validating their utility at continental scale but also quantifying their causal impact on yield- thus advancing
963   understanding of how vegetation health and moisture availability mediate crop outcomes under variable
964   climate conditions.
965   In addition to vegetation indices, this study employed Net Primary Productivity (NPP) as both a
966   disaggregation proxy and an ecologically meaningful indicator of productivity. This dual role is central to
967   our approach: NPP guided the spatial disaggregation of national yield statistics into high-resolution synthetic
968   targets and provided a biophysically relevant feature for yield modelling. This enabled machine learning
969   models to learn from both macro-level productivity patterns and fine-scale environmental variability,
970   enhancing predictive power across diverse agro-ecological contexts. Our causal analysis further revealed
971   that climate extremes- particularly drought indicators such as SPI-1 and SPI-3 have strong negative effects
972   on yield. This insight is consistent with the observed decline in model accuracy between 2019 and 2021,
973   which coincided with widespread rainfall deficits across sub-Saharan Africa (Ayugi et al., 2022).
974   These events exposed vulnerabilities in rainfed agricultural systems and demonstrated the urgent need for
975   climate-resilient strategies, including early warning systems, drought-tolerant crops, and adaptive land
976   management.
977   Taken together, these findings highlight the novel integration of causal inference, remote sensing, and
978   climate extremes in our framework. By embedding such variables into yield prediction models, we enable
979   more accurate forecasting, enhance explanatory depth, and provide actionable insights for resilience
980   planning under increasing climatic uncertainty.

982   5.3   Potential of tabular foundation models for crop yield prediction
983
984   The advent of Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs), particularly the TabPFN model, represents a paradigm
985   shift in data-driven approaches to crop yield prediction. In contrast to traditional machine learning and deep
986   learning models that necessitate extensive dataset-specific training, TabPFN employs in-context learning
987   and is pretrained on millions of synthetically generated datasets derived from structural causal models
988   (SCMs). This pretraining strategy allows TabPFN to generalize effectively across diverse tabular prediction
989   tasks, requiring little to no task-specific fine-tuning.
990   Our results indicate that TabPFN delivers competitive performance even when trained on substantially
991   smaller datasets compared to other models such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and HDNN. A key challenge in
992   crop yield prediction, particularly in developing countries such as those in Africa, is the limited availability
993   of large, high-quality training datasets. The absence of openly accessible and large-scale datasets suitable
994   for deep learning has significantly constrained the advancement of accurate predictive models for crop yield
995   (Lin et al., 2024). In this context, TabPFN offers a distinct advantage: unlike conventional deep learning
996   approaches, it does not rely on large training datasets. Despite its minimal data requirements, TabPFN
997   consistently demonstrated strong spatial prediction accuracy (Fig. 6 and Fig. 11), especially in regions
998   characterized by data scarcity and agro-climatic heterogeneity. Notably, it outperformed traditional models
                                                                                                           31

 999   in reducing mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) and more effectively captured fine-scale yield
1000   variability across the African continent with higher spatial resolution.
1001   TabPFN also has the unique advantage of producing probabilistic predictions, which sets it apart from most
1002   traditional models. This means it can estimate the uncertainty in its predictions, which is critical for
1003   agricultural decision-making under climatic volatility. Since crop yields are influenced by many
1004   unpredictable factors, having a model that reflects this uncertainty can improve risk assessments and support
1005   better early warning systems. That said, Tabular Foundation Models like TabPFN are not without limitations.
1006   T|he current version used in our study can handle only up to 10,000 rows and 500 columns, which can be a
1007   constraint when working with larger or more complex datasets. To stay within this limit, we had to sample
1008   10,000 data points from each training fold. As the amount of data and the number of years increased, it
1009   became harder to ensure that the sample fully represented the original dataset. This has likely contributed to
1010   TabPFN's drop in performance in recent years, where models like XGBoost performed better. Additionally,
1011   because TabPFN is based on a transformer architecture and uses a full forward pass for each prediction, it
1012   can be quite demanding in terms of GPU memory and computational resources-especially if applied at scale
1013   or in real-time settings.
1014   Despite their current limitations, Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs) represent a promising direction for
1015   crop yield prediction. Their ability to generalize well across diverse regions, perform effectively with limited
1016   training data, and provide built-in uncertainty estimates makes them especially valuable in data-scarce and
1017   agro-ecologically diverse settings. As future releases of these models overcome constraints related to input
1018   size and feature dimensionality, their potential for large-scale, high-resolution agricultural monitoring will
1019   likely grow.
1020
1021   5.4   Limitation and uncertainty of the study
1022
1023   A fundamental aspect of our study involves disaggregating national-level FAO maize yield statistics into
1024   high-resolution, pixel-level yield estimates by employing Net Primary Productivity (NPP) as a proxy. This
1025   strategy enables the development of spatially explicit models at scale in contexts where field-level
1026   observations are lacking. However, it also introduces several limitations. Notably, the availability of NPP
1027   data is restricted both in time and space. Accessing long-term NPP time series data across Africa- or globally
1028   -is particularly challenging due to limited open-source data availability. Consequently, our analysis was
1029   confined to the 2009–2022 period, corresponding to the temporal coverage of the WAPOR dekadal NPP
1030   dataset, which is available exclusively for Africa. This restriction precluded the use of earlier FAO yield
1031   records that could have extended the training period and enhanced the model's generalizability. Additionally,
1032   the assumption that NPP exhibits a consistent and linear relationship with maize yield across diverse
1033   agroecological zones may not always be valid. Crop yields are influenced by a variety of factors beyond
1034   biomass production, such as nutrient limitations, pest infestations, and stress conditions occurring after
1035   anthesis. In regions where these factors have a stronger impact, relying on NPP as a proxy for yield
1036   disaggregation may lead to spatial inaccuracies -particularly in ecologically diverse or data-scarce areas.
1037   Secondly, while our model integrates a diverse set of Earth Observation (EO) features- including climate,
1038   vegetation, and soil parameters- some of these inputs are derived from coarse-resolution datasets. For
1039   example, climate data from ERA5-Land are available at an approximate spatial resolution of 9 km, which
1040   is considerably coarser than the 250 m resolution of the final yield estimates. This disparity in spatial scales
1041   can introduce artefacts in the model outputs, particularly in regions characterized by sharp climatic gradients.
1042   Such effects are especially pronounced in low-yielding areas of Nigeria, as illustrated in Figure 11h–i. The
1043   mismatch in resolution may lead to spatial smoothing or misrepresentation of fine-scale microclimatic
1044   conditions that are critical to crop growth and yield formation.
1045
                                                                                                             32

1046   Third, our spatial evaluation results (Figure 6) indicate that model prediction errors are substantially higher
1047   in climatically unstable regions such as the Sahel, the Horn of Africa, and in small countries with complex
1048   terrain. These regions experience highly erratic rainfall, frequent droughts, and diverse agroecological
1049   conditions, often combined with sparse and fragmented agricultural land. Such environmental variability
1050   limits the model’s generalizability, especially when it has been trained on data from more stable agro-
1051   climatic zones such as South Africa and Egypt. Moreover, our cross-validation approach, while robust in
1052   temporal generalization, does not explicitly assess spatial transferability. Since the samples from the same
1053   regions may appear in both the training and testing sets, true out-of-region evaluation remains untested. In
1054   addition, the lack of accurate, high-resolution cropland maps -along with persistent misclassification of
1055   agricultural areas - further introduces noise in the data. This is particularly evident in countries like Lesotho,
1056   Eswatini, Botswana, Somalia, Sudan, Niger, Eritrea, and Libya, where these challenges contribute to higher
1057   prediction MAPE and diminished accuracy.
1058
1059   Fourth, Spatial yield variability may need a much higher resolution than 250 meters due to the small farm
1060   and field sizes (smallholder farming) in several parts of Africa and crops are also grown in mixtures, e.g.
1061   agroforestry, in some places.
1062
1063   Finally, our model does not account for agricultural management practices- such as fertilizer application,
1064   sowing techniques, and pest control -or socio-economic factors like market accessibility and land tenure
1065   arrangements, all of which are known to play a critical role in shaping yield outcomes. The lack of
1066   harmonized, spatially comprehensive datasets capturing these dimensions across Africa presents a key
1067   limitation, preventing a more holistic representation of the factors driving yield variability. As a result, the
1068   model may overlook important context-specific influences, particularly within smallholder farming systems
1069   where such variables are especially consequential.
1070   In sum, while our framework marks a significant step forward in high-resolution, continent-wide crop yield
1071   prediction, several limitations remain. Enhancing the spatial resolution of climate data, extending the
1072   temporal coverage of yield labels, and integrating information on socio-economic and management
1073   practices- alongside addressing persistent data availability challenges- will be crucial for improving the
1074   robustness, reliability, and relevance of yield prediction systems across diverse African agricultural
1075   landscapes and beyond.
1076
1077   6       Conclusion
1078   This study presents a scalable, data-driven framework for high-resolution maize yield prediction across 42
1079   African countries by integrating multi-source Earth Observation (EO) data, synthetic yield disaggregation
1080   based on Net Primary Productivity (NPP), and advanced machine learning techniques. By aligning input
1081   features with crop phenology and employing an expanding window cross-validation strategy, our framework
1082   generates pixel-level yield estimates that capture spatial heterogeneity often masked in administrative-level
1083   statistics.
1084   Among the models evaluated, XGBoost demonstrated strong and consistent temporal performance, while
1085   TabPFN excelled in spatial generalization, particularly in climatically diverse and data-sparse regions. The
1086   incorporation of EO-derived variables- such as NDVI, NDWI, evapotranspiration, and drought indices-
1087   together with irrigation practices and some climate data, significantly enhanced predictive accuracy. Further,
1088   ablation and causal inference analyses helped us better understand how different groups of features
1089   contribute to the model accuracy. Validation against national yield data from FAOSTAT confirmed the
1090   alignment of our high-resolution predictions with officially reported trends. Nonetheless, several limitations
1091   remain. The use of NPP for yield disaggregation, while effective, assumes stable biomass–yield
1092   relationships that may vary across agroecological zones. Temporal constraints in NPP availability restricted
1093   our modelling to 2009-2022, limiting historical comparisons. The coarse spatial resolution of climate inputs
                                                                                                         33

1094   (e.g., ERA5-Land) also introduces spatial mismatches that can reduce pixel-level accuracy. Additionally,
1095   model performance declined in climatically volatile regions such as the Sahel, where sparse cropland
1096   distribution and high interannual variability challenge model generalizability. Architectural limitations of
1097   TabPFN, including fixed training sample sizes and high computational costs, further constrained its
1098   application in more recent years. Looking ahead, enhancing label accuracy through field-level observations
1099   or alternative biophysical proxies could improve both the fidelity and temporal range of disaggregated yield
1100   datasets. Integrating agricultural management variables- such as fertiliser use, planting dates, irrigation
1101   practices, and pest control- alongside socio-economic factors like market access or policy changes, could
1102   significantly boost model interpretability and operational relevance. Future efforts should also explore
1103   downscaling techniques for coarse climate inputs to better align with high-resolution EO data, especially in
1104   ecologically heterogeneous regions. As Tabular Foundation Models continue to evolve, upcoming versions
1105   with higher capacity and scalability may overcome current input limitations, unlocking their full potential
1106   for continent-wide monitoring. Incorporating forecasted climate scenarios will be essential to shift from
1107   retrospective analysis toward real-time forecasting and future yield projections, thereby enhancing early
1108   warning systems and policy responsiveness.
1109   In conclusion, this study demonstrates the feasibility, novelty, and practical value of a high-resolution,
1110   machine learning- enabled prediction framework tailored to Africa’s data-scarce yet climatically dynamic
1111   agricultural landscapes. With continued methodological innovation and deeper data integration, such
1112   approaches offer transformative potential for supporting climate-resilient agricultural planning and
1113   improving food security across the continent.
