ABSTRACT

Agricultural productivity is increasingly threatened by limited arable land, population growth and climate change, emphasising the need for effective crop protection. Deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has shown promise for automatic plant disease detection. However, performance is often limited by insufficient and non-diverse training data. This study addresses this challenge by reorganising and merging two datasets—PlantVillage (PV) and EdenLibrary (EL)—based on disease common names rather than crop-disease pairs to improve generalisation. PV provides a large volume of lab images with limited variability, whereas EL offers fewer but more diverse field images. Three lightweight CNNs, MobileNet-v3, ShuffleNet-v2 and SqueezeNet, were fine-tuned via transfer learning on the reorganised datasets. Results show that disease-common-name-based labelling enhances generalisation to unseen plant species while maintaining performance on known species. Moreover, combining PV and EL improved cross-domain generalisation on the PlantDoc dataset. Ensemble averaging of the three models further boosted performance, with the ternary ensemble outperforming individual and binary models. The ensemble achieved accuracies of 99.93% and 94.46% on seen and unseen species from the PV test set, respectively, and 98.81% and 91.89% on seen and unseen species from the EL dataset. In the cross-dataset evaluation, it reached 67.45% accuracy on the PlantDoc dataset, outperforming individual models by up to 13.09%. These findings highlight the value of dataset integration and ensemble techniques in building robust plant disease detection systems adaptable to diverse real-world scenarios, without requiring additional in-field data.

1 Introduction

In recent years, agriculture has faced significant challenges including the decreasing availability of arable land, population growth and the impacts of climate change, all of which threaten global food security (Ngugi et al. 2021). Addressing these issues requires a sustainable increase in crop productivity, which can be achieved by effectively protecting crops from pests and diseases. In response to these challenges, there has been growing interest in the use of image processing and machine learning techniques for the automatic detection of plant diseases. These technologies have become a highly active area of research, showing great promise for the accurate, rapid, and early identification and classification of plant diseases. In particular, deep learning methods, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been shown to be highly effective for image-based plant disease detection (Ngugi et al. 2021; Rezaei et al. 2024; Shehu et al. 2025).

However, training deep CNNs requires large, diverse, and expertly labelled image datasets, which presents a significant challenge in the agricultural domain, where such datasets are difficult to collect. The few publicly available datasets for plant disease detection are generally small and lack diversity. To address this limitation, many studies employ data augmentation to expand the dataset and use transfer learning, which involves transferring knowledge gained from a general task with abundant data to a specific task with a smaller dataset. Among the available datasets, PlantVillage (Hughes and Salathé 2015) stands out as one of the largest freely accessible collections, with over 54,000 images, and is widely used by researchers. However, the images in PlantVillage are captured under controlled laboratory conditions, typically featuring a single leaf against a uniform background. As a result, CNN models trained solely on this dataset often have limited generalisation capabilities when applied to more complex, real-world field images, where variations in lighting and background are common (Ahmad et al. 2020; Ferentinos 2018).

To address the lack of diversity in training data, new repositories such as EdenLibrary (Mylonas et al. 2022) have emerged, offering datasets with field-captured images, albeit in smaller quantities. Given that these datasets alone are insufficient to train robust CNN models, a recent study (Aarizou and Merah 2022) proposed combining laboratory images from PlantVillage with field images from EdenLibrary for a binary classification task (‘Healthy’ and ‘Unhealthy’ classes). The results showed that this combination improves CNN performance on field images without compromising accuracy on lab images. Building on this approach, our first contribution is to explore the effect of using a combined dataset of PlantVillage and EdenLibrary images on CNN performance in identifying five different plant pathologies, namely bacterial spot, Esca, leaf mould, powdery mildew and spider mites, across 20 different crop species.

However, merging multiple datasets for CNN training requires that these datasets share several common classes. As a matter of fact, PlantVillage and EdenLibrary datasets are both organised based on crop-disease pairings, leading to only a few common classes between them, which limits the study to two diseases. Moreover, studies have shown that using crop-disease pairings can cause models to learn irrelevant features, such as leaf shape or background, rather than focusing on the disease symptoms (Mohanty et al. 2016; Toda and Okura 2019). Additionally, collecting datasets that cover all possible crop-disease pairs is practically impossible (Lee et al. 2020).

To address this issue, Lee et al. (2020) proposed a novel method of organising plant disease datasets by focusing only on the disease names, ignoring the specific plant species. They found that training a VGG16 CNN on a reorganised PlantVillage dataset improved the model's generalisation to new crops and unseen datasets. In our work, we will apply this reorganisation approach to the PlantVillage and EdenLibrary images to increase the number of common classes between them and assess its impact on complex field images, as previous studies have only considered lab images (Lee et al. 2020). As a second contribution in this paper, we will examine how this reorganisation affects disease recognition across new species and in more challenging environments.

To conduct our studies, we employ transfer learning on three state-of-the-art lightweight CNNs: MobileNet-v3 (Howard et al. 2019), SqueezeNet (Iandola et al. 2016) and ShuffleNet-v2 (Ma et al. 2018). These CNNs were chosen for their efficient architectures, making them suitable for deployment in resource-constrained systems such as embedded devices or smartphones. Our goal is to assess the feasibility of developing a plant disease detection system for in-field and farmer-level applications, where only limited computational resources are typically available. We compare the performance of these three models and select the one with the best generalisation capabilities across different image types (lab and field) and datasets.

The main challenge in plant disease detection is the variability and complexity of disease symptoms, which are often subtle and difficult to distinguish visually. Environmental factors such as lighting and background further complicate visual assessments. As a result, individual CNNs often struggle to perform consistently across diverse disease symptoms and environmental conditions (Nutulapati et al. 2024). Some models may excel with certain types of diseases or images but struggle with others that present a higher level of complexity. To enhance the performance of individual models, several studies have proposed the use of ensemble learning techniques (Bruno et al. 2022; Chompookham and Surinta 2021; Nutulapati et al. 2024; Sharma and Kumar 2022; Sharma et al. 2022; Vo et al. 2023). Ensemble learning involves combining the predictions of multiple models to achieve better performance than any single model could reach on its own. One of the most popular ensemble techniques is bootstrap aggregating, or bagging, which consists of training multiple models separately and then combining their predictions through weighted or unweighted averaging, or majority voting (Vo et al. 2023). Our third contribution involves applying the unweighted ensemble averaging technique to the three evaluated CNNs—MobileNet-v3, SqueezeNet and ShuffleNet-v2. We will evaluate and compare all possible combinations of these models to further improve diagnostic accuracy and generalisation on complex field images. This approach is particularly relevant in the context of lightweight models and resource-constrained systems, where the use of more complex and deeper models is not feasible.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Datasets

2.1.1
PlantVillage

PlantVillage1 (PV) (Hughes and Salathé 2015) is a free and public dataset widely used for the automatic detection of plant diseases. It comprises 54,303 expertly labelled images of both healthy and diseased plant leaves. These images are categorised into 38 classes covering 14 plant species and 26 diseases. All images in the PlantVillage database were captured under controlled conditions, featuring a single leaf against a uniform grey or black background. Figure 1 depicts some image samples from the PlantVillage dataset. Using only this database for training a CNN can lead to inaccuracies when classifying real-field data (Ahmad et al. 2020; Ferentinos 2018). To mitigate this issue, we supplement the PlantVillage dataset with the EdenLibrary dataset, which includes images captured in real-field conditions.

2.1.2
EdenLibrary

EdenLibrary2 (EL) (Mylonas et al. 2022) is a platform that offers various open-access crop databases including both proximal and aerial images of diverse plant species. These databases are continuously updated and they currently include up to 200 datasets, covering 28 different crop species and multiple weeds, with 54 datasets featuring diseased crops. A significant advantage of the EL databases is that all images are captured in real-field conditions, under natural illumination and against diverse backgrounds (Figure 2). As a result, the images display entire plants including various parts (leaves, stems, flowers, fruits, etc.), within complex backgrounds (soil, weeds, fallen leaves, sky, etc.).

2.1.3
PlantDoc

PlantDoc3 (PD) (Singh et al. 2020) is a dataset specifically designed for visual plant disease detection. It consists of 2598 images spanning 13 crops and 17 disease classes. As shown in Figure 3, the images in this dataset include field images captured under real-world conditions of illumination and background, as well as some images with simpler backgrounds similar to those in the PV dataset. The distribution of images in PD follows the crop-disease pair distribution, with the same classes of diseases and species as the PV dataset. In this study, we utilise the PD dataset only in the testing phase to evaluate the models on a new and unseen dataset (cross-dataset generalisation).

2.2 Data Preparation

Combining PV and EL images into a single training set requires that both datasets share several classes. Both PV and EL datasets are organised into classes based on crop-disease or crop-healthy pairs, with only six common classes between them. These common classes include only four crops (grape, pepper, potato, tomato) and two diseases (Esca, leaf mould). To address this, we reorganised images from both datasets by considering only the health status of the plant, independently from its species. Unhealthy classes were grouped based on the disease's common (or scientific) name, while all healthy classes were combined into one ‘Healthy’ class. This reorganisation increased the number of classes containing images from both datasets to six: five distinct diseases (bacterial spot, Esca, leaf mould, powdery mildew and spider mites) and one healthy class, covering 20 different species. This approach, initially explored by Lee et al. (2020), demonstrated that training the VGG16 CNN on the restructured PV dataset, which considers diseases independently of crops, enhances generalisation capabilities to new crops and datasets.

Unlike the PV dataset, the EL platform provides datasets with fewer images, but with higher resolution reaching 48 Megapixels, which makes each image complex, diverse and rich in terms of learnable information. To increase the number of images in the EL datasets and reduce the size and complexity of images, we performed a series of cropping operations on all the used EL images. Specifically, a series of square cropping operations were performed on each RGB image starting from the top-left corner (pixel coordinates [0, 0]). The crop size was defined as half of the smallest image dimension. At each iteration, the cropping window was shifted horizontally or vertically using a stride equal to half the crop size. For instance, for an image with a resolution of 4000 × 3000 pixels, a crop size of 1500 × 1500 pixels and a stride of 750 pixels were used. These parameters were carefully chosen to preserve the visual complexity and field-like characteristics of the EL images within each crop, ensuring that diverse plant regions and background elements were retained. The resulting cropped images were then sorted to exclude those without plants or symptoms.

To avoid imbalanced class distributions, we randomly selected a subset of healthy images from each crop-healthy class. The numbers of images taken from each crop-healthy class were, at most, 200 images for EL datasets and 345 images for PV datasets. Additionally, we applied data augmentation techniques to the ‘Cucumber-Bacterial spot’ and ‘Tomato-Leaf mould’ cropped EL classes, and the ‘Cherry-Powdery mildew’, ‘Grape-Esca’, ‘Tomato-Spider mites’ and ‘Tomato-Leaf mould’ PV classes, as these classes contained fewer images compared to the others. Data augmentation was performed using geometric transformations (rotation, horizontal and vertical flip) and colour transformations (brightness and contrast). This augmentation was applied only to the training set, after splitting the datasets into training, validation, and test sets following a 64:16:20 ratio.

The PV-EL combined dataset with the disease common names organisation was used only for the training and validation sets: All crop-disease pair classes with the same disease from both datasets were merged into a single class representing the disease common name, and all healthy images were combined into one ‘Healthy’ class. The crop-disease pair distribution was maintained for PV and EL test sets in order to evaluate model performance on each species and on both lab and field images separately. To assess the generalisation capability of the models on new crops (unseen during the training phase), all images related to pepper and olive tree crops were excluded from the training and validation sets for both PV and EL datasets. This consisted of removing the ‘Pepper-Bacterial spot’ and ‘Pepper-Healthy’ classes from the PV training and validation sets, and the ‘Pepper-Healthy’ and ‘Olive tree-Healthy’ classes from the EL training and validation sets. These excluded classes were used as an unseen-crops test set, and are referred to as PV-Unseen and EL-Unseen test sets for PV and EL datasets, respectively. Test sets with crops seen at training phase are named PV-Seen and EL-Seen. Table 1 provides a summarised description of the PV and EL datasets employed in this study. 2.3 Transfer Learning and Pre-Trained Models

Transfer learning is a machine learning technique that leverages knowledge acquired from training a model on one task to improve performance on a different, yet related task, especially when the latter involves smaller and less diverse datasets (Goodfellow 2016). This approach is particularly advantageous in plant disease detection, where data are often limited (Ngugi et al. 2021). Among the various methods for implementing transfer learning, fine-tuning has proven to be the most effective for this domain (Brahimi et al. 2018; Kaya et al. 2019). Fine-tuning involves taking a pretrained model, initially trained on a large, general-purpose dataset such as ImageNet, and replacing its final fully connected layers with new layers suited to the specific target task. These new layers are randomly initialized and the entire model is then retrained on the target dataset, allowing for optimization of the model's weights across all layers.

In this study, we employed the fine-tuning technique on three pretrained CNNs: MobileNet-v3 (Howard et al. 2019), ShuffleNet-v2 (Ma et al. 2018) and SqueezeNet (Iandola et al. 2016). These CNNs were selected for their lightweight and efficient architectures, which make them well-suited for deployment on mobile and embedded devices with limited computational power. Additionally, these models are designed to deliver low inference latency, which is critical for real-time applications. The choice of these models was driven by the need to develop automated plant disease detection systems that can operate effectively in field environments at the farmer level, as well as in small-scale urban farms and domestic settings, where high-performance resources are typically unavailable and only embedded systems or mobile applications are used.

2.4 Ensemble Averaging

In machine learning, ensemble averaging involves training multiple base models on the same task and then combining their predictions by averaging their estimated output probabilities (Sharma et al. 2022). The purpose of using this method is to improve the overall accuracy and robustness of the ensemble by taking advantage of strengths and compensating for weaknesses of each base model, resulting in more reliable predictions than any single model can achieve individually.

In our study, after fine-tuning the three aforementioned CNNs on the combined PV-EL dataset, we employed the ensemble averaging technique. The fine-tuned resulting models were used as base learners to create four distinct ensemble classifiers: one ternary ensemble (TE) that includes all three models and three binary ensembles (BE), each combining two of the models. For each ensemble, the output probabilities which were estimated by the base models across the three test sets (PV, EL and PD) were averaged. The final predicted class of the ensemble for each test sample was determined by selecting the class with the highest average probability output. 2.5 Experiments

Our experiments involve three main studies:

Effect of dataset distribution on generalisation to unseen crops and datasets: In this first study, we investigated the impact of using the disease-common-name-based dataset distribution on generalisation capabilities to unseen crops and datasets, for both lab and field images. The three pretrained CNNs, MobileNet-v3, SqueezeNet and ShuffleNet-v2, were separately fine-tuned on the PV and EL train sets organised using both the crop-disease pair (CDP) and the disease common name (DCN) distributions. These training strategies are referred to as PV-CDP, EL-CDP, PV-DCN, EL-DCN strategies, respectively. The results from the PV-CDP and EL-CDP strategies are respectively compared to those obtained from the PV-DCN and EL-DCN strategies.

Impact of using the combined PV-EL dataset on model performance: To evaluate the effectiveness of combining lab and field images into the training set, we examined how using the combined PV-EL dataset affects the models' performances in generalising to unseen data. To this end, we compared the performances of models trained only on one type of images (either lab or field) to those trained on the combined train set. The three pretrained CNNs are fine-tuned on the PV-EL combined train set with the DCN distribution (PV-EL-DCN). The test results of the PV-DCN and EL-DCN strategies, used in the first study, are compared to those of the PV-EL-DCN strategy. It is important to note that images used in PV-EL-DCN are the same as those used in PV-DCN and EL-DCN.

Ensemble averaging and individual model performance comparison: In this study, we first compared the performances of the three transfer-learning CNN models—MobileNet-v3, ShuffleNet-v2 and SqueezeNet—individually. We analysed the strengths and weaknesses of each model on different datasets. Then, we analysed and discussed the results we obtained by using the ensemble averaging technique on these three models. This analysis focuses only on models that were trained on the PV-EL-DCN dataset.

All experiments were conducted on a NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU using Python 3.10.12 and the PyTorch 2.2.1 framework. The fine-tuning process employs the following parameters: 25 training epochs; a batch size of 8; the cross entropy loss function; and the stochastic gradient descent optimizer, with a learning rate of 0.001, and a momentum of 0.9. The resulting models are tested on the PV-Seen, PV-Unseen, EL-Seen, EL-Unseen and PlantDoc test sets. 3.1 Effect of Dataset Distribution on Generalisation to Unseen Crops and Datasets

3.1.1 Comparison of PV-CDP and PV-DCN

Figure 4 presents the top-1 test accuracy results of the three CNNs which were trained using the PV-CDP and PV-DCN strategies and tested on PV-Seen, PV-Unseen, global EL which combines EL-Seen and EL-Unseen, and PD datasets. From Figure 4 and Table 3, we observe that models trained using both PV-DCN and PV-CDP approaches achieved comparable accuracy results on the PV-Seen test set, with values exceeding 99.73%. The ShuffleNet-v2 model demonstrated a slightly higher accuracy with the PV-DCN approach at 99.87%, compared to 99.80% with the PV-CDP approach. In contrast, MobileNet-v3 and SqueezeNet performed better with the PV-CDP approach, achieving accuracies of 99.97% and 99.90%, respectively, compared to 99.93% and 99.73% with the PV-DCN approach. When tested on the PV-Unseen test set, the PV-DCN approach yielded higher accuracy for all models, with top-1 accuracy values ranging from 87.03% to 96.60%, whereas the PV-CDP approach gave values from 69.36% to 87.51%.

The best generalisation performance was given by the MobileNet-v3 model, with top-1 accuracy values of 61.68% on the PD test set and 39.13% on the global PV test set, representing increases of more than 19% and 8%, respectively, compared to the EL-CDP approach.

3.1.3 Discussion

The obtained results demonstrate that the DCN distribution significantly enhances same-dataset generalisation performance on images of new species across all three networks that we examined in this study. Furthermore, the DCN method improves generalisation on new species without affecting performance on species seen during training, as shown in Figures 4 and 5, where the accuracy results are nearly identical for both DCN and CDP strategies on seen species. This suggests that networks trained on datasets that are reorganised based on plant health status while ignoring the crop species exhibit better symptom recognition compared to crop-disease pair classification, which leads to more accurate generalisation on new species. In contrast, models that are trained using the CDP distribution may prioritise species-specific characteristics, such as leaf shape and colour, for classification while ignoring the symptom (Lee et al. 2020). Being consistent with the findings reported by Lee et al. (2020), who highlighted the superiority of the DCN approach on the PV database and VGG16 CNN, our results also demonstrate the efficiency of the DCN strategy on complex field images using the EL dataset across all three models.

The analysis of the cross-dataset generalisation results reveals that the different nature of the test sets (lab or field images) compared to the training sets significantly impacts the models' performance. For MobileNet-v3, the PV-DCN approach surpassed the PV-CDP on both global EL (field images) and PD (mix of lab and field images), indicating better generalisation across new datasets and image types. In contrast, both ShuffleNet-v2 and SqueezeNet had better performance on EL and PD with the PV-CDP approach compared to PV-DCN, even though the SqueezeNet model trained using both approaches struggled on EL images. This suggests that models trained solely on lab images struggle to identify symptoms in complex images at inference. Additionally, networks trained using the PV-CDP approach performed better by relying on species-specific characteristics for classification, especially for healthy species. When trained on EL, all models trained using the EL-DCN distribution showed high performance on both global PV and PD datasets compared to EL-CDP, except for the SqueezeNet model, which also struggled in classifying PV images for both approaches, suggesting that this model lacks robustness on new types of images (either lab or field).

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that the DCN approach significantly enhances same-dataset generalisation to new species without reducing performance on seen crops. Furthermore, compared to the CDP distribution, the DCN approach improves cross-dataset generalisation, especially when train and test images have the same nature (field or lab). However, this method alone does not provide high accuracy for generalisation on images of different natures, particularly for models trained on laboratory images (PV) and tested on complex field images (EL and PD). For this reason, in the next section, we will demonstrate the impact of using a combination of field and laboratory images on generalisation to new datasets.

To further evaluate the models' cross-dataset generalisation performance on both types of images (lab and field), all the models were tested separately on lab images and field images taken from the PlantDoc dataset, referred to as PD-Lab and PD-Field test sets.

Similarly, models trained on EL-DCN attained overall accuracy values of 92.94%, 88.14% and 92.30%, respectively, on the EL test set.

3.2.2 Performance of Models Trained on Combined PV-EL Dataset

Figure 6 shows that models trained on the combined PV-EL-DCN dataset demonstrated high performance on both lab and field images. On the PV dataset, all models produced results comparable to the PV-DCN models. Specifically, the MobileNet-v3 model trained on PV-EL-DCN achieved 95.48% accuracy, with an increase of 1.40% over the PV-DCN model. Table 3 indicates that this increase is primarily related to the PV-Unseen test set, where the PV-EL-DCN achieved 90.18% accuracy compared to 87.03% for the PV-DCN. Conversely, ShuffleNet-v2 and SqueezeNet models trained with the PV-EL-DCN strategy were slightly less efficient compared to the PV-DCN-trained models, with a decrease of less than 1.50% in accuracy. For ShuffleNet-v2, the decrease was observed in both PV-Seen and PV-Unseen test sets, while for SqueezeNet, the decrease was limited to the PV-Unseen test set. On the EL test set, the EL-DCN and PV-EL-DCN approaches achieved similarly high performances, except for the SqueezeNet model, where the PV-EL-DCN approach resulted in a decrease of more than 9% in accuracy, primarily related to the EL-Unseen test set.

Table 3 shows that the PV-EL-DCN method performed well on both EL-Seen and EL-Unseen sets, with accuracy values ranging from 98.08% to 98.72% for EL-Seen and from 79.35% to 88.93% for EL-Unseen. For ShuffleNet-v2, the PV-EL-DCN approach improved performance on the EL-Unseen test set compared to the EL-DCN strategy.

When generalising to the PD dataset, the combined PV-EL-DCN outperformed both PV-DCN and EL-DCN for all models, except for MobileNet-v3, where the EL-DCN surpassed the PV-EL-DCN with accuracy values of 61.68% and 54.36%, respectively. The best accuracy was achieved by the ShuffleNet-v2 model, with an accuracy of 65.39%, and an increase of 4.40% compared to the EL-DCN strategy. For SqueezeNet, which had the lowest accuracy when trained on EL-DCN, the PV-EL-DCN increased the accuracy to 60.12%, with an improvement of 8.71%. Table 4 reveals that this increase in accuracy on the PD dataset for SqueezeNet and ShuffleNet-v2 is attributed to improvements in both lab and field images. However, for MobileNet-v3, an increase in accuracy on PD-Field images was associated with a decrease in accuracy on PD-Lab images, resulting in a slight decrease in overall accuracy on the PD dataset. To further examine the PV-EL-DCN models' behaviour on field images and new datasets, we examined the per-class performances on the EL, PD-Lab and PD-Field test sets (Tables S2–S4, S7–S9 and S12–S14). The analysis revealed that using the PV-EL-DCN training set significantly enhanced the performance of all models on unhealthy classes across all test sets, compared to models trained on EL-DCN.

3.2.3 Discussion

Based on this comparison, we can draw several important conclusions. As demonstrated by our results and supported by previous studies (Ahmad et al. 2020; Ferentinos 2018; Mohanty et al. 2016), training CNNs exclusively on lab images from the PV dataset results in models that poorly generalise on field images or new datasets. This limitation is primarily due to the lack of diversity in the PV dataset compared to in-field-captured datasets such as EL or PD (Lee et al. 2020). In contrast, as shown in Figure 6 and Table 4, models trained on field images from the EL dataset (EL-DCN) show better generalisation on lab images and new datasets compared to PV-DCN-trained models. This improvement is likely due to the greater diversity of the EL dataset in terms of species, backgrounds and capture conditions. Consequently, we conclude that training CNNs using complex field datasets can be more advantageous than relying solely on lab images. However, collecting large and diverse datasets from field conditions is a challenging and time-consuming process. Additionally, our results reveal that the EL-DCN training strategy can still yield low accuracy on new datasets, particularly when tested on lab images from the PV dataset, as observed with the SqueezeNet model. Therefore, the use of field images for the training of CNNs is typically more effective than the use of laboratory images alone. Nevertheless, this approach remains inadequate for achieving efficient generalisation to novel datasets and varying capture conditions.

On the other hand, our results show that using a combined dataset (PV-EL-DCN) maintains high generalisation performance on both lab and field images and enhances cross-dataset generalisation for all tested CNNs. Across all models, the PV-EL-DCN approach consistently matches or surpasses the accuracy of the PV-DCN and EL-DCN approaches for PV and EL test sets, respectively. These findings indicate that merging datasets of different types does not negatively impact model performance on the initial training set; in fact, it may even enhance performance on field images or unseen species.

On the PD dataset, which contains a mix of lab and field images, the PV-EL-DCN approach demonstrates superior performance compared to both the PV-DCN and EL-DCN approaches, particularly with the SqueezeNet and ShuffleNet-v2 models. This indicates that the combination of laboratory and field images in the training process enhances the models' capacity to generalise to novel and diverse datasets. Moreover, the models trained using the combined PV-EL-DCN dataset exhibited superior performance in classifying unhealthy classes of field images from both the EL and PD datasets when compared to those trained using the EL-DCN approach. This improvement suggests that adding laboratory images into the training set helps the models to focus more on the intrinsic characteristics of plant diseases, rather than relying on external cues from the field environment (Aarizou and Merah 2022).

In conclusion, the combination approach (PV-EL-DCN) effectively leverages the strengths of both individual datasets (lab and field), resulting in more robust models that better generalise across different types of images and new datasets. This approach demonstrates that integrating small field datasets with a large laboratory dataset enhances generalisation performance on complex field images and new crops, reducing the need for collecting additional real field data which is a challenging and time-consuming process.

3.3 Individual and Ensemble Averaging Model Performance Comparison

3.3.1 Individual CNN Comparison

Table 3 indicates that across the three models trained on PV-EL-DCN, MobileNet-v3 achieved the highest top-1 accuracy on the PV-Seen dataset with 99.87%, closely followed by ShuffleNet-v2 and SqueezeNet, both at 99.80%. However, SqueezeNet outperformed the other models on the PV-Unseen dataset, achieving a top-1 accuracy of 94.42%, whereas MobileNet-v3 and ShuffleNet-v2 achieved 90.18% and 90.10%, respectively. This positions SqueezeNet as the top performer for the PV dataset.

Conversely, in the EL test set, MobileNet-v3 excelled with the highest accuracy on both seen and unseen crops, with 98.72% and 88.93% accuracy values, respectively, resulting in the highest overall accuracy of 90.66% for the EL dataset. ShuffleNet-v2 ranked second on the EL dataset with an overall accuracy of 88.82% (achieving 98.35% on EL-Seen and 86.78% on EL-Unseen). Although SqueezeNet showed strong performance on the PV test set, it had the lowest accuracy on EL-Seen (98.08%) and EL-Unseen (79.35%), resulting in the lowest overall accuracy of 82.65%.

For the PD dataset, ShuffleNet-v2 demonstrated the best generalisation with an overall accuracy of 65.39%, surpassing SqueezeNet at 60.12% and MobileNet-v3 at 54.36%. However, as shown in Table 4, model performances varied between lab and field images. SqueezeNet excelled on lab images with 81.55% accuracy but was the weakest on field images at 49.64%. MobileNet-v3 had a more balanced performance on both types, with the second-highest accuracy on PD-Field (56.77%), but the lowest on lab images at 49.40%. In contrast, ShuffleNet-v2 maintained high performance across both types, being the best on field images (57.64%) and closely trailing SqueezeNet on lab images with 81.25%, which reflects its strong overall performance on the PD dataset.

In cross-dataset generalisation, the ensembles showed varied performance between lab and field images. Across the four ensembles, accuracy values varied in the range of 76.48% to 85.42% for PD-Lab test set, and from 53.71% to 59.24% for PD-Field test set. BE3 achieved the highest accuracy across all ensembles on the PD-Lab test set; however, it had the lowest performance on PD-Field images. Similarly, BE1 led the performance on the PD-Field test set but recorded the smallest accuracy on the PD-Lab test set. The TE ensemble ranked second on both PD-Lab and PD-Field images, achieving accuracy values of 85.12% and 58.81%, respectively, which were close to the best-performing binary ensembles. Consequently, the TE ensemble achieved the highest overall accuracy of 67.45% on the PD dataset, indicating that the combination of all three models offers a more balanced and robust generalisation across different image types.

3.3.3 Discussion

Based on the previous comparison, we can make several deductions. Among the three individual CNNs, MobileNet-v3 excelled in same-dataset generalisation, especially on field images from the EL dataset. However, its poor cross-dataset performance, notably on the PD-Lab test set, indicates potential overfitting to the training data. In contrast, SqueezeNet proved to be the best for lab settings, demonstrating strong generalisation on lab images from both the PV and PD datasets, where images typically feature a single leaf against a uniform background. ShuffleNet-v2, on the other hand, showed the best overall generalisation on new data, maintaining a solid balance across lab and field images. These results highlight that each individual CNN has its strengths and weaknesses across different types of images and datasets. Therefore, by combining MobileNet-v3's robustness on field images, SqueezeNet's precision in controlled laboratory scenarios, and ShuffleNet-v2's overall generalisation stability, the ternary ensemble leverages complementary representations and mitigates individual model biases, resulting in more robust and consistent performance across datasets.

Figure 7 presents the top-1 test accuracy results of the individual and ensemble averaging models for PV, EL and PD datasets. It also shows the top-1 test accuracy values of all the three test sets taken together. As summarised in Figure 7, the ensemble classifiers consistently outperformed the individual models across all test sets. On the PV test set, the highest accuracy was achieved by BE2, closely followed by the TE. All ensemble classifiers surpassed the individual models, except for BE1, which was slightly outperformed by ShuffleNet-v2. On the EL test set, the TE led the performance, with BE1 close behind. However, the BE2 and BE3 classifiers were outperformed by MobileNet-v3. On the PD dataset, the TE once again gave the highest performances, being the only ensemble model to surpass all individual models, as the binary ensembles were outperformed by ShuffleNet-v2.

Top-1 test accuracy of individual CNN and ensemble classifiers on the PlantVillage (PV), EdenLibrary (EL) and PlantDoc (PD) test sets, as well as on all test datasets combined. Despite the strong and consistent performance of the ternary ensemble across datasets, cross-dataset generalisation on PD remains challenging due to pronounced domain shifts. As indicated by the per-class performance analysis (Table S20), most failure cases were associated with diseased classes. The strong performance observed for the Healthy class is likely related to both the higher number and greater visual diversity of healthy samples in the training data. Notably, all healthy images were original and not augmented, which may have further improved generalisation to PD samples. In contrast, several diseased classes exhibit limited visual diversity, particularly in the EL training set, which negatively affects cross-dataset recognition.

To better contextualise the 67.45% accuracy achieved by the TE on the PD dataset, a qualitative error analysis was conducted. This analysis revealed that common misclassification cases include heavy occlusion, extreme illumination conditions, and visually subtle symptoms that differ from those observed in the PV and EL datasets, often leading diseased samples to be misclassified as healthy. Additional confusion arises from visual similarities between disease symptoms, particularly among bacterial spot, leaf mould and spider mites. This behaviour is further supported by the relatively high per-class top-3 accuracies observed for diseased classes (Table S20), indicating that, while the correct pathology is frequently captured among the top predictions, it is not always ranked as the most probable class.

In conclusion, TE was the only ensemble model that consistently outperformed all individual and binary models across all datasets, which is reflected in the highest accuracy of 92.91% on all test datasets taken together. While the binary ensembles were occasionally outperformed by individual models on specific test sets, they still showed superior accuracy on the combined datasets. Among the binary ensembles, BE1 led with an accuracy of 92.12%, followed by BE2 (90.81%) and BE3 (89.96%). These values were higher than those of the individual models, with MobileNet-v3 reaching 89.80%, followed by ShuffleNet-v2 (89.76%), and SqueezeNet (87.16%). This highlights the robustness and effectiveness of ensemble models, particularly the TE, in providing a more balanced and superior performance across diverse datasets.

4 Conclusions

In this paper, we conducted a series of studies to improve the generalisation capabilities of CNN-based classifiers, specifically MobileNet-v3, ShuffleNet-v2 and SqueezeNet. Initially, we explored the impact of reorganising two datasets, PlantVillage (PV) and EdenLibrary (EL), by focusing on disease names independently of plant species. These datasets, originally structured around crop-disease pairings, were restructured based only on plant health status, covering 20 different crop species and six classes including one healthy and five disease classes. Our experiments demonstrated that this restructuring improved same-dataset generalisation to new plant species not included in the training data, without compromising performance on species that were seen during training. Additionally, the results indicated enhanced cross-dataset generalisation on the PlantDoc (PD) dataset when training and testing images share the same context (either field or lab). However, this method showed limitations in generalising to images of different natures, particularly when models trained on lab images (PV) were tested on complex field images from EL and PD.

In the second study, we investigated the effect of combining the PV and EL datasets, both reorganised using the disease common names (DCN) distribution. Our findings revealed that models trained on the combined dataset exhibited significant improvements in cross-dataset generalisation, for both lab and field images. This demonstrates that integrating small field datasets with a larger lab dataset, organised by DCN, enhances generalisation to complex field images and new crops. This can be achieved without the need for additional field data collection, which is often challenging and time-consuming.

In the final study, we compared generalisation performances of the three lightweight models fine-tuned on the combined PV-EL-DCN dataset. We found that each individual CNN has unique strengths and weaknesses across different datasets and image types, and that their performances complement one another. To further enhance model performance, we implemented ensemble averaging with these three models, evaluating both binary and ternary ensemble combinations. The results showed that the ternary ensemble (TE) consistently outperformed individual models and binary ensembles across all datasets. The TE achieved remarkable accuracies of 99.63% and 92.73% for same-dataset generalisation on seen and unseen crops, respectively, and 67.45% for cross-dataset generalisation, which resulted in the highest overall accuracy of 92.91%. This demonstrates that the TE effectively balances the strengths of the individual CNNs, providing more accurate and reliable predictions, particularly in cross-dataset generalisation scenarios, without the need for deeper CNN architectures.

In summary, combining diverse training data with disease common names distribution and ensemble models proves to be a powerful strategy for developing CNN models that generalise effectively across a wide range of datasets and conditions, making them more robust and practical for real-world applications. From a practical perspective, this approach lowers the barrier for developing regional disease detection tools by reducing the need for exhaustive local field data collection and enabling the use of existing public laboratory datasets.
