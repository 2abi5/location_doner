Abstract

Leaf area index (LAI) is one of the most important plant parameters when observing agricultural crops and a decisive factor for yield estimates. Remote-sensing data provide spectral information on large areas and allow for a detailed quantitative assessment of LAI and other plant parameters. The present study compared support vector regression (SVR), random forest regression (RFR), and partial least-squares regression (PLSR) and their achieved model qualities for the assessment of LAI from wheat reflectance spectra. In this context, the validation technique used for verifying the accuracy of an empirical–statistical regression model was very important in order to allow the spatial transferability of models to unknown data. Thus, two different validation methods, leave-one-out cross-validation (cv) and independent validation (iv), were performed to determine model accuracy. The LAI and field reflectance spectra of 124 plots were collected from four fields during two stages of plant development in 2011 and 2012. In the case of cross-validation for the separate years, as well as the entire data set, SVR provided the best results (2011: R2cv = 0.739, 2012: R2cv = 0.85, 2011 and 2012: R2cv = 0.944). Independent validation of the data set from both years led to completely different results. The accuracy of PLSR (R2iv = 0.912) and RFR (R2iv = 0.770) remained almost at the same level as that of cross-validation, while SVR showed a clear decline in model performance (R2iv = 0.769). The results indicate that regression model robustness largely depends on the applied validation approach and the data range of the LAI used for model building.

1. Introduction

Ecological conditions and current management techniques have a major influence on the spatial heterogeneity of agricultural fields. One of the most important challenges of modern agriculture is in regard to adapted treatment of fields to avoid over- and/or undersupply of agricultural inputs, which often causes ecological problems and economic drawbacks. Remote-sensing data can help to overcome these problems, as these provide area-wide spatial and spectral information on agricultural fields that can be used to identify infield variability and can support decision making in precision farming.

Chlorophyll concentration, leaf area index (LAI), above-ground biomass, nitrogen concentration, and canopy water content are key parameters for monitoring agricultural crops. These parameters are indicators for current growth conditions and crucial input variables for agricultural production models. For this reason, it is essential that biochemical and structural properties of vegetation stands can be estimated using a fast and non-destructive method (Cox Citation2002; Mulla Citation2013; Schueller Citation1992).

LAI is among the most important biophysical plant parameters and a key variable for climatological, meteorological, ecological, and agricultural models (Asner, Scurlock, and Hicke Citation2003; Broge and Mortensen Citation2002; Moran, Maas, and Pinter Citation1995). It is defined as the total one-sided area of leaf tissue per unit ground area and provides information about the photosynthetic performance of plants (Chen and Black Citation1992; Duchemin et al. Citation2006; Herrmann et al. Citation2011; Watson Citation1947). In agronomical modelling, LAI is an integrative measure of the influence of biotic and abiotic conditions and therefore an important factor for yield estimates, since it is significantly influenced by yield-limiting and -reducing factors such as plant diseases and mismanagement (Boegh et al. Citation2002; Carter Citation1994; Daughtry et al. Citation1992; Van Ittersum et al. Citation2003).

Various methods for LAI determination exist, differing in the type of measurement methodology and the requirements of the technical equipment (Bréda Citation2003; Zheng and Moskal Citation2009). Destructive methods for measuring LAI normally provide more precise results, but the assessment is time-consuming, expensive, and therefore often limited to small areas (Bréda Citation2003). The increasing use of multispectral remote-sensing data in recent decades has significantly improved the spatial and non-destructive assessment of LAI. Additionally, an increasing number of air- and space-borne hyperspectral sensors are available, which allow the improvement of LAI estimates and can be used as databases for precision farming applications (Gray and Song Citation2012; Haboudane et al. Citation2004; Lee et al. Citation2004).

In recent years, two complementary groups of approaches have been developed for LAI estimation from remote-sensing data. On the one hand, the inversion of physically based radiative transfer modelling approaches provides the potential to retrieve LAI and other biophysical vegetation parameters (Bacour et al. Citation2002; Fang, Liang, and Kuusk Citation2003; Jacquemoud et al. Citation1995, Citation1996; Koetz et al. Citation2007; Vohland and Jarmer Citation2008; Weiss et al. Citation2000). On the other hand, empirical–statistical regression models (e.g. partial least-squares regression (PLSR), support vector regression (SVR), random forest regression (RF), neural net regression (NNR), or multivariate adaptive regression splines (MARS)) can be calibrated with in situ LAI measurements to establish a relation to reflectance spectra or spectral indices (Curran Citation1994; Duchemin et al. Citation2006; Koppe et al. Citation2010; Thenkabail, Smith, and De-Pauw Citation2000; Viña et al. Citation2011).

The number of biophysical/-chemical parameter samples and corresponding field spectra measured during a field campaign is often too low to facilitate an independent validation of an empirical–statistical model (Cohen et al. Citation2003). In this case, cross-validation is widely used as an appropriate method for determining the quality of a regression model (e.g. Efron Citation1983; Oredein, Olatayo, and Loyinmi Citation2011; Snee Citation1977; Steyerberg et al. Citation2001). However, a frequently recognized problem of empirical–statistical models is overfitting. This phenomenon can be defined as an error that occurs in regression modelling when a model is too closely fit to a limited number of samples (Babyak Citation2004; Hawkins Citation2004). Consequently, transferring a cross-validated model developed from the data for one agricultural field to another with the same crop has mostly limited success and often leads to inaccurate results.

The goal of this study was to estimate the LAI of winter wheat from spectral field measurements with different regression techniques (PLSR, SVR, and RFR). In this context, ‘leave-one-out’ cross-validation and independent validation were comparatively performed with LAI data measured during two field campaigns conducted in 2011 and 2012. Furthermore, we investigated whether the LAI data of two different phenological stages can help to improve the robustness of regression models and their prediction capability.

2. Study area

The study area (11°54′ E, 51°47′ N) is located in the eastern part of Germany in the federal state of Saxony-Anhalt (see ) and is intensively used for agriculture. The region is characterized by a slightly undulating tertiary plain at an altitude of 70 m above sea level that is covered by a thin loess layer up to 1.2 m deep. Since the area is situated in the rain shadow of the Harz Mountains, it is distinctly dry with 430 mm mean annual precipitation. The mean annual temperature is between 8°C and 9°C. Chernozems, in conjunction with Cambisols and Luvisols, are the predominant soil type characterized by highly diverse soil properties, resulting in a fine-scale pattern of soil texture and organic matter. Within the study area, four wheat fields (A–D) of size 60–90 ha were selected for the assessment of LAI.

3. Data and methodology

3.1. Data

During two field campaigns from 7–10 May 2011 and 24–25 May 2012, the LAI of 124 winter wheat plots (each of size 50 × 50 cm) distributed on the investigated fields were measured non-destructively with a SunScan device (Delta-T Devices Ltd., USA) in 2011 and an LAI 2000 (LI-COR Inc., USA) in 2012. Satellite data of previous years were used prior to the field campaigns to develop an adjusted strategy for allowing a representative sampling of the four investigated fields. In order to cover a wide range of values, LAI was acquired for the two different phenological stages of stem elongation (EC 31–34) in 2011 and ear emergence (EC 51–60) in 2012. In both years, LAI was measured six times for each plot. Mean LAI values for each plot were calculated from all measurements of that plot within the simple standard deviation to exclude outliers from further investigation. In parallel, reflectance measurements of each plot were collected with two ASD FieldSpec III spectroradiometers (ASD Inc., USA) in 2011 and one ASD FieldSpec III, as well as one SVC HR-1024 spectroradiometer (Spectra Vista Corporation, USA) in 2012. All instruments covered the spectral range 350–2500 nm. The measurements were taken 1.4 m above the ground in nadir view using 25° field of view (FOV) optics in the case of the ASD instruments, and a 14° FOV optic with the SVC instrument. All measurements were converted to absolute reflectance values using a reflectance standard of known reflectivity (Spectralon, Labsphere Inc., USA). Five spectral measurements were acquired per plot and averaged to one single measurement for further analysis. The exact position of each plot was located by a GPS device and photos were taken for documentation. Because of the lower spectral resolution of the SVC HR-1024 spectral data (1024 spectral bands), the measured ASD FieldSpec III spectral signatures (2151 bands) were resampled to the spectral resolution of the SVC HR-1024 data to make both data sets comparable. The bands in the region of atmospheric water absorption (1350–1480 nm and 1780–1990 nm) were deleted, leaving 867 spectral bands between 350 and 2500 nm.

3.2. Methodology

The R environment for statistical computing and graphics was used in combination with different additional packages for regression model calibration and validation (R Core Team Citation2014). These packages included an implementation of the regression algorithms used (PLSR, SVR, and RFR).

PLSR was originally developed for chemical applications, but it has also been used in remote sensing for many years to predict biophysical and biochemical parameters (e.g. Darvishzadeh et al. Citation2008; Hansen and Schjoerring Citation2003; Herrmann et al. Citation2011; Jarmer Citation2013; Nguyen and Lee Citation2006; Pu Citation2012). In this study, wide-kernel PLSR (Rännar et al. Citation1994) was performed with the R-package ‘pls’ (Mevik and Wehrens Citation2007). The maximum number of latent variables (LV) used in the PLSR was limited to 10. The optimum number of LV used for the regression was determined by comparing the root mean squared error (RMSE) of predictions obtained from models to different numbers of LV.

SVR traces back to statistical learning theory and, for some years, it has been adapted to solve classification and regression problems in the field of remote sensing. Karimi et al. (Citation2008), Siegmann et al. (Citation2013) and Tuia et al. (Citation2011), for example, showed the potential of this regression technique for the estimation of plant parameters in agriculture. In the present study, ε-SVR models with Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) kernel were built using the R-package ‘kernlab’ (Karatzoglou et al. Citation2004). In order to determine the optimal parameter values for C (regularization parameter), σ (hyperparameter), and ε (loss function parameter), kernlab uses a grid search technique based on an implementation in ‘libsvm’ (Chang and Lin Citation2011).

RFR was realized with the R-package ‘randomForest’, which contains a direct implementation of Breiman’s Random Forest algorithm for classification and regression problems (Breiman Citation2001). To date, only a few studies have demonstrated the potential of this regression algorithm to derive plant parameters from remote-sensing data (e.g. Powell et al. Citation2010; Vuolo et al. Citation2013). For the assessment of LAI in this study, each RFR model was made up of 500 individual trees. Each tree was built with two-thirds of the training data randomly selected (bootstrap samples), while the remaining one third (out-of-bag samples) was used for a model internal validation. At each node of a tree, only a small number of features (square root of all features) were randomly selected for decision making.

First, regression models were separately generated for the data from the two years. In all cases, model validation was performed by leave-one-out cross-validation (cv), which means that each sample was estimated by a regression model that was calibrated using the remaining (n – 1) samples (Otto Citation2007).

As a second step, regression models were built on the data of both years. This time, however, two different validation techniques were used. First, a leave-one-out cross-validation was performed analogous to models built for the individual years. Second, the 124 LAI values were arranged in ascending order according to their size. Finally, the data set was split into three subsets by choosing every third value for each subset. Thus, three approximately equal-sized data subsets were generated (subset A with 42 samples, subsets B and C both with 41 samples), each of which almost covered the range of values measured in 2011 and 2012. Subsequently, the corresponding field spectral measurements were arranged into the corresponding three subsets. For independent validation (iv), each subset of the data set was used once for validation, while the models were built using the samples from the other two subsets.

The coefficient of determination (R2) and RMSE were calculated to assess prediction accuracy. In accordance with Herrmann et al. (Citation2011), a two-tailed t-test was conducted to determine whether there is a significant difference between coupled R2 values. Additionally, the residual prediction deviation (RPD) was determined as an additional indicator for the robustness of the regression models. The RPD value is the ratio of the standard deviation of the observed values divided by the RMSE of the predictions (Malley, Martin, and Ben-Dor Citation2004). Williams (Citation2011) developed a RPD scale with different-value classes for applications in food chemistry, where high-resolution NIR laboratory spectra are normally used. The quality of this scale is commonly not achieved for precision farming, because spectral field measurements have a lower spectral resolution. Therefore, the RPD value limits suggested by Dunn et al. (Citation2002) for soil science applications, which are more applicable for precision farming requirements, were used in this study to evaluate the robustness of the regression models.

4. Results and discussion

The LAI of all measured wheat plots covered a range between 0.50 m2 m–2 at minimum and 5.70 m2 m–2 at maximum (see ). The mean LAI of 2.80 m2 m–2 indicated that lower LAI values predominated. Due to the earlier stage of plant development (EC 31–34), the LAI of the wheat plots in 2011 were relatively low, varying between 0.50 and 3.40 m2 m–2, with an average of 1.64 m2 m–2. In 2012 the measured plots had higher LAI, because plant development was already more advanced (EC 51–60) than in 2011. The range of measured LAI with a minimum of 1.04 m2 m–2 and a maximum of 5.70 m2 m–2 was relatively high, suggesting a more pronounced variability of the wheat stands in comparison with those measured in 2011. However, only three plots in 2012 had values less than 2. Most of the LAI measurements varied between 2.5 and 6. Thus, the values measured in both years covered a different value range. 4.1. Leave-one-out cross-validation

Leave-one-out cross-validation was initially performed to assess model accuracy for both individual years. SVR provided the best results for the separate analysis of 2011 and 2012 data, with R2cv values of 0.739 and 0.850, respectively, RMSEcv being relatively low at 0.242 and 0.376. A reason for the higher RMSEcv value in 2012 was the wider range of measured LAI in the field that is typical for subsequent stages of canopy development. PLSR yielded lower R2cv values (R2cv = 0.696 (LV = 5) in 2011, R2cv = 0.790 (LV = 3) in 2012) than SVR. However, the RMSEcv of PLSR (RMSEcv = 0.244 in 2011, RMSEcv = 0.408 in 2012) was comparable to that of SVR. In contrast, RFR showed significantly lower model accuracy compared with SVR and PLSR. In 2011 in particular, it was not possible to build a robust model with RFR (R2cv = 0.420, RMSEcv = 0.337). In 2012, RFR provided much better results (R2cv = 0.715, RMSEcv = 0.476), but still showed lower model performance than SVR and PLSR. RPDcv values calculated for SVR (2.709 for 2011 and 2.389 for 2012) and PLSR (2.688 and 2.196, respectively) were all higher than 2.0. According to Dunn et al. (Citation2002), regression models with RPD values higher than 2.0 are well suited, while values between 1.6 and 2.0, like those of RFR for both years, can only be regarded as acceptable. summarizes the results of the cross-validated models using the different regression algorithms.

4.2. Independent validation

Independent validation can be considered the most robust method for assessing regression model accuracy, because validation data are not involved in the process of model development. Nevertheless, it is common to carry out cross-validation when data division is not possible because of inadequate availability of samples, as in this study in the case of the separate years. The high R2iv, together with the RPDiv of well over 3.0 (mean RPD = 3.336), were indicators of successful model calibrations (Dunn et al. Citation2002). SVR (mean R2iv = 0.769, mean RMSEiv = 0.645) and RFR (mean R2iv = 0.770, mean RMSEiv = 0.643) prediction accuracies were comparable to each other, but much lower than PLSR prediction accuracy. Additionally, the mean RPDiv for both regression algorithms was just over 2.0, suggesting the lower robustness of these models.

4.3. Comparison of regression model results

First, two-tailed t-tests were conducted to determine whether there was a significant difference between coupled R2 values. The p-values of the t-tests obtained in the case of cross- and independent validation were all over 0.05 (except in one case). This indicated that the compared R2 values were the same because there was no advantage in any particular data formation.

The individual data sets of the separate years consisted of a relatively low number of samples (57 samples in 2011 and 67 samples in 2012). Consequently, leave-one-out cross-validation was performed to determine model quality. In comparison with the cross-validated regression models built with the data of both years, model accuracies for the single-year data sets were distinctly lower for all considered regression algorithms (see and ). This was mainly due to the fact that using the entire data set (2011 and 2012) increased the number of samples and enlarged the LAI range by including LAI measurements of different phenological stages. This is in accordance with studies by Haboudane et al. (Citation2004), Herrmann et al. (Citation2011), and Nguy-Robertson et al. (Citation2014), who also achieved better correlations between vegetation indices and LAI measurements using data of different phenological stages during the growing season. In addition, the scatter plots for the regression models incorporating the data of both years illustrated a substantial increase in model robustness, less scattering, and a better fit of the regression line to the 1:1 line in relation to the scatter plots of the single-year regression models (see and ). Furthermore, the order of regression algorithm performance was equal for the single-year and complete data sets. In each case, SVR provided the best results, closely followed by PLSR and then RFR – with distinctly lower model performance.

The subsequent independent validation of the complete data set led to a very different result. For all validation data subsets, PLSR produced the most robust models, with R2iv and RPDiv values comparable to those of the cross-validation. In contrast, independently validated SVR models provided much lower accuracies, which were on the same level as RFR results (see ). While the model performances of PLSR and RFR were almost identical for cross- and independent validation on the combined data set, SVR showed a significant decline in R2iv and RPDiv, as well as an extensive increase in RMSEiv at the same time. For the data used in this study, it seemed that SVR models were overfitted with respect to the training data. In the case of cross-validation, this led to very high model accuracy but, at the same time, an unknown sample could not be predicted with the same high accuracy, which was shown in the case of independent validation. The same problem for overfitting was not detected for PLSR and RFR. Both regression algorithms produced almost stable accuracy for both validation strategies, with PLSR providing results at a substantially higher level. The better and more stable results of PLSR compared with SVR differed from some recent remote-sensing studies, where machine learning approaches like SVR have outperformed PLSR, or have at least provided comparable results for the retrieval of biophysical and biochemical parameters from agricultural fields (e.g. Arenas-Garcia and Camps-Valls Citation2008; Wang, Huang, and Lou Citation2011).

Obviously, the validation technique for verifying the performance of a regression model is very important. This study showed that PLSR and RFR performance were less sensitive to the validation technique used. In contrast, SVR model quality depended more on the type of validation. Independent validation was the more robust approach for verifying regression models, because training and validation data are statistically independent from each other. Thus, model overfitting during calibration can be prevented.

In recent years, several different validation techniques have been developed. For example, Wang, Huang, and Lou (Citation2011) randomly divided their data into two-thirds for calibration and one third for validating regression models. Another method for independent validation was presented by Tuia et al. (Citation2011), who conducted two experiments by randomly splitting the data into 80 and 50% for calibrating the model and validation, respectively. Each experiment was repeated 40 times and results were averaged to avoid skewed data distribution. In the present study, a random division of calibration and validation data was not planned. Instead, the LAI values were arranged in ascending order according to their size and every third value was chosen for one of the three resulting data subsets. Therefore, the data range and distribution of LAI values in each subset were very similar, which is a key requirement, because a statistically robust model validation can only be conducted on independent data that cover the same data range as used for model calibration. In this context, it was not possible to build regression models with the data set acquired in 2011, and predict the data of 2012 and vice versa, because the value range covered for the two years was different. The LAI range covered by the LAI data from both years was more appropriate for model building to predict LAI from unknown spectral data. However, in order to build a universal model, the LAI measurements of earlier (EC 10–30), later (EC 61–70), and phenological stages in between (EC 35–50) also had to be integrated into model calibration to cover the entire range of possible LAI during the growing season.

Additionally, the spatial domain plays an important role in calibrating and validating regression models. The data used in this study were acquired from four different agricultural fields within a region and mixed to build independent data subsets for model calibration and validation. Consequently, the samples were not spatially independent. Therefore, further research is required to investigate an approach that is spatially more independent. In this context, regression models have to be calibrated with data collected from one field. Subsequently, the model validation can be conducted on data from a neighbouring field with the same crop. This approach can help in the development of spatially independent regression models that are transferable within a region.

The derivation of LAI from remote-sensing data for the assessment of the current growth conditions of crops in agriculture requires high accuracy. In this regard, independent validated regression models covering a wide range of cultivar-specific LAI have the potential for providing LAI estimates with sufficient accuracy; in addition, they could be transferred to multi- or hyperspectral airborne and satellite data for entire fields. This additional spatial information on LAI distribution can help to improve many applications in precision farming.

Besides the validation procedure, which was applied for assessing regression model quality, the use of different spectroradiometers (ASD FieldSpec III and SVC 1024) and LAI measuring devices (LAI 2000 and SunScan) can have an influence on the regression results. In order to ensure comparable spectral measurements, all instruments were calibrated by the manufacturers less than one year before the field campaigns and the spectral data were converted to absolute reflectance values using reflectance standards of known reflectivity. Additionally, the spectral measurements were acquired at the same height above ground in both years. Nevertheless, the divergent FOV of the spectroradiometers used and the different plant heights in 2011 and 2012 can may have had some influence on the spectral measurements. The different LAI devices used in 2011 (SunScan) and 2012 (LAI 2000) were also calibrated by the manufacturers. Moreover, according to Wilhelm, Ruwe, and Schlemmer (Citation2000), comparable measurements are possible as long as the operator follows the procedures described in the user manuals. Thus, the use of different measuring devices as an influencing factor on the regression results can be reduced to a minimum.

Furthermore, multi-collinearity is a known problem using regression analysis applied to hyperspectral data sets. This can also have an impact on model calibration, validation, and consequently on model prediction. The best results in the case of independent validation, however, were achieved for PLSR, which is less influenced by this problem because the high number of spectral bands is reduced to a distinctly lower number of uncorrelated LV representing an orthogonal linear combination of the original spectral bands (Atzberger et al. Citation2010; Yu et al. Citation2015). Nevertheless, more attention has to be paid to the problem of multi-collinearity in future research, especially regarding the use of SVM and RFR.

5. Conclusions

In this study, the potential of three regression algorithms (PLSR, SVR, and RFR) was investigated to predict wheat LAI from field reflectance spectra collected from different fields and at different plant development stages. The results clearly show that model performance markedly depends on the validation technique used to assess model accuracy. In the case of leave-one-out cross-validation, SVR provided the best results for individual years and for the entire data set, but revealed significant weaknesses when independent model validation was performed. This suggested a problem in data over-fitting during model calibration, which cannot be proved by leave-one-out cross-validation, as model calibration and validation are performed using almost identical data. PLSR and RFR yielded lower performance for leave-one-out cross-validated models of individual years. Although model accuracy for the entire data set achieved with both regression algorithms showed a clear improvement, it was still lower compared with SVR. However, in contrast to SVR model accuracy, independently validated PLSR and RFR models provided nearly identical model performance, similar to that obtained with leave-one-out cross-validation. This indicated that PLSR and RFR are less influenced by data over-fitting and hence less sensitive to the validation techniques used. However, PLSR provided distinctly better results than RFR and was, therefore, in this study, better suited for predicting LAI.

Furthermore, it was shown that an extended data range through the inclusion of the LAI data at different stages of plant development was essential for improving model performance for all regression algorithms considered. Models calibrated with LAI measurements of numerous growth stages, which cover a wide range of values, could additionally be used as an independent validation data set for LAI assessment from spectral field, airborne, or satellite data of other wheat fields within the region.

Validation of empirical–statistical models based on spectral data is an important research topic in many remote-sensing studies, especially for agricultural applications. This study demonstrated, by the example of wheat LAI, that independent validation is necessary to prove the ‘real’ robustness of a regression model. This is particularly important for LAI prediction from spectral images acquired by a rising number of imaging systems mounted on agricultural machines or aircraft, which allow for assessment of LAI and other plant parameters in large areas that are spatially differentiated.
