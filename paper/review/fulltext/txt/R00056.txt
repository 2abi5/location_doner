10                                                       Abstract

11   Recently, statistical machine learning techniques, especially deep learning, have been widely used for corn
12   yield prediction. Though successful, machine learning models generated within a specific spatial domain
13   often lose their validity when directly applied to new regions. To address this issue, we designed an
14   unsupervised adaptive domain adversarial neural network (ADANN). Specifically, through domain
15   adversarial training, the ADANN model reduced the domain shift by projecting different domain data into
16   the same subspace. Also, we trained the ADANN model in an adaptive way to guarantee the model can
17   learn the domain-invariant features and perform accurate yield prediction simultaneously. Informative
18   variables including time-series vegetation indices and sequential weather variables were extracted from
19   different data sources and the ADANN model was established based on the extracted features and observed
20   yield. The U.S. corn belt was used as the study area and counties under study were grouped into two diverse
21   ecological regions. The experimental results showed that the developed ADANN model outperformed three
22   other state-of-the-art machine learning models in both local experiments (train and test in the same region)
23   and transfer experiments (train and test in different regions). As the first study using the adversarial learning
24   for crop yield prediction, this research demonstrates a novel solution for improving model transferability
25   on crop yield prediction.

26   Keywords: Yield prediction, Remote sensing, Transfer learning, Domain adaptation, Adversarial training

28       1. Introduction

29             As the world’s largest corn producer and exporter, the U.S. harvested 366.6 million metric tons of
30   corn and supplied over 30% of global corn production in 2018 (Guo et al., 2019; USDA, 2020). Accurate

                                                             1
31   and timely estimation of corn yield in the U.S. is therefore of great importance for food security monitoring
32   and market planning (Jiang et al., 2019; Johnson, 2014). Moreover, under pressure of increasing
33   environment stress (Crane-Droesch, 2018; Lobell et al., 2013, 2009), corn production in the U.S. has
34   experienced increasing inter-year variability (Lobell et al., 2014; Sibley et al., 2014). Timely and reliable
35   corn yield prediction can facilitate better assessments of its response to environmental stresses, and also
36   help inform decision making about commodity trading and insurance assessment (Kang et al., 2020).

37           With the advance of data science, various machine learning models, including deep learning models,
38   have been developed for crop yield prediction using multiple data sources. For example, Johnson (2014)
39   built tree-based regression models using remotely sensed vegetation indices (VIs) and weather observations
40   for county-level corn and soybean yield prediction across the U.S. corn belt. Khaki and Wang (2019)
41   developed a fully connected multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to estimate corn yield at the field-level in the
42   U.S. using weather and soil variables. You et al. (2017) added the Gaussian process to Convolutional Neural
43   Networks (CNN) for county-level soybean yield prediction using MODIS imagery. Jiang et al. (2019) built
44   a phenology-based Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model for rain-fed corn yield prediction at the
45   county level in nine U.S. Midwestern states. Similarly, Schwalbert et al. (2020) developed a LSTM model
46   for municipality-level soybean yield prediction in Brazil using satellite imagery and climate data and
47   achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.24 mg/ha. Kang et al. (2020) systematically compared the
48   performance of different machine learning models, including support vector regression (SVR), random
49   forest (RF), extreme gradient boost (XGBoost), CNN, and LSTM, for county-level corn yield prediction in
50   twelve states in the U.S. Midwest.

51           However, most existing models established between reported yields and remote sensing
52   measurements are location specific. Due to the phenomenon known as domain shift (Kouw and Loog, 2019),
53   models generated within a specific spatial domain often lose their validity when directly applied to new
54   regions (Hao et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018). To address this challenge, transfer learning,
55   which aims to apply knowledge learned from the source domain to the target domain, has become a viable
56   solution. To apply transfer learning with deep neural networks, a common strategy is to fine-tune a
57   pretrained network weights (trained from the source domain) by using the labeled samples from the target
58   domain. For example, Lu et al. (2017) first trained a CNN model from the linear feature elimination task,
59   and then directly fine-tuned a pre-trained model with labeled UAV images to fulfill the task of classifying
60   cultivated land. Wang et al. (2018) adapted a deep CNN model trained with data from Argentina to predict
61   province-level soybean yields in Brazil by fine-tuning the pre- trained model with labeled data from Brazil.
62   Russello (2018) explored transfer learning between two ecoregions in the U.S. for county-level soybean
63   yield estimation by training a CNN model with labeled data from one ecoregion and fine-tuning it in another


                                                          2
64   region. When only a small number of labeled samples are available in the target domain, there would be
65   concerns about overfitting if fine-tune the entire network (Mehdipour Ghazi et al., 2017). Therefore, some
66   research freezed the weights of earlier layers of a pretrained deep learning architecture and customized the
67   model to a given task through fine-tuning the last few layers. This framework is based on the idea that the
68   earlier layers of a neural network learn generic features that can be used in relevant domains (Yosinski et
69   al., 2014). For example, Barbedo (2018) fine-tuned the last few layers of a pretrained GoogleNet with a
70   plant disease database and adapted the network for plant disease classification. Abdalla et al (2019) fine-
71   tuned a well-trained VGG16 with a small oilseed rape images dataset to classify plants in fields with high-
72   density weeds. Similarly, Chen et al. (2020) freezed the learned weights in top layers of a VGG19 trained
73   on the ImageNet dataset and fine-tuned the last three layers with crop disease images to customize it for
74   disease classification.

75           Despite several successful cases, these fine-tuning based methods still require a certain amount of
76   labeled data from the target domain.. Since the collection of yield records heavily depends on field surveys
77   and censuses, which are financially expensive, labor-intensive, and time-consuming, it is possible that some
78   agricultural areas lack ground reference for either directly training or fine-tuning the supervised learning
79   models. To address the issue of missing labeled data in the target domain, the Domain-Adversarial neural
80   network (DANN) is a promising approach, since it intentionally alleviates domain shift through an
81   unsupervised domain adaptation strategy which does not require any labeled data from the target domain
82   (Ganin et al., 2017; Li and Chen, 2020). Specifically, the DANN consists of three parts, including the
83   feature extractor, domain classifier, and label predictor. The feature extractor is trained to minimize the
84   prediction loss for the label predictor while maximizing the domain loss for the domain classier. Since it is
85   trained adversarially against the domain classifier, the feature extractor can be updated towards generating
86   domain-invariant features to help alleviate the negative impact from domain shift. The effectiveness of
87   DANN has been demonstrated in several classification tasks. For example, Ganin et al. (2017) achieved
88   effective transfer learning from the MNIST dataset to the MNIST-M dataset for handwritten digits
89   classification. Wang et al. (2018) designed a DANN for speaker recognition and overcame the domain shift
90   between two independent telephone corpora datasets. Han et al. (2019) designed a convolutional DANN
91   for mechanical faults classification, which generalized well for conditions not observed during training.
92   Despite success in classification tasks, the DANN cannot be directly applied for yield prediction which is
93   a regression task. This is mainly because the predefined weighting parameter in the original DANN model,
94   which controls the trade-off between the prediction loss and the domain loss, needs to be adjusted according
95   to the yield magnitudes which can change dramatically at different agricultural production regions.




                                                          3
96            In this study, we designed an adaptive DANN (ADANN) for county-level corn yield prediction, in
97    which the weighting parameter is adjusted adaptively. The U.S. corn belt was used as the study area and
98    counties under study were grouped into two diverse ecological regions. Informative variables including
99    time-series VIs and sequential climate observations were first extracted from multiple data sources and
100   aggregated to the county level. The ADANN was established based on the extracted features and observed
101   yield. It was compared to three other state-of-the-art machine learning models including the original DANN
102   model in both local experiments (i.e., train and test in the same region) and transfer experiments (i.e., train
103   and test in different regions). Finally, we further interpreted the proposed ADANN model and discussed
104   potential future works.

105   2. Materials

106           In this study, the U.S. corn belt was selected as our study area since it is the top corn-producing
107   area in the world with abundant historical corn yield records. For the model development and evaluation,
108   three types of data were collected, including yield records, remote sensing products, and weather
109   observations. A detailed description of each data type and preprocessing steps are provided below.

110   2.1 Study area and yield records

111           The experimental area included twelve states in the U.S. corn belt (Johnson, 2014; Kang et al.,
112   2020), including Michigan, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Wisconsin, North Dakota, Nebraska, Minnesota, Iowa,
113   South Dakota, Missouri, and Kansas, which account for over 70% of the annual corn production in the U.S.
114   (USDA, 2020). Historical yield records from 2006 to 2019 in these states were collected at the county level
115   from the USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS) database (USDA, 2020).

116           To evaluate the model’s transferability across different regions, the counties under study were
117   grouped into two diverse ecological regions based on the United States Environmental Protection Agency
118   (EPA) (EPA, 2001), including the Eastern Temperate Forests (ETF) region and the Great Plains (GP) region
119   (Fig. 1). The ETF is characterized by a warm, humid, and temperate climate, with humid summers and mild
120   to cold winters. It is mainly covered by dense and diverse forests and has a dense population. Great plains,
121   on the other hand, mainly consists of flat grasslands, and has a scarcity of forests. The change of seasons in
122   GP is more obvious, with very hot summers and harsh winters. This region is also subjected to drought,
123   due to the scarcity of forests and lack of rainfall (Omernik, 1987; Omernik and Griffith, 2014).

126   2.2 Satellite imagery and weather observations

127           We considered VIs as predictors in this study since VIs have been proven to be indicative of crop
128   growth and production (Feng et al., 2020; Johnson, 2014). Specifically, four complementary VIs have been
129   extracted from the daily MODIS MCD43A4 product at 500m spatial resolution (Schaaf and Wang, 2015),
130   including NDVI, Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), Green Chlorophyll Index (GCI), and Normalized
131   Difference Water Index (NDWI). NDVI is one of the most representative VIs used to assess vegetation
132   vigor (Wang et al., 2020). EVI is an improved NDVI that can correct aerosol scattering and ground cover
133   signals and has enhanced sensitivity in high biomass regions (Gao et al., 2000; Huete et al., 2002). GCI
134   quantifies the light use efficiency by measuring the canopy chlorophyll content and can be used as a
135   measurement for crop health (Gitelson et al., 2005). NDWI measures the vegetation moisture content, and
136   thus it is widely employed to monitor the water content changes in crop leaves (Bolton & Friedl, 2013;
137   Gao, 1996). These four VIs were calculated as below:

150   al., 2008).

151   2.5 Preprocessing

152           Remote sensing imagery and weather observations were first aggregated spatially to the county
153   level on Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform (Gorelick et al., 2017). Then, the USDA-NASS Cropland
154   Data Layer (CDL) at 30-m spatial resolution was used to mask out irrelevant areas of corn from the MODIS
155   and PRSIM data. Afterward, the daily sequential predictors from 2006 to 2019, including VIs and weather
156   variables, were aggregated to an 8-day interval from April 15th to October 15th to cover the entire planting
157   and growing season for corn in the U.S. corn belt. We started from 2006 when the CDL was publicly
158   available and fully covered the U.S. corn belt (Johnson, 2014). Finally, the officially reported corn yield of
159   each county for a particular year was paired with the corresponding time-series predictors with a total length
160   of 198. The total number of data samples in ETF and GP are 5358 and 5393, respectively. A complete

164   3. Methodology

165           A conventional deep neural network (DNN) associates input data samples 𝐱 ∈ 𝑋 with data labels 𝐲
166   ∈ 𝑌 by learning the distribution 𝐷(𝐱,𝐲), in which 𝑋 and 𝑌 are input feature space and output space,
167   respectively. In the scenario of yield prediction, 𝐱 are remote sensing and weather variables and 𝐲 are yield
168   records. Given two datasets from the source domain 𝒟𝑠 with 𝑛𝑠 data samples and target domain 𝒟𝑡 with 𝑛𝑡
169   data samples, they are likely to have different distributions 𝐷𝑠(𝐱,𝐲) and 𝐷𝑡(𝐱,𝐲) due to the domain shift
170   caused by different environments. Consequently, a data-driven machine learning model trained with data
171   samples from 𝐷𝑠 would have low performance if directly applied to 𝐷𝑡.

172           To address this issue, the unsupervised domain adversarial neural networks (DANN) has been
173   proposed to reduce the domain shift in original feature space by projecting inputs from two different
174   domains into a common subspace (Ganin et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018; Zhuang et al., 2019). As illustrated
175   in Fig. 2, the architecture of the DANN is based on traditional feed-forward neural networks but has two
176   output layers, which output yield prediction 𝐲 ∈ 𝑌 and domain label 𝐝 ∈ {[1,0],[0,1]}, respectively. Denote
177   with 𝑑𝑖([0,1] or [1,0]) for the 𝑖th sample, which indicates whether 𝐱𝑖 comes from the source domain (𝐱𝑖~𝐷𝑠
178   (𝐱) if 𝐝𝒊 = [1,0]) or the target domain (𝐱𝑖~𝐷𝑡(𝐱) if 𝐝𝒊 = [0,1]). Specifically, a DANN model consists of
179   three parts, including a feature extractor 𝐺𝑓, a domain classifier 𝐺𝑑, and a yield predictor 𝐺𝑦 (Fig. 2). From
180   left to right in Fig. 2, the 𝑖th data sample 𝐱𝑖 from 𝒟𝑠 or 𝒟𝑡 is first fed to the feature extractor 𝐺𝑓 for feature
181   extraction. Then, the extracted features 𝐱𝑐𝑖 is forwarded into both the domain classifier 𝐺𝑑 and yield
182   predictor 𝐺𝑦 to predict the domain label 𝐝𝑖 and crop yield 𝑦𝑖, respectively. More formally, the mapping
183   functions are:

202           Though successful, the performance of the DANN is significantly affected by the weighting
203   parameter 𝜆 which is typically predefined experimentally. Also, the DANN has been widely used in
204   classification tasks where cross-entropy loss is used for both the prediction loss and the domain loss, which
205   are of similar magnitude. While in yield prediction which is a regression task, the mean squared error is
206   adopted as the yield prediction loss which can have quite different magnitude with the cross-entropy based
207   domain classification loss. To address these issues, we developed an adaptive domain adversarial neural
208   network (ADANN) approach for yield prediction, in which the weighting parameter 𝜆 was adjusted
209   adaptively. Specifically, motivated by Ganin et al. (2017), we used a schedule to initialize λ at 0 and
210   gradually increased it as training proceeded. Meanwhile, we normalized the 𝜆 with the ratio of prediction
211   loss and domain loss to offset the magnitude imbalance. A formal definition of the adaptive 𝜆 is given as
212   follows:

213   in which 𝑝 is the training progress increasing linearly from 0 to 1; 𝑟 is the normalization term defined as
214   the ratio between the prediction loss and domain loss. This schedule makes sure there is a small weight on
215   domain loss during the early training which allows the domain classifier to be less sensitive to noisy signal
216   at the early stages of the training procedure (Ganin et al., 2017).

217           Through experimental analysis, the architectures of the proposed ADANN were designed to have
218   a depth of seven (Fig. 2). The feature extractor started with an input layer followed by four fully connected
219   hidden layers with 256, 128, 64, and 32 neurons, respectively. Both the domain classifier and the yield
220   predictor consisted of two fully connected hidden layers with 64 and 32 neurons followed by an output
221   layer. A GRL was used to connect the feature extractor and domain classifier. In each hidden layer, the
222   Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) was used as the activation function and batch normalization (BatchNorm)
223   and dropout (Dropout) were adopted to address internal covariate shift and overfitting (Ioffe and Szegedy,
224   2015). In training, we set the total number of epochs as 200 with a batch size of 512. Stochastic gradient
225   descent (SGD) with a momentum term of 0.9 was used as the optimizer to update trainable parameters after
226   each epoch (Goodfellow et al., 2016), and its learning rate was initiated as 0.01 and decayed by half every
227   20 epochs.

228   4. Experiments and Discussion

229   4.1 Experimental Setup

230           The spatial generalizability and transferability of the proposed ADANN model was evaluated
231   through both local experiments and transfer experiments within the U.S. corn belt, and the two ecoregions


                                                             9
232   (i.e., GP and ETF) were alternatively used as the source and target domain. The developed ADANN model
233   was compared to three other approaches, including RF, DNN and the original DANN model with a fixed 𝜆
234   in the loss function. For a fair comparison, the DNN model was designed to have an architecture with the
235   same depth and number of neurons as the ADANN but without the GRL and domain classifier, and the
236   DANN had the same architecture as the ADANN as well. All the deep learning models were developed
237   with PyTorch 1.5 in Python 3.7 (Paszke et al., 2019) and the RF was implemented with the scikit-learn
238   library in Python 3.7 (Pedregosa et al., 2011).

239            All the models were evaluated under two cases: 1) local experiments (i.e., GP→GP and ETF→ETF)
240   where models were trained and tested on the same domain, and 2) transfer experiments (i.e., GP→ETF and
241   ETF→GP) where models were trained on one domain and tested on the other. Using all preceding years
242   since 2006 for model training, the models were evaluated on four testing years 2016 - 2019. For example,
243   if the testing year was 2018, labeled source samples and unlabeled target samples from 2006 to 2017 were
244   used for model training. Two accuracy metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R2) and root
245   mean square error (RMSE), were used to evaluate the yield prediction results, and they were calculated as

260           In the local experiments, both DNN and RF could make satisfactory predictions, and the DNN
261   outperformed the RF in most cases since informative features could be learned through multiple neural
262   network layers. However, it was observed that the performance of RF and DNN degraded in most transfer
263   experiments due to the domain shift. For example, in 2019, the RF and DNN could make accurate
264   predictions in the local experiments, achieving an R2 of 0.76 and 0.79 in GP→GP, respectively. However,
265   the accuracies were significantly reduced to 0.49 and 0.30 when the trained RF and DNN were directly
266   applied to ETF (i.e., GP→ETF). To improve transferability across domains, both DANN and ADANN
267   applied the domain adaptation strategy by projecting data from two domains into a common space through
268   adversarial learning. The adversarial learning acted as a cross domain regularizer, which helped reduce the
269   domain shift. Consequently, we observed that, in most cases, the DANN and ADANN had improved spatial
270   generalizability than the RF and DNN. For example, in 2019, both DANN and ADANN yielded higher R2
271   and lower RMSE than the RF and DNN in the transfer experiment from ETF to GP.
272           We further compared the performance between DANN and ADANN. The results showed that in
273   most cases, the ADANN performed better than DANN. Also, it was observed that the DANN performed
274   unstably from case to case. For example, it achieved a high R2 of 0.75 in 2019 when transfer from ETF to
275   GP. However, in 2017, its performance degraded and only achieved an R2 of 0.44 when transfer from GP
276   to ETF. Similar unstable performance was also observed in local experiments. For example, in the local
277   experiment GP→GP, the DANN achieved an R2 of 0.81 in 2019, while had a much lower accuracy in 2017
278   (R2 = 0.64). The unstable performance of the DANN was mainly due to the predefined 𝜆 (i.e., set as 1 in
279   our experiments) which could not universally balance the trade-off between the two quantities in the overall
280   loss function Eq. (8), including the prediction loss and domain loss. Instead of using a fixed 𝜆, the proposed
281   ADANN model could adaptively adjust this weighting parameter within the training process which could
282   help properly balance the prediction performance and domain adaptation. A more detailed discussion in
283   this regard is presented in Section 4.3.

297   4.3 Model Interpretation

                                                        14
298           To further investigate the difference between the developed ADANN model with the original
299   DANN model, we compared their training loss performance over the transfer experiments. Specifically, the
300   training loss includes two parts, the prediction loss 𝐿𝑦 and the domain loss 𝐿𝑑, and 𝜆 is defined as the
301   weighting parameter that adjusts the trade-off between domain loss and prediction loss (see Eq. (8)). An
302   example results in 2018 were shown in Fig. 4. Both transfer experiments showed that the ADANN
303   eventually converged on lower prediction loss but similar domain loss as the DANN model. Specifically,
304   as shown in Fig. 4, the prediction loss of the ADANN decreased at higher rates while its domain loss
305   required more iterations to converge. Since its 𝜆 was nearly zero during early training, the ADANN was
306   mainly updated towards the direction of making accurate prediction and thus converged faster on the
307   prediction loss than on the domain loss. On the other hand, the DANN converged slowly on the prediction
308   loss but much faster on the domain loss. This is because a fixed 𝜆 which is larger than zero was used in the
309   original DANN. Moreover, with a fixed 𝜆, the domain loss in the DANN could be overweighted in the later
310   training stage and caused more fluctuations on the yield prediction loss curve. Overweighting domain loss
311   also prevented the DANN from further reducing the prediction loss and thus it converged on a high
312   prediction loss error than the ADANN. Furthermore, there was no obvious difference between their
313   capabilities to extract domain-invariant features since both DANN and ADANN converged on a similar
314   domain loss after 50 epochs (Fig. 4 (b1)-(b2)).

318           Finally, to provide visual insights into the effects of domain adaptation, we used the t-distributed
319   Stochastic Embedding (t-SNE) algorithm, an unsupervised nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique
320   (Maaten and Hinton, 2008), to visualize feature distributions. The t-SNE algorithm projects a high-
321   dimensional feature vector to a two-dimensional space for visualization since spatial analysis in the original
322   high-dimensional feature space is challenging. Fig. 5 shows the visualization results for the transfer
323   experiments in 2018. The two domains are color-coded, with green representing for the ETF and red for
324   the GP. Specifically, Fig. 5(a) shows the original data distribution without domain adaptation, and Fig. 5(b)-
325   (c) show the data distribution after the domain adaptation using the proposed method ADANN, with one
326   from GP to ETF (Fig. 5(b)) and one from ETF to GP (Fig. 5(c)). From the results, we can see that the
327   original data from the two domains are separated, suggesting that the distribution discrepancy existed
328   between the two ecological regions (GP and ETF). Therefore, directly applying a model trained from one
329   region to another would have unsatisfactory results due to the domain shift. On the other hand, after the
330   domain adaptation, it was notable that the distributions of extracted features from ETF and GP were much
331   closer. It validated that the ADANN was able to bridge the domain discrepancy effectively and thus
332   overcome the domain shift problem in corn yield prediction.

337   5. Conclusion

338           Accurate and timely estimation of corn yield is essential for regional and global food security, and
339   machine learning models have been explored for yield modeling. However, due to domain shift, machine
340   learning models generated within a specific spatial domain often lose their validity when directly applied
341   to new regions. In this study, to enhance machine learning models’ transferability, we designed an ADANN
342   model to realize unsupervised domain adaptation for corn yield prediction based on remote sensing and
343   weather variables. Through adversarial learning, the ADANN model could extract domain-invariant
344   features and help reduce the domain shift effectively. Experiments on two diverse ecological regions in the

                                                           16
345   U.S. corn belt showed that the proposed ADANN model outperformed RF, DNN, and the original DANN
346   model in both local experiments and transfer experiments in all four testing years (2016, 2017, 2018 and
347   2019). Model interpretation showed that the ADANN model could flexibly adjust the weight between
348   prediction loss and domain loss during training, which ensured the ADANN model reduce domain shift and
349   perform accurate yield prediction simultaneously. Further improvements may be obtained by designing the
350   feature extractor using more advanced architectures, such as LSTM and self-attention network which have
351   been proven to be able to well handle time-series features (Jiang et al., 2019; Vaswani et al., 2017).
