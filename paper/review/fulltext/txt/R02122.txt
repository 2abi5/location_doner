Highlights

What are the main findings?

UAV multispectral imagery with machine learning predicted cover crop biomass with high overall cross-validation accuracy (R2 up to 0.89), but accuracy within individual planting dates was far lower for late-planted stands, driven by their compressed biomass range rather than by genuinely poorer prediction.

A single well-timed spring flight matched or exceeded multi-date combinations, and crop growth stage at image acquisition influenced accuracy more than the choice of machine learning model.

What are the implications of the main findings?

Low-cost UAV imagery is a practical, labor-saving alternative to manual harvest for cover crop biomass estimation, provided imaging is timed to periods of sufficient canopy development.

Model performance in stratified field studies should be judged using deviation statistics and the sampled biomass range alongside R2, and independent cross-environment validation is needed to realistically assess transferability, since within-season R2 alone can overstate predictive ability.

Abstract

Winter pea grown as a cover crop in monoculture and mixture with grasses poses remote sensing challenges, including spectral mixing among peas, weeds and intercrop canopies and wide variation in canopy establishment and biomass across planting dates and years. The effect of these factors, cropping system, and model choice on UAV-based biomass prediction and whether such models transfer across years remain untested. Over two seasons (2022–2024) in Freeville, NY, we evaluated winter pea across four planting dates and two cropping systems, combining UAV multispectral imagery with four machine learning models to predict biomass from vegetation indices. Spring imagery achieved the highest cross-validation accuracy (R2 up to 0.894), with early plantings far more predictable than later ones. Crop growth stage at acquisition influenced accuracy more than model choice: a single well-timed spring flight matched or exceeded multi-date combinations. In the evaluation of an independent test set, models ranked plots reliably but did not generalize in absolute magnitude. Low-cost UAV imagery is thus practical for cover crop biomass monitoring, provided accuracy is interpreted relative to the biomass range and recalibrated across years.

Keywords:
UAV; biomass prediction; machine learning; vegetation indices; intercropping; winter peas; cross-environment prediction

1. Introduction

Pea (Pisum sativum L.) is grown worldwide as a vegetable, pulse, or fodder crop and is also used as a cover crop, with spring peas used during the main growing season and winter peas as a fall-planted, overwintering cover crop [1]. Winter pea offers multiple ecosystem services, including weed suppression, biological nitrogen fixation, addition of soil organic matter, and reduction in soil erosion [2,3,4,5]. In cover crop breeding efforts, evaluating traits such as plant vigor and biomass has typically been conducted through visual estimation and manual biomass sampling [6,7], which are time- and labor-intensive and subject to human error.

Several factors including planting date, cropping system, and environmental conditions can influence the performance of winter peas. Regional planting-date guidance exists [8] but remains limited and highly region- and species-specific [9]. Early planting generally increases biomass accumulation, winter survival, and the delivery of other ecosystem services in cereals like triticale [10]. However, peas transition from rosette to upright growth if planted too early, which can ultimately reduce survival and biomass accumulation [11,12]. Cropping systems can also affect winter survival and biomass production: growing a cereal–legume mixture can improve winter survival of some legumes, but late planting of a cereal–legume mixture can increase competition from the cereal and reduce legume biomass [13,14]. Winter pea genotypes also differ substantially in winter survival and productivity, and genotype-by-environment interactions have been reported in previous studies [1,15,16].

The planting date and cropping system effects on establishment, survival, and growth habit translate directly into challenges for UAV-based remote sensing of total biomass. Differences in ground cover, canopy composition, and canopy density create several distinct remote sensing regimes. At the earliest planting dates, excessive early growth can reduce pea survival, and where pea plants die, weeds frequently colonize the gaps, so the total biomass at a given level of green cover reflects a shifting mix of pea, weed, and cereals (mixture plots only), each with a different canopy structure and reflectance. At the latest planting dates, plants remain small, and ground cover is sparse, so exposed soil dominates the pixel signal, increasing soil background noise and compressing the already narrow range of total biomass values available to a predictive model. Superimposed on this gradient, spectral mixing among pea, weed, and cereal canopies means that vegetation indices respond to the combined canopy in ways that depend on its composition, so a single index value can correspond to different total biomass depending on planting date and cropping system. These features (asynchronous establishment, planting-date-dependent biomass ranges, variable soil exposure, and composition-dependent spectral mixing) define the specific problem this study addresses. Prior UAV studies have estimated biomass, yield, and maturity in monoculture peas [17,18,19] and legume–grass mixtures [20,21] but have generally examined single planting dates or already-established canopies, rather than the conditions described above.

Extending UAV-based trait estimation to these conditions therefore requires a sensing pipeline, whose accuracy is governed by the type of sensor, vegetation index (VI), and the predictive model used, as these components may respond differently to variation in soil exposure, canopy composition, and canopy structure. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is one of the most widely used VIs for estimating plant biomass, growth, and vigor because of its simplicity and effectiveness in monitoring vegetation dynamics [18,22,23]. However, NDVI has notable limitations, saturation in dense vegetation and sensitivity to soil background and illumination variability [24,25]. With UAV-based multispectral imaging, a broader range of VIs can be extracted using diverse spectral bands. Red-edge and soil-adjusted indices, such as NDRE, SAVI, RTVI, and NGRDI, as well as visible-band indices, such as GNDVI, VARI, and ExGR, have shown stronger correlations with biomass and higher prediction accuracy than NDVI across a range of crops, including olive, vineyard, cereal rye, and oat systems [26,27,28]. Thus, evaluating multiple vegetation indices, rather than relying on NDVI alone, is likely to improve biomass prediction accuracy in winter pea, where canopy density and soil exposure vary widely across planting dates and cropping systems.

Collectively, the above-mentioned studies leave three questions unresolved for UAV-based monitoring of winter pea cover crops. First, existing UAV-biomass studies have not systematically evaluated how planting date, cropping system, and model choice jointly affect prediction accuracy within a single experiment, making it difficult to know whether reported accuracy reflects genuine biomass sensitivity or is inflated by pooling structurally different planting dates. Second, most multi-temporal UAV studies assume that combining additional flight dates improves prediction without testing this assumption under independent, cross-year validation. Third, UAV-biomass studies typically report only cross-validation accuracy within a single season, which does not, by itself, establish that a model generalizes to an independent growing season.

Machine learning (ML) models are increasingly used to model crop traits, including random forest regression (RF), gradient boosting (GBM), artificial neural network (ANN), elastic net (ENET), and partial least squares (PLS), which have been applied to predict growth, biomass, and stress in pea and other crops [26,27,29,30]. Because relative model performance depends on data type, trait, and crop canopy, comparison within our specific scenario is needed to identify the best predictor.

To address these gaps, a two-year study was conducted to (a) evaluate the accuracy of UAV-based image phenotyping, relative to traditional visual estimation, for predicting winter pea biomass across planting dates and cropping systems, distinguishing between- and within-planting-date accuracy and identifying the most effective vegetation indices and models; and (b) determine how prediction accuracy depends on planting date, flight timing, and model choice, including generalization to an independent test set from a held-out growing season.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Study Site

A two-year study was conducted over the 2022–2023 and 2023–2024 growing seasons (hereafter referred to as Year 1 and Year 2 respectively) on certified organic fields at the Homer C. Thompson Vegetable Research Farm in Freeville, NY. A split-plot design with four replications was implemented, with planting dates as the main plots (Table 1). Subplots consisted of four pea cultivars (Table 2), each grown in monoculture and in mixture with triticale, plus a triticale-monoculture entry grown at each planting date to characterize triticale’s spectral signature in isolation. This yielded nine subplot treatments per planting date (4 cultivars × 2 cropping systems + 1 triticale monoculture). Due to the lack of seed availability, ‘Icicle’ ‘and ‘Windham’ were planted for only one year of the experiment (Table 2).

Table 1.
Planting dates (PD) represented across the two years of the experiment. Planting began in early September and continued at two-week intervals through mid-October.

Table 2.
Winter pea cultivars included across two years of the experiment. Due to limited seed availability, some cultivars were planted in only one year of the experiment.

Fields were planted using an Almaco Heavy-Duty Grain Drill (Almaco Inc., Nevada, IA, USA) at a target seeding depth of 7.62 cm. Seeding rates were 850 plants m−2 for monoculture pea plots, 425 plants m−2 for mixture pea plots, 60 seeds m−2 (22.417 kg ha−1) for triticale in mixture plots, and 180 seeds m−2 in triticale monoculture plots. Fertilizers, herbicides or fungicides were not applied. Pea seeds were inoculated with rhizobia prior to planting. Each plot measured 4.6 m in length and 1.33 m in width.

Growing season temperature and snowfall varied considerably across the two years (Figure 1 and Figure 2). Minimum temperatures (TMIN) reached similar magnitudes in both years (Year 1: −6.9 °C in February; Year 2: −4.9 °C in January), but their monthly patterns differed: in Year 1, mean monthly minimum temperatures remained below 0 °C from December through March, while in Year 2, they remained below 0 °C from November through February and returned above 0 °C by March. Total seasonal snowfall was slightly higher in Year 1 (621 mm) than in Year 2 (582 mm), but its distribution differed markedly: Year 1 snowfall was spread across four months, with peaks in December (177 mm) and March (203 mm) separated by a February decline (76 mm), whereas Year 2 snowfall was concentrated in a single large January peak (342 mm), with a smaller secondary peak in March (152 mm). Both years showed minimal snowfall from April onward.

Figure 1.
Monthly minimum (TMIN), maximum (TMAX), and average (TAVG) temperatures in Year 1 (2022–2023) and Year 2 (2023–2024) growing seasons at Freeville, NY.

Figure 2.
Total monthly snowfall (mm) in Year 1 (2022–2023) and Year 2 (2023–2024) growing seasons at Freeville, NY. Snowfall corresponds to the NOAA SNOW variable (new snow depth per month, in millimeters), which is distinct from liquid precipitation (PRCP).

2.2. Field Data Collection

For biomass estimation, three 1 m rows per plot were harvested in 5/18 in Year 1 and from 5/20 to 5/22 in Year 2. Each plot was divided lengthwise into two halves; biomass was collected from only one half in both years. Pea, triticale, and weed biomass were manually separated and oven-dried for one week before weighing to determine dry biomass (kg/ha).

2.3. Image Acquisition and Processing

Flights were conducted once a week in Year 1 and twice a week in Year 2. A SwitchBlade-Elite tricopter (Vision Aerial Inc., Bozeman, MT, USA), equipped with a MicaSense RedEdge-P multispectral camera (MicaSense, Seattle, WA, USA) and a Here3 GPS unit (CubePilot, Geelong, Australia), was used to capture aerial imageries (Figure 3). In addition to the Here3 GPS, five tarps were placed—one at each corner and one at the center of the field for radiometric calibration. The RedEdge-P camera includes five spectral bands: Blue (475 nm center, 32 nm bandwidth), Green (560 nm center, 27 nm bandwidth), Red (668 nm center, 14 nm bandwidth), Red Edge (717 nm center, 12 nm bandwidth), and Near-IR (842 nm center, 57 nm bandwidth). The UAV was flown at an altitude of 20 m, with an average speed of 3 m/s, typically between 10 am and 2 pm, maintaining 80% front and side overlap.

Figure 3.
Image acquisition and processing pipeline. (a) Field images were acquired using a multispectral camera mounted on a UAV. (b) Raw images were stitched into orthomosaics using Pix4Dmapper. (c) Mean pixel values for five spectral bands were extracted at the plot level using ImageBreed. (d) Vegetation indices were calculated, and machine learning models were trained in RStudio.

Orthomosaics were generated in TIFF format using Pix4D mapping software version 4.90 (Pix4D Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA) and uploaded to ImageBreed [31] for plot-level extraction of mean pixel values and thresholded denoised means across all bands (Figure 3). A total of 13 VIs were computed using plot level thresholded denoised mean pixel values (Table 3).

Table 3.
Vegetative indices calculated from UAV multispectral imagery, with their mathematical formulas and original sources.

2.4.1. Data Preparation and Filtering for Biomass Prediction Using UAV

An overview of the analytical workflow, from UAV image acquisition through model evaluation, is shown in Figure 4. Year 1 was used as the training set, and Year 2 was used as the testing set. To ensure temporal comparability between seasons, growing degree days (GDD) accumulated from each UAV flight date to harvest were calculated for both years using daily temperature data. UAV flight dates in the testing year were matched to training-year flight dates based on equivalent GDD accumulation, resulting in three paired flight combinations for all 13 VIs: TRD231–TSD245, TRD246–TSD255, and TRD257–TSD262, where TRD and TSD denote training and testing flight respectively and numbers represent number of days from 31 August to flight day.

Figure 4.
Analytical workflow for UAV-based prediction of total biomass in winter pea cover crop systems, from UAV image acquisition and vegetation-index extraction through model training (RF, GBM, Elastic Net, PLS) to two separately reported evaluation paradigms: (a) cross-validation within the 2022–2023 training season and (b) prediction on an independent 2023–2024 test set, assessing generalization to an untested environment. Inset scatter plots are illustrative.

To address multicollinearity among spectrally correlated VIs, an iterative Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) filtering procedure was applied separately for each flight date combination. Both the Pearson correlation analysis used to identify the protected predictor and the iterative VIF filtering were performed exclusively on the training-year (Year 1) data. The vegetation-index set selected from the training year was then held fixed and applied unchanged to the independent 2024 test set; no correlation or VIF screening was performed on the test data. At each iteration, the predictor with the highest VIF was removed until all remaining predictors had VIF < 10. The VI with the highest absolute Pearson correlation with total biomass was designated as a protected variable and retained throughout the filtering process regardless of its VIF value.

To evaluate the effect of including imagery from multiple UAV flights on prediction accuracy, two cumulative flight combinations were tested in addition to single-date models: (1) TRD231–TSD245 + TRD246–TSD255 and (2) TRD231–TSD245 + TRD246–TSD255 + TRD257–TSD262. For each combination, VIF filtering was re-applied on the pooled VI set before model training. This analysis assessed whether incorporating additional temporal information from multiple flights improved biomass prediction accuracy beyond single-date models.

2.4.2. Machine Learning Models to Predict Total Biomass

Four machine learning algorithms were evaluated for total biomass prediction: random forest (RF), gradient boosting machine (GBM), partial least squares regression (PLS), and elastic net (ENET). RF is a widely used ensemble method that averages predictions from multiple decision trees built on bootstrap samples, making it robust to noise and overfitting [47,48]. GBM is an ensemble method that sequentially fits weak learners to the residuals of previous ones to build a strong predictive model [49]. ENET is a regularized regression method that combines the L2 penalty of ridge regression with the L1 penalty of lasso and is particularly useful for datasets with many correlated predictors [50]. All models were fitted using spectral vegetation indices as the sole predictors, so that model performance reflected spectral information alone. Because between-planting-date differences accounted for 48.6% of total biomass variance, predictions from each model were additionally partitioned by planting date and by cropping system to assess predictive performance within each stratum.

All models were implemented using the caret package [51] in R. Model performance was assessed under two distinct paradigms. First, models were trained and internally evaluated on the training-year data using 10-fold cross-validation repeated three times, which was used both for hyperparameter tuning and for internal (within-year) performance estimation. Second, each tuned model was refit on the full training-year dataset and applied to the independent Year 2 (test-year) data to obtain independent test set predictions, providing an assessment of cross-year generalizability. For the independent test-set evaluation, each model was refit using the full training data and the corresponding training-year-selected vegetation indices, and predictions were generated for the test-year plots using the fitted model’s predict function. To assess whether prediction error reflected a loss of relative (rank) information or a systematic offset in absolute magnitude, the slope and intercept of the linear regression observed on predicted test-set biomass were also computed; a slope near 1 with an intercept near 0 indicates good calibration, whereas a slope departing from 1 combined with a high correlation indicates that rank ordering was preserved but predictions were poorly calibrated in terms of scale. Because the test season spanned a narrower range of biomass within individual planting dates than the training season, R2 computed within individual planting dates on the test set is highly sensitive to this reduced reference variance and is reported only as a diagnostic; within-planting-date test-set performance is instead summarized using RMSE, MAE, and correlation (Supplementary Table S1). RF models were trained using 500 trees, with the number of predictors randomly sampled at each split (mtry) tuned over a grid including floor(√p), floor(p/3), 2, and 3, where p is the number of predictors. GBM models were tuned over a grid of number of trees (100, 300, 500), interaction depth (1, 2, 3), shrinkage rate (0.01, 0.05, 0.1), and minimum observations per node (5, 10). ENET models were fit using the glmnet implementation, with the mixing parameter alpha tuned over seven values (0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0) spanning Ridge regression (alpha = 0) through LASSO (alpha = 1), and the regularization parameter lambda was tuned over 30 values on a log scale from 10−4 to 103. All predictors were centered and scaled prior to ENET and PLS model fitting. PLS models were tuned for the number of latent components from 1 to min (10, p), with variable importance assessed using Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores, where VIP > 1 indicated meaningful contribution.

3. Results

3.1. Distribution of Total Biomass by Planting Date and Cropping System

Total biomass varied by planting date and cropping system in both years, though the pattern differed between seasons (Figure 5). In Year 1, median biomass was highest for the second planting date (mid-September) rather than the earliest for both monoculture and mixture plots before declining at the two later planting dates. In Year 2, biomass declined monotonically from the earliest to the latest planting date, and both the median and maximum biomass at the earliest planting dates exceeded those observed in Year 1. In both years, pea–triticale mixture plots generally had equal or higher total biomass than pea monoculture plots at the first three planting dates, consistent with the combined pea and triticale biomass contributing to the mixture total; this difference diminished at the latest planting date, where both cropping systems produced comparably low biomass.

Figure 5.
Distribution of total biomass by planting date and cropping system in 2022–2023 training season (Year 1) and 2023–2024 testing season (Year 2). PD1–PD4 correspond to planting dates in late August, mid-September, late September, and mid-October, respectively. Boxes show the median and interquartile range; points show individual plot-level observations, jittered horizontally for visibility.

3.2. Vegetation Index Correlation with Total Biomass

Most VIs showed consistently strong positive correlations with total biomass across the growing season in both years (Figure 6). NDRE exhibited the highest positive correlation overall, followed by SR_RE and GNDVI, both of which outperformed the commonly used NDVI throughout the season. Conversely, BG Ratio, R:G Ratio, and ExGR showed low or negative correlations in both years.

Figure 6.
Pearson correlation (r) between each vegetation index and total biomass across UAV flight dates in Year 1 (2022–2023) and Year 2 (2023–2024) at Freeville, NY. The x-axis shows day of year (days from 31 August); the dashed horizontal line indicates r = 0. Most indices maintained consistently strong positive correlations with biomass across the season, while B:G ratio and ExGR were weakly or negatively correlated in both years, and R:G ratio was consistently negatively correlated.

After iterative VIF filtering (threshold < 10), four to five VIs were retained per single-date model, and six to nine VIs were retained per multi-date model (Table 4). ExGR, SR_RE, NDRE, VARI, BG Ratio, and GNDVI were selected across multiple models. NDRE was dropped from three-flight multi-date models due to high multicollinearity when combined with indices from other flight dates, though it was retained as a protected predictor within its respective single-date model to preserve temporal information.

Table 4.
Vegetation indices (VIs) retained per model after iterative Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) filtering (threshold < 10) for three single-date models and two cumulative multi-date models. Rows are grouped by the flight date at which each VI was derived.

3.3. Cross-Validation Performance

When all planting dates were analyzed together, all four models achieved strong overall predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.79–0.89; RMSE = 996–1653 kg ha−1; Table 5). However, partitioning the cross-validated predictions by planting date revealed that prediction accuracy was substantially higher for PD1 (R2 = 0.69–0.84) and PD2 (R2 = 0.64–0.84) than for PD3 (R2 = −0.30–0.47) and PD4 (R2 = −2.00–0.48) across models, likely reflecting the limited canopy development of later-planted stands at fixed imaging dates (Table 5).

Table 5.
Within-planting-date cross-validated R2 and RMSE (kg ha−1) for random forest, elastic net, partial least squares, and gradient boosting models predicting total biomass from vegetation indices only (planting date and cropping system excluded as predictors) across five spring flight combinations. Cross-validation was performed within the 2022–2023 training year (10-fold, three repeats).

Among single-date models, the April 19 training/May 3 testing combination (TRD231·TSD245) with gradient boosting (GBM) gave the best overall performance (Figure 5; R2 = 0.89; RMSE = 996 kg ha−1). For PD1, the harvest-window TRD246·TSD255 combination with GBM was most accurate (R2 = 0.84; RMSE = 1223 kg ha−1). For PD2, the harvest-window TRD257·TSD262 combination with GBM was most accurate (R2 = 0.82; RMSE = 1145 kg ha−1). For PD3, GBM on TRD246·TSD255 performed best (R2 = 0.47; RMSE = 643 kg ha−1), and for PD4, GBM on TRD231·TSD245 was most accurate (R2 = 0.48; RMSE = 591 kg ha−1).

Adding additional flight dates (two flights vs. three flights) to multi-date models improved R2 in PD2, PD3, and PD4 for ENET, PLS, and GBM but not RF (Table 5). The three-flight GBM combination (TRD231.TSD245 + TRD246.TSD255 + TRD257.TSD262) achieved the best within-PD gains for PD2 RF, EN, and PLS models relative to the corresponding single-date models. However, the best single-date spring models (TRD231·TSD245 and TRD246·TSD255) matched or exceeded these multi-date combinations for overall R2 across all planting dates except PD2 (e.g., GBM 0.84 single-date vs. 0.80 three-flight for PD1 models).

Overall, GBM models using spring imagery (April–May) consistently delivered the best biomass prediction accuracy across planting dates, and the incremental gain from multi-date stacking was model- and PD-dependent (Table 5). Predictive performance was maintained across both pea monoculture and pea–triticale mixture (Supplementary Table S2), indicating the approach was not specific to a single cropping system.

Variable-importance analysis showed consistent patterns across GBM and RF (Table 6). In both of the best-performing single-date models, NDRE was the dominant predictor in each algorithm (relative importance = 100). For the April combination (TRD231·TSD245), VARI contributed strong secondary importance in both algorithms (67.2 RF; 65.1 GBM), with B:G Ratio contributing modestly in RF (30.7) and negligibly in GBM. For the mid-May combination (TRD246·TSD255), by contrast, ExGR was the secondary predictor (25.4 RF; 5.7 GBM), and VARI contributed negligibly in both, indicating that the complementary indices supporting NDRE differed between acquisition dates. In the three-flight model, from which NDRE had been removed during VIF filtering, VARI from the April acquisition became the top predictor in both algorithms (relative importance = 100), followed by GNDVI (44.9 RF; 89.4 GBM) and SR_RE (31.1 RF; 27.6 GBM) from the late-season acquisition, indicating that red-edge and green-normalized information was retained across the season through these indices. Because tree-based importance measures distribute credit unpredictably among correlated predictors, low importance values do not necessarily indicate that an index lacks predictive information.

3.4. Independent Test-Set Performance

Each model, trained on the Year 1 data with the training-year-selected vegetation indices, was applied to the independent Year 2 field to assess prediction in an untested environment (Table 7). A single mid-May flight (TRD246.TSD255) was the only single-date acquisition that transferred positively across all four models (test-set R2 = 0.44 for random forest and gradient boosting, 0.66–0.67 for the linear models), with near-unity calibration (slope 0.85–0.89). In contrast, the April flight (TRD231.TSD245), despite the highest cross-validation accuracy, transferred poorly (test-set R2 = −1.07 to −0.39), as did the late-season flight and the multi-date combinations (test-set R2 up to 0.27). Correlation between observed and predicted 2024 biomass remained moderate to strong even where test-set R2 was negative (r = 0.75–0.83 for the spring single-date models), while regression slopes departed from unity (0.51–1.37). This combination preserved rank ordering, with a slope far from one indicating that the models correctly ranked plots in the untested environment but were poorly calibrated in absolute magnitude, consistent with the narrower biomass range sampled in Year 2 (1218–9018 kg ha−1) relative to Year 1 (630–12,933 kg ha−1). Tree-based models degraded more gracefully than the linear models under this range shift (three-flight slope 0.49 for gradient boosting vs. 0.31 for partial least squares).

4. Discussion

4.1. Spectral–Biological Interpretation of Vegetation Indices

In both best-performing single-date spring models, NDRE was the dominant predictor in each algorithm (relative importance = 100; Table 6). This reflects NDRE’s use of the red-edge region, where reflectance is strongly governed by leaf chlorophyll content and canopy nitrogen status, and which saturates at higher biomass than the red band used by NDVI [38,45]. The complementary indices supporting NDRE differed between the two spring acquisitions: for the April model, VARI contributed strong secondary importance (67.2 RF; 65.1 GBM), whereas for the mid-May model, ExGR was the secondary predictor (25.4 RF; 5.7 GBM), and VARI contributed negligibly. VARI and ExGR are visible-band indices that respond primarily to fractional green cover rather than to chlorophyll concentration [41,52], so their varying secondary roles likely reflect differences in canopy closure and green-cover fraction between the two acquisition dates. The B:G ratio, sensitive to the contrast between soil and vegetation reflectance in the visible region [32], contributed modestly in the April RF model (30.7) but little elsewhere, consistent with a soil-background index carrying more information where canopies are less complete.

A notable shift occurred in the multi-date models. When indices from multiple flights were pooled, the red-edge indices from different acquisitions were mutually collinear, and VIF filtering removed one or more of them, including NDRE itself, despite its dominance in the single-date models. This removal is a direct consequence of the filtering criterion: VIF removes an index because it is statistically redundant with other predictors already in the pooled set, not because it lacks predictive value. In the three-flight model, VARI from the April acquisition became the top predictor in both algorithms (relative importance = 100), and NDRE’s chlorophyll- and structure-related role was assumed principally by GNDVI (44.9 RF; 89.4 GBM) and SR_RE (31.1 RF; 27.6 GBM) from the late-season acquisition (Table 6).

This substitution is spectrally and biologically reasonable. GNDVI, a green–NIR normalized index, and SR_RE, a red-edge simple ratio, both draw on physiological signals that overlap those of NDRE: GNDVI is sensitive to chlorophyll concentration through the green and near-infrared bands [41], while SR_RE, like NDRE, exploits the red-edge region [45]. The three indices differ in their exact band combinations and in their saturation and sensitivity characteristics, but all convey related chlorophyll and canopy structure information; thus, when NDRE was removed, the red-edge and green-normalized information it carried remained represented across the season through these correlated indices rather than being lost. Because tree-based and projection-based importance measures distribute credit unpredictably among correlated predictors, a low importance value for a retained index does not necessarily indicate that it lacks predictive information. It may instead indicate that its information is largely shared with a higher-ranked correlated index.

4.2. Performance of Biomass Estimation Model

The full pooled models yielded high overall R2 (0.79–0.89); however, partitioning the cross-validated predictions by planting date revealed substantially lower within-planting-date R2, particularly for the two latest plantings. This divergence reflects the variance structure of the data rather than a corresponding loss of predictive accuracy. Because R2 expresses error relative to the variance of the evaluation set, it is not comparable across strata differing in response range [53,54]. As all treatments were terminated on a common date, later-planted stands spanned a much narrower biomass range (Figure 7), so within-planting-date R2 fell even though mean RMSE also declined (from ~1506 to ~783 kg ha−1). A variance partition confirmed that planting date dominated pooled variance (48.6%), whereas cropping system did not (9.0%), indicating that range compression, not stratification per se, drove the decline and motivated the separate within-planting-date diagnostics.

The two latest planting dates (PD3, PD4; teal and purple) occupy a compressed range at the lower end of the biomass gradient, which underlies their lower within-planting-date R2 despite predictions lying close to the 1:1 line (Table 5).

Beyond this statistical effect, a biophysical factor also contributed to genuinely weaker prediction at the latest plantings. Because later-planted stands had substantially lower canopy cover at the fixed spring imaging dates, despite sharing a common termination date, a larger fraction of the sensed signal originated from soil rather than vegetation. In such low-canopy conditions, soil background reflectance increasingly influences spectral indices and reduces their sensitivity to among-plot biomass variation [55,56]. The resulting limited spectral contrast among PD3 and PD4 plots, rather than any inherent model limitation, is the most plausible explanation for their reduced within-PD predictive performance.

Flight date combination had a greater influence on model prediction accuracy than algorithm choice across all planting dates. Two spring flight pairings, TRD231·TSD245 and TRD246·TSD255, consistently produced the highest prediction accuracy regardless of algorithm and planting date. This underscores the primacy of imaging timing and stage-specific model development over algorithm selection in UAV-based biomass estimation, consistent with findings in soybean [57]. This effect was proportionally greater for PD3 and PD4 than for earlier plantings, as the spectral signal from late-established, lower-biomass canopies is more sensitive to the precise developmental stage captured at imaging.

Two factors help explain why multi-date stacking yielded only modest gains over the best single-date spring models. First, because total biomass was destructively sampled once per season, the number of response observations was fixed at the number of plots per season regardless of how many flight dates were combined. Adding flights therefore increased predictor dimensionality without increasing sample size, raising the risk of overfitting, particularly as the additional indices were often highly collinear with the strongest single-date predictors and contributed limited independent information. Second, the VIF filtering required to manage the inflated collinearity of the pooled predictor set removed NDRE, the most transferable single-date predictor; a sensitivity analysis retaining NDRE recovered much of the lost interannual accuracy (RF interannual R2 rising from 0.17 to 0.38 for the three-flight model), confirming that predictor selection, not stacking alone, drove part of the decline. Together, these factors indicate that, for a once-per-season destructive response, a single well-timed acquisition is preferable to multi-date stacking, as it is both more parsimonious and more robust across years or untested environments.

4.3. Model Behavior and Cross-Year Transfer

The four algorithms represent two families with different inductive biases. The tree-ensemble models (RF, GBM) partition the predictor space into piecewise-constant regions and cannot extrapolate beyond the range of the training data, whereas the linear models (Elastic Net, PLS) fit global linear relationships and can extrapolate outside it. This difference was evident in independent test-set transfer: the tree models degraded more gracefully (GBM three-flight interannual slope 0.49) than the linear models (PLS three-flight slope 0.31), which over-dispersed their predictions when applied to the compressed second year biomass range. Within cross-validation, GBM and RF achieved marginally higher R2 and lower RMSE than PLS and Elastic Net, but these algorithm differences were small relative to the effect of flight timing, and no single algorithm was best across all planting dates and years.

4.4. Comparison with Prior Work and Generalizability

Our cross-validation accuracy compares favorably with prior UAV-based biomass studies in related cover crop and forage systems. Using an identical 13-index pool and VIF-based feature selection for cereal rye, KC et al. [26] reported cross-validation R2 of 0.24–0.59 and independent-test R2 up to 0.67, while Sharma et al. [27] reported training R2 of 0.50–0.60 for oat forage biomass. Comparable accuracies have been reported for multi-species mixtures and cover crop systems incorporating winter pea and other legumes [21,58]. Our best single-date spring models achieved comparable or higher cross-validation accuracy (R2 up to 0.89), which may reflect the wider biomass range spanned by our multi-planting-date design and the strong performance of red-edge indices in winter pea canopies.

The present study differs from the comparable studies in the stringency of its generalization test. Prior UAV-biomass studies have typically evaluated model transfer using separate fields or plots sampled within a single growing season [21,26,27], whereas we assessed predictions across different planting dates, cultivars, cropping system, and on an independent field in a different year. Under this more demanding test, accuracy declined substantially (test-set R2 ranging from 0.67 to strongly negative depending on flight timing), even though cross-validation accuracy remained high, a divergence that within-season validation alone would not reveal. Together, these comparisons indicate that within-season accuracy metrics, whether from cross-validation or same-season held-out data, may overstate the operational transferability of UAV-based biomass models and that reporting independent cross-environment validation, as done here, provides a more realistic assessment of generalizability.

4.5. Limitation and Future Direction

The use of total aboveground biomass (i.e., the summed weight of pea, triticale, and weed components) as the response variable, rather than isolating the pea-specific spectral contribution, is one of the limitations of this study. Pixel-level classification and spectral unmixing approaches (e.g., random forest classification, linear spectral mixture analysis) were considered but judged infeasible for this dataset: canopies in mixture plots were dense and structurally interleaved, with pea, triticale, and weed foliage occupying overlapping and adjacent positions within individual image pixels rather than forming spatially distinct, classifiable patches. Reliable unmixing or classification requires either pure endmember spectra collected under comparable canopy conditions or pixel-level ground-truth labels, neither of which could be obtained from these dense, mixed canopies without destructive subplot sampling at a spatial resolution beyond the scope of this study. We therefore modeled total aboveground biomass as the response variable most consistent with the UAV spectral signal, which itself integrates reflectance across the full canopy rather than resolving individual species. Future work using higher-resolution imagery, hyperspectral sensors, or targeted endmember collection may enable pea-specific biomass estimation in mixture plots.

A further limitation is that cultivar was not included as a predictor or grouping factor in the biomass prediction models, despite known differences in winter survival and productivity among cultivars [15,16]. Because the vegetation-index-based models were fitted across all cultivars pooled within each planting date and cropping system, any cultivar-driven variation in canopy development or biomass is embedded within, rather than resolved by, the reported prediction accuracy.

Although the present study used model-specific and model-agnostic importance measures such as impurity and permutation-based importance, VIP scores, and regularized coefficients to interpret predictor contributions, these provide only global, dataset-level explanations. Explainable-AI (XAI) methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) could instead give per-plot attributions of each index’s contribution and help disentangle how canopy composition drives predictions in mixture plots [59]. SHAP has already been applied productively to UAV-based cover crop biomass estimation in cereal rye [26]. High-resolution imagery combined with object-detection or semantic-segmentation models (e.g., YOLO-based architectures) could estimate species-specific cover fractions within mixture plots [60], potentially enabling the pea-specific biomass estimation not feasible with the plot-level spectral data used here.

5. Conclusions

UAV-based multispectral imagery combined with machine learning provided accurate, labor-efficient estimation of total cover crop biomass in winter pea across two growing seasons, four planting dates, and two cropping systems. Prediction accuracy depended most strongly on imaging timing: a single, well-timed spring flight performed as well as or better than multiple-flight combinations, whereas the choice among machine learning algorithms mattered comparatively little, and predictions transferred reliably across pea monoculture and pea–triticale mixture within a season. However, cross-validation accuracy substantially overstated performance in an independent field and growing season, where predictions preserved plot rank ordering but were poorly calibrated in absolute magnitude, underscoring that independent cross-environment validation and interpretation of accuracy relative to the biomass range sampled are essential for realistically assessing UAV-based biomass models. Overall, a single, well-timed, low-cost UAV flight can support rapid, non-destructive cover crop biomass monitoring, provided that cross-environment calibration is addressed for operational use across seasons and sites.
